# Form vs. Matter (SEP) — Walk-Through

## Complete Walk-Through

**Author:** Thomas Ainsworth  

**Entry:** Form vs. Matter (Fall 2024 Edition)

## Overview: The Structural Arc

Hylomorphism is born to solve **one problem**: explain *change* without violating "nothing comes from nothing." Form and matter enter as a clean contrastive pair, purpose-built for that job.

The drama of the entry is watching that tidy contrast get progressively strained — and at points nearly broken — as Aristotle puts the same pair to work on problems it wasn't designed for. Each of the three controversies (prime matter, individuation, matter-involving forms) is a site where the neat form/matter opposition buckles under load. **Ainsworth's closing note is decisive**: the contrast is "no less elastic than explanatorily powerful." This means the form/matter distinction is **explanatorily productive but metaphysically fragile**. Keep that arc in view and the whole thing reads as a single escalating pressure test.

## Section 1: Matter and Form Introduced

### The Problem: Change Without Creation Ex Nihilo

The notions are **reverse-engineered from an analysis of change**. Aristotle divides changes into two types:

1. **Accidental change**: A substance gains or loses a property
    - Example: Socrates turns blue; he gains weight
    - The substance (Socrates) persists; only properties shift
2. **Substantial change**: A substance comes into or goes out of existence
    - Example: Birth, death, acorn becoming oak tree
    - The *substance itself* is what's generated or destroyed

### Three Requirements for Any Change

For any change, Aristotle insists there are three elements:

1. Something that underlies and persists through the change
2. A "lack" (one pole of a pair of opposites)
3. A form acquired during the course of change

In accidental change, the persisting thing is obvious: Socrates himself stays the same while moving from unmusical to musical.

**The pressure point**: In substantial change, the *substance* is exactly what's coming or going, so it can't be the thing that persists. What does? **The substance's matter** — bricks survive the building of the house; elements survive the death of an organism.

If you deny this, you face an unwelcome conclusion: things pop in and out of existence from nothing (*ex nihilo*). Aristotle, siding with Parmenides, won't allow it. **Methodological texture**: His metaphysics takes observed phenomena as starting points and tries to preserve common sense beliefs.

### Key Refinement 1: Form ≠ Shape

The word "form" misleads. Artefact examples (the form of a bronze statue *is* its shape) obscure. **For organisms, form is definition or essence** — and essence is tied to *function*.

- A statue is human-shaped but isn't human (can't do what humans do)
- A thing's form is what-it-is-to-be-that-kind-of-thing (essence)
- **Human function**: thinking, perceiving, moving, desiring, eating, growing

The **homonymy principle** (extremely important): A dead body is only "homonymously" a body, the way a painted eye is only homonymously an eye. Both bear the name by resemblance but cannot perform the function the thing exists *for*.

*This functional, life-form-relative way of individuating is the deep Aristotelian backdrop to neo-Aristotelian naturalism (Thompson, Foot, Geach). The "dead flesh is only homonymously flesh" move is the same logic making natural-historical judgments life-form-relative.*

### Key Refinement 2: Collapse of the Four Causes

Aristotle lists four kinds of cause (material, formal, efficient, final), but notes that for living things the last three tend to collapse into one answer:

- A human is **defined as** something that lives a rational life (formal)
- That's **what a human is for** (final)
- Humans are **made by** humans (efficient)

So the four causes really reduce to **two ineliminable explanatory roles: matter and form**.

### The Core Puzzle: Artefact Matter vs. Organic Matter

**Artefact matter** is only *contingently* enmattered: the same bronze can be a statue, then melted into jewelry. That contingency lets matter play the "persisting substrate" role.

**Organic matter** looks different:

- Your body doesn't obviously pre-date you (unlike bricks before the house)
- By homonymy, it doesn't survive you either (dead body ≠ real body)
- Yet a human needs matter to underlie coming-to-be and passing-away

So human matter must be *contingently* alive (to underlie generation) **and** *essentially* alive (functionally defined). **This is a flat contradiction.**

### The Resolution: Levels of Matter

**This is the most important structural idea in Section 1.**

Matter is always **relative** — matter *of* something — and itself decomposes into matter-plus-form. It's hierarchical:

- Body → Flesh & bones → ... → Four elements

**Proximate matter** (the living body) is essentially alive and exists exactly as long as the organism does.  

**Remote matter** (ultimately the elements) need not be alive and can survive the organism's death.

The substrate underlying substantial generation lives at the *bottom* of the hierarchy. **This dissolves the contradiction.** The organism's proximate matter is essentially alive, but the substrate for generation-and-destruction is the remote matter (elements), which can be lifeless.

The homoiomerous (structureless stuffs)/heteromerous (structured organs) distinction and the denial of atomism (matter is infinitely divisible) ride along here.

## Section 2: Prime Matter

### The Question

How far down does the substrate go? **The pressure point is elemental change.**

The four elements transform into one another (water→air). By Aristotle's own analysis, this requires something persisting through the change. But it can't be *any* element — it must take on each element's properties in turn (hot, cold, wet, dry).

This seems to demand a **bottom rung: prime matter** — pure potentiality with no essential properties of its own. Aristotle contrasts it with unmoved movers (pure actuality). The Platonic ancestry is clear (the *Timaeus* receptacle, motivated by the identical elemental-transformation problem).

### The Two Revisionist Challenges

**Textual Challenge**

- Aristotle uses "prime matter" but says it's ambiguous (proximate vs. ultimate)
- He's often noncommittal: the *Metaphysics* ix 7 conditional ("*if* there is something primary…")
- The vii 3 passage ("by matter I mean that which in itself is neither substance nor quantity…") is strongest pro-prime-matter text, but opponents can read it as *reductio*

**Philosophical Challenge: Charlton's Move**

- Deny that change *always* requires something persisting
- In elemental change: the initial element underlies but doesn't persist; it's consumed
- **Ainsworth's objection**: Without persistence, what distinguishes water→air from water vanishing-into-nothing and air materializing-from-nothing? That's exactly what Aristotle forbids.

### The Two Objections to Prime Matter

**Epistemic objection** (weaker): Prime matter is unknowable; we only perceive what it underlies.  

*Ainsworth's reply*: Unobservables earn their keep through theoretical work (quarks).

**Logical objection** (stronger): Prime matter is supposed to have no essential properties (to take any form), yet we call it eternal, invisible, the ultimate bearer. **Worse**: when Socrates turns blue, the prime matter underlying him also "turns blue," so it's simultaneously invisible *and* blue.

*Proposed fix*: Distinguish prime matter's essential from accidental properties. But this threatens a regress (what underlies *those* essential properties?). **The stipulation**: prime matter underlies its own essential properties without being a further compound. Whether that's principled or ad hoc remains debated (Oderberg & Brower defend it; Koslicki resists).

## Section 3: The Principle of Individuation

### Popper's Dissolution (The Game-Changer)

**This is the single most clarifying move in the entry.**

Anscombe and Lukasiewicz look opposed (matter vs. form as principle of individuation), but they're answering **different questions**:

**Lukasiewicz**: What makes a thing-with-many-parts **one** individual rather than a heap?  

→ A question about **unity** → **Form** answers it.

**Anscombe**: What makes *this* individual **numerically distinct** from another of the same kind?  

→ A question about **distinctness** → **Matter** answers it.

**Both can be true at once.** Terminology:

- Call the unity-answer a **principle of unity**
- Reserve **principle of individuation** for the distinctness-answer

### Metaphysical vs. Epistemological

Second framing question: Is individuation **metaphysical** (what makes Socrates distinct from Callias?) or merely **epistemological** (how do we tell them apart)? Charlton presses the latter. (The skeptical challenge: maybe distinctness is just *brute* — nothing makes them distinct, they simply *are*.)

### Fine's Puzzle: The Argument

**This is the centerpiece and most technically important part.**

Here's a *valid argument* that matter-as-individuation cannot survive:

1. It's *possible* that Socrates and Callias are composed of numerically the same matter (at different times)
2. Socrates and Callias have the same form
3. Each is a compound of matter and form
4. **Therefore**, it's possible that Socrates and Callias are numerically the same — **which is absurd**

At least one premise must go.

### The Four Escape Routes

**Route 1: Equivocate on "matter"**  

Same *remote* matter (elements), different *proximate* matter (body).  

*Problem*: Push the "same form" requirement down through every level of matter, and the equivocation closes.

**Route 2: Reject P1** (matter is essential to the person)  

Plausible for proximate matter (homonymy), but not for remote matter: elements survive in dead stumps and migrate to new trees.  

*Prime matter doesn't rescue you either.*

**Route 3: Reject P2** (co-specific things share a form) → Requires **particular forms**  

*Problem*: If particular forms are just universals indexed to bearers, the form depends on the substance, and a form can't individuate the thing it depends on. Particular forms need independent definition (Koslicki bites this bullet).

**Route 4: Reject P3** (compounds are matter-plus-form *at a time*) → Relativize to times/worlds  

*Problem*: Clashes with Aristotle's unwavering commitment that form *unifies* the compound (*Metaphysics* viii 6, vii 17). Time can't be one more ingredient to unify, and letting form unify the same matter into different objects at different times stops deserving "unification." **Exegetically dead.**

### Ainsworth's Landing

Separate the two questions one final time:

- **Unity question**: What keeps *this* giraffe the same giraffe over time? ("Giraffeness in general" may suffice.)
- **Individuation question**: What makes *this* giraffe distinct from *that* one? (Neither shared universal nor potentially-shared matter can do it.)

**Particular forms** are the only candidate left standing.

## Section 4: Matter-Involving Forms

### The Motive: Anti-Platonism

Aristotle wants his forms *enmeshed in matter* (unlike Plato's separable Forms). The banner case is the **snub** — concavity-realized-in-a-nose. You cannot specify the form without mentioning the matter.

The question: How literally to take this? Does the form *itself contain* a specification of its matter (Balme, Charles, Peramatzis), or is that a conflation of the very roles form and matter were introduced to keep apart (Frede, Corkum, Meister)?

### The Textual Underdetermination

*Metaphysics* vii 11 is genuinely undecidable:

- The pivotal sentence about flesh and bones can be printed as a **statement** ("in truth no, they are the matter…" — flesh/bones are *not* part of form) or a **question** ("or are they rather matter…?" — leaving open that they *are*)
- Punctuation is editorial; the Greek doesn't decide
- Surrounding passages cut both ways

**Verdict**: The textual battlefield is a draw.

### The Philosophical Motivation

**Only material things change.** If forms are to explain a thing's characteristic changes, they must be matter-involving.

Example: Falling-when-unsupported in humans → explained by being made of heavy elements.

**The deflationary reply** runs through **hypothetical necessity** and ***idia***:

- Properties like "all and only humans can laugh" attach necessarily to a species but aren't part of its essence
- Laughter *follows from* rationality *plus how the world is*
- Same with "all ducks waddle": waddling isn't in the duck's function — anything meeting the functional spec must, in a world like ours, walk inelegantly

This is the **natural-historical judgment** that Thompson builds *Life and Action* around, and Foot's Aristotelian categoricals.

**Key point**: A characteristic generic can be necessary and life-form-relative *without* being written into the essence — so the form needn't ingest its matter to explain the change.

### Four Positions on Matter-Involvement

(Ascending order of matter-involvement)

1. **Pure forms**: The compound's form/essence is not matter-involving
2. The compound's form *involves* matter, but *that* form has a further essence that's pure
3. As (2), but forms themselves *have no* further essences
4. As (2)/(3), and forms are themselves matter-form compounds with further matter-involving essences, all the way down

**Position (4) generates a vicious infinite regress** — an endless series of forms behind every compound, which finite knowers could never complete.

**Position (2) escapes** because its second-order form is pure.

**Position (3) is unstable**: Forms are *definitions*, definitions have structure with separable parts. You can ask of those parts whether they're matter-involving. If some are, you slide toward the (4) regress; if none are, you become "intolerably similar to (2)." Can't hold ground.

**The apparently-verbal (1) vs. (2) question isn't merely verbal**: (2) posits *two* essences/forms per substance. Since an essence is a *thing in the world* (the human essence *is* a soul), that's a genuine extra ontological commitment. (1) needs only a broader *definition* mentioning matter, which costs nothing. Unless matter-involving forms earn real theoretical work, **parsimony favors pure forms**.

## Conclusion: The Structural Rhyme

Across all three controversies, the same pattern:

1. **Traditional reading**: Aristotle makes a robust metaphysical commitment (prime matter; matter-as-individuator; matter-involving forms)
2. **Revisionist reading**: Deflates or relocates the commitment (non-persisting substrate; particular forms; pure-forms-plus-broad-definition)
3. **Deciding consideration**: Textual underdetermination **plus** charity/parsimony argument

**It's a remarkably consistent dialectical engine.**

The net effect: **Hylomorphism is explanatorily powerful but metaphysically fragile.** The form/matter contrast that elegantly solves the change-problem buckles under load when applied to individuation, prime matter, and the form-matter interface. Yet Aristotle keeps deploying it because, despite the strain, it still *works* across a wide range of explanatory domains. That productive elasticity is the entry's deepest insight.
