# Creativity, Art, and Artificial Intelligence: A Comparative Analysis of Margaret Boden and Mark Coeckelbergh

## **Introduction**

Margaret A. Boden’s *“Creativity and Artificial Intelligence”* and Mark Coeckelbergh’s *“Can Machines Create Art?”* present two distinct philosophical approaches to understanding creativity in the context of AI and art. Both authors engage with debates in aesthetics, the philosophy of mind, and the philosophy of technology, but they do so with different assumptions and methods. This essay reconstructs each author’s argument structure – laying out their explicit and implicit premises, theoretical assumptions, and dialectical strategies – and then contrasts their views. The sharpest contrast emerges in how Boden and Coeckelbergh treat the nature of creativity, the role of aesthetic judgment, and the boundary (or lack thereof) between human and machine agency. Throughout, we focus on conceptual clarity and rigorous analysis of their arguments, using minimal citation for key points.

## **Margaret Boden: *Creativity and Artificial Intelligence***

Boden’s project is to **demystify creativity** and show that it can be understood (and potentially replicated) through ordinary cognitive processes. Her approach is grounded in cognitive science and AI research, treating creativity not as a miraculous gift but as a puzzle that science can solve. 

**Explicit Premise 1:** *Creativity is a fundamental, natural feature of human intelligence, not an ineffable mystery.* 

**Explicit Premise 2:** *Creative ideas are those that are both novel and valuable (or meaningful) in some context.* 

From these starting points, Boden argues that we can analyze creativity in computational terms.

**Structural Argument:** Boden begins by distinguishing two levels of novelty in creative ideas: *psychological creativity* (P-creativity), where an idea is new to the person who thought of it, and *historical creativity* (H-creativity), where an idea is new to humanity at large. She emphasizes P-creativity because it points to the **underlying mental mechanisms** that generate originality – mechanisms that *“may, or may not, be simulated or even instantiated in computers”*. In other words, if we understand the cognitive processes that produce new ideas in an individual, we can ask whether a computer program could implement similar processes. This reflects an **implicit theoretical assumption** in Boden’s work: *human minds are, at some level, information-processing systems*, so *any mental ability (creativity included) might be reproducible in an artificial system*.

Next, Boden introduces **three types of creativity**, corresponding to three types of *surprise* we feel when encountering a new idea. Each type is linked to a different psychological mechanism:

- *Combinational creativity*: generating novel ideas by unfamiliar combinations of familiar concepts (e.g. metaphor in poetry, collage in art). This relies on associative memory and recombination.
- *Exploratory creativity*: innovating by exploring a structured *conceptual space* or style governed by certain rules or constraints (e.g. writing a novel fugue within the rules of Baroque counterpoint). The process involves searching within established boundaries for new configurations.
- *Transformational creativity*: achieving radical innovation by transforming the defining constraints of a conceptual space, thereby creating something previously “impossible” or unthinkable (e.g. Picasso’s invention of Cubism, which initially shocked audiences by breaking prior pictorial rules). This entails altering or dropping some foundational constraints to generate a new space of possibilities.

By articulating these categories, Boden structurally shows that **creativity can be analyzed in terms of rule-based operations and search spaces** rather than divine inspiration.

 **Explicit Premise 3:** *If creativity results from identifiable cognitive mechanisms (combination, exploration, transformation), then a sufficiently designed AI could implement these mechanisms.* 

In support of this, Boden and others in computational creativity have documented software that produces *“respectable, albeit lesser, examples of (apparent) AI creativity* – for instance, programs that compose music, draw paintings, or solve mathematical conjectures in ways that surprise even their programmers. Such examples reinforce her claim that creativity is not an exclusively human magic but a spectrum of **intelligent behaviors that machines can mimic or instantiate**.

**Dialectical Method:** Boden’s essay has a subtle dialectical structure: she sets up the **Romantic mystique** of creativity (the view that creativity is an inscrutable gift of genius or inspiration) as the foil, and then she systematically dismantles that mystique by providing a **naturalistic explanation**. An explicit theoretical stance she takes is that *“creativity depends on ordinary mental abilities”* and should be explained scientifically rather than shrouded in mystery. She even characterizes her mission as one of *“disenchantment”* – replacing magical or romantic notions with clear cognitive accounts. In this dialectic, Boden anticipates the objection famously raised by Lady Lovelace (that machines only do what they are programmed to do and therefore cannot truly create). She responds by reframing the issue: 

**Explicit Premise 4:** *Originality can occur in AI when the system’s output was not explicitly anticipated by the programmer.* 

For example, an evolutionary art program might “surprise” its creator by generating an unforeseen design. Boden implies that when a computer’s processes yield a novel, valuable result by following its own rules (however programmed), we have at least *the appearance* of creativity. She downplays the metaphysical worry about whether the machine is “**truly**” creative, calling that a *philosophical question about the meaning of ‘creative’* rather than a scientific question of mechanismfile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. In her view, if the outcomes meet the criteria for creativity (novelty and value) and arise from a generative process, then practically speaking the machine has **achieved** creativity (whether or not one labels it “real” creativity is a semantic choice).

Underlying Boden’s argument is the **assumption of continuity between human and machine agency**. She treats human creativity as an advanced form of problem-solving that can be studied and potentially replicated. Thus, she **blurs the human–machine boundary**: a computer program that explores a space of musical patterns or recombines ideas is fundamentally doing, in her eyes, what a human creator does (albeit in a limited domain). Any differences are of degree, not of kind. The **implicit premises** include a form of computational functionalism (the idea that what matters are the information processes, not the substrate – silicon or neurons) and a confidence that *evaluation* of creativity (determining value) can be handled externally by human judges or by specifying criteria. Boden does not extensively delve into aesthetic philosophy; she mostly assumes that if experts or audiences recognize a work as novel and valuable, then it qualifies as creative art. In short, Boden’s contribution is a **structural theory of creativity**: she provides a framework of how new ideas arise and contends that machines, by implementing these structured methods, *can cross into the territory of creativity*. Her argument is rigorous in breaking creativity into analyzable components and optimistic in drawing the boundary between human and machine as permeable.

## **Mark Coeckelbergh: *Can Machines Create Art?***

Coeckelbergh approaches the question of machine creativity from a **philosophical and conceptual angle**, rather than a purely cognitive or technical one. His method is explicitly dialectical and interrogative: instead of assuming a fixed definition of creativity or art, he **breaks the main question into three sub-questions** and examines each in turnfile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. These guiding questions are: (1) *What is art creation (the creative process)?* (2) *What do we mean by art (the product or outcome)?* and (3) *What does it mean for machines to “create” art (the agency or attribution)?* By unpacking the big question in this way, Coeckelbergh aims to clarify the conceptual terrain and avoid simplistic yes/no answers.

**Structural Argument:** Coeckelbergh’s analysis proceeds systematically through the dimensions of *process* and *product*. He notes that in discussions of computational creativity, one can focus on **creative process** (the *internal* workings that lead to an artwork) or on the **creative product** (the *external* artifact or performance)file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. This mirrors two intuitive criteria for answering “Can machines create art?”:

- **Process-centric view:** A machine truly *creates* art only if the *way* it produces the art involves something analogous to human creativity (imagination, intentionality, perhaps even consciousness or emotion). This view demands that the machine’s internal process meets certain standards (often tied to having a mind or genuine originality). It aligns with what Coeckelbergh calls “strong computational creativity” – the machine doesn’t just mimic a creative process, but *has* one in a meaningful sensefile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. For example, one might argue that to *create art* a machine should have its own intentions or imaginative capacity, not just churn out combinations randomly.
- **Product-centric view:** A machine creates art if the *outcome* it produces is indistinguishable from human-made art or is recognized as artistically valuable. This is akin to an *“art Turing test”*file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7 – if people judge the output as art of human-level quality, then we may say the machine created art, regardless of how it was generated. Coeckelbergh cites Margaret Boden’s formulation of this test: a program would pass if it produces artwork that is *“indistinguishable from one produced by a human and/or is seen as having as much aesthetic value as a human-produced work”*file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7.

Coeckelbergh highlights that passing such an output test leads to a further question: *even if a machine’s art fools us or pleases us, is the machine **really** creative?*file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7 This question pushes the debate from the product back to the process (echoing the Lovelace objection in another form). Many researchers, including Boden, respond by shifting to the process-centric approach – trying to define creativity in a way that requires certain capacities (like imagination or self-evaluation) beyond just producing pretty outputsfile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. This move introduces **implicit Premise 1 in Coeckelbergh’s argument:** *the criteria for creativity can be categorized as either external/behavioral (focused on outcomes) or internal/essential (focused on processes and capacities).*

Coeckelbergh’s theoretical contribution is to **critically examine these criteria** and their stability. He points out that the distinction between focusing on process versus outcome, and similarly between **objective criteria** (measurable features of the product or process) versus **subjective criteria** (judgments by observers or creators), is not as clear-cut as it seemsfile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. For instance, an “objective” measure of creativity (like novelty or statistical rarity of the artwork) ultimately relies on a context of human appreciation to matter. Conversely, a “subjective” judgment (like a viewer finding a piece moving or original) often assumes some objective features in the work that elicit that response. **Implicit Premise 2:** *Our evaluation of art and creativity is a hybrid of objective features and subjective interpretation, and the two aspects cannot be completely separated.* Coeckelbergh thus argues that traditional criteria for creativity are **unstable** or porous: focusing solely on the artwork’s qualities or solely on the creator’s mind misses the interplay between the two. Creativity might require both a certain kind of process *and* a reception that deems the result creative.

To further complicate matters, Coeckelbergh encourages expanding the discussion beyond an either/or stance of human vs. machine. He introduces the idea of **non-human forms of creativity and human–machine collaboration**. Rather than only asking if a standalone machine can be an artist, he suggests we consider scenarios where humans and AI systems create art together, or cases where the machine’s role in the creative process is significant but not entirely autonomousfile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. This reflects an **implicit theoretical assumption** grounded in philosophy of technology and posthumanist thinking: *creativity need not be an exclusively human trait; it can emerge from interactions in a network of human and technological agency*. By examining examples of algorithmic art, interactive music systems, or robotic co-creators, Coeckelbergh shows that the boundary between tool and creative partner can be blurry. Here his dialectical method is to **soften the strict binary** of human vs machine creativity. If a human artist uses an AI system that unpredictably contributes to the outcome, who is the creator? Coeckelbergh uses such questions to illustrate that our concept of “artist” might need revision when technology is deeply involved.

Crucially, Coeckelbergh also **reflects on the role of the observer and the artworld** in granting the status of art. He notes that some definitions of computational creativity *“put the criterion on the side of human observation”* – what matters is not an intrinsic property of the machine, but whether humans *“deem the behavior or product creative”*file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. In other words, if an unbiased observer would judge the machine’s painting as creative, then by that definition the machine is creative. This perspective ties into institutional and relational theories of art: art is something that gets recognized and interpreted as art by human communities.

A painting created by an algorithm might be considered true art if, say, critics and audiences treat it as such in galleries and imbue it with meaning. After analyzing process vs product and subjective vs objective angles, Coeckelbergh takes a step back in a **dialectical turn**. He **questions the very approach of trying to settle the issue with fixed criteria**file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. Perhaps, he suggests, no checklist of conditions will definitively tell us when a machine is creating art. Instead, he proposes an alternative insight: *the artistic or creative status of machines may be something that is **revealed in practice**, in the encounter between humans and machines, before we even formulate theories or criteria.* In the closing of his paper, Coeckelbergh invokes a phenomenological and existential perspective (alluding to Heidegger’s notion of *poiesis*, or the bringing-forth of art). He hints that when humans interact with creative machines – when we listen to a musical piece composed by AI or behold a painting generated by an algorithm – **something happens in that encounter** that can show us the machine’s potential as an artist **prior** to our conceptual verdict. He writes that this experience can be a *hybrid human-technological and emergent (even “poetic”) process*, one that *“leaves more room for letting ourselves be surprised by creativity – human and perhaps non-human”*file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. Here Coeckelbergh’s **implicit Premise 3** is that *art and creativity are, at root, experiential and emergent phenomena*: they arise through engagement and cannot be fully captured by abstract definitions alone. This stance is a philosophical move to **transcend the binary debate**. Coeckelbergh is effectively saying: *if we remain too rigid in defining art or creativity, we might miss new forms of creativity that do not fit our prior definitions*. Instead, we should remain open to being surprised and to revising our concepts as technology expands the realm of the possible.

In summary, Coeckelbergh’s argument is both analytic and exploratory. He uses a **dialectical method**: posing questions, examining opposing views (e.g. a Romantic view of artistic creation vs a more mechanistic view, subjective vs objective tests), and then synthesizing a more nuanced perspective. His theoretical assumptions are rooted in *philosophical aesthetics* (that art’s meaning emerges in context and perception) and in *philosophy of technology* (that our relation with technology can redefine human capacities). Unlike Boden, who largely treats the meaning of “creativity” as given for the sake of scientific inquiry, Coeckelbergh delves deeply into *what we mean* by creativity and art. This makes his approach fundamentally more reflexive: he is not only asking *can machines do it*, but also *what does “it” entail?* and *how might machines change our understanding of it?*

## **Contrasting Perspectives: Creativity, Judgment, and Agency**

Both Boden and Coeckelbergh engage with whether machines can be creative and produce art, but they diverge sharply along key conceptual axes. The contrast can be summarized as **Boden’s naturalistic, process-based optimism** versus **Coeckelbergh’s critical, redefinition-oriented analysis**. We examine three focal points of contrast: their views on *creativity itself*, on *aesthetic judgment*, and on *the human–machine agency boundary*.

### **Conceptions of Creativity**

For Margaret Boden, *creativity* is a phenomenon that can be **explained and engineered**. She treats creativity as comprised of identifiable cognitive abilities (like recombination, exploration of conceptual spaces, etc.), which are ultimately ordinary mental processes arranged in extraordinary ways. An underlying assumption in her work is that **there is no mystical gap** separating creative thought from other kinds of thought – it’s all potentially within the reach of AI once we understand the algorithms of innovation. This yields a fundamentally **continuist view**: human creativity is on a continuum with machine operations. Boden’s **dialectical stance** is explicitly anti-mystical; she challenges the notion that creativity is a sacred human-only quality. In her structural reconstruction, *if X can be described in mechanistic terms, then X can in principle be implemented by a machine.* Thus, she is inclined to believe that *machine creativity is possible (and already partially realized)* because the **premises that deny it (e.g. “only humans can genuinely create”) rest on Romantic intuitions rather than analysis**.

Mark Coeckelbergh, however, is concerned that *creativity* as a concept might **lose important dimensions if we rush to reduce it to algorithms**. He acknowledges Boden’s project of explaining creativity scientifically, but he brings to light what Boden sets aside: questions of *meaning, imagination, and embodiment*. For Coeckelbergh, an **explicit premise** is that *how we define creativity crucially affects whether we think machines can have it*. If creativity is defined **thinly** – say, as mere novelty-generation – then machines can certainly appear creative. But if defined **thickly** – involving insight, intentionality, or conscious experience – then current machines fall short. Unlike Boden, who largely treats creativity as *problem-solving + surprise*, Coeckelbergh entertains the possibility that *creativity might require elements (like subjective experience or an embodied perspective) that AI does not yet possess*. He discusses arguments that **imagination or physical embodiment** might be prerequisites for genuine creativityfile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. These are theoretical assumptions often found in philosophy of mind: for instance, one might argue that a bodiless algorithm can’t truly *imagine* or that creativity arises from the richness of human experience in the world – something AI lacks.

The **sharpest axis of contrast** here is *explanation vs. interpretation*: Boden’s stance is that creativity *can be fully explained* by scientific theory (making it amenable to replication), whereas Coeckelbergh suggests creativity *may need to be interpreted or reconceived* when considering machines. Boden is content to say a machine that follows certain procedures can be creative in a meaningful way. Coeckelbergh is more hesitant – he keeps asking, *what do we really mean by “creative” in the first place?* This philosophical turn leads him to consider new forms of creativity (e.g. collaborative or “emergent” creativity) rather than simply extending the human model to machines. In essence, Boden *naturalizes* creativity, while Coeckelbergh *problematizes* it. Boden’s theoretical orientation is firmly within *cognitive science* (creativity as an algorithmic capability), whereas Coeckelbergh’s is within *philosophical hermeneutics and phenomenology* (creativity as a concept whose meaning can evolve and whose manifestation might surprise us).

### **Aesthetic Judgment and Value**

Another major difference lies in how each author treats **aesthetic judgment** – the evaluation and appreciation of art and creativity. Boden’s treatment of value is straightforward and **externalist**: a creative idea or artwork is one that is *recognized* as novel and valuable by the relevant community (experts, society, etc.). She implicitly relies on the art world or domain experts to validate the products of creativity. For example, if a computer program composes a musical piece, and human listeners find it genuinely interesting or beautiful (meeting the standards of the genre), then the piece qualifies as creative art. Boden does not deeply investigate *who* the judge is or *what* makes something aesthetically valuable beyond novelty and usefulness; she presumes a common-sense notion of value. In philosophical terms, she leans on a **quasi-objective** criterion: creativity can be partially measured (novelty can be quantified to some degree, value can be assessed by consensus). She is not concerned that aesthetic judgment might be subjective or culturally contingent – for her purposes, if the machine’s output passes the “as good as humans” test, that sufficesfile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7.

Coeckelbergh, on the other hand, delves into the **subjective and intersubjective** nature of aesthetic judgment. He explicitly discusses the difference between *subjective* criteria of creativity (e.g. *“Do I as an observer feel this is creative or artistic?”*) and *objective* criteria (e.g. *“Is this piece structured in a formally novel way?”*). He argues that these distinctions are unstable, meaning you cannot cleanly separate the value of art from the experience of value. A painting might be statistically novel (objective), but if no one perceives or appreciates it as such, can we call it creative? Conversely, people might *feel* an artwork is profoundly creative (subjective) even if, by some metrics, it’s derivative. Coeckelbergh emphasizes the role of the **observer and the context**: in one approach, *“what matters is not whether a computer is creative but whether it can **appear** to be creative”*file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7. This draws from aesthetic theory the notion that art is something that happens in the encounter – a view resonant with thinkers like Dewey or Heidegger who locate art’s significance in the experience. Thus, Coeckelbergh is much more sensitive to the *interpretative* aspect of aesthetic judgment: art isn’t art until we (as an audience or community) engage with it and give it meaning.

The contrast can be summarized as *Boden’s criterion-driven value vs. Coeckelbergh’s encounter-driven value*. Boden implies a stable standard: novelty + value (where value can be pragmatic or aesthetic quality) determined by relatively straightforward evaluation (even if by humans). Coeckelbergh suggests that the *evaluation itself is part of what defines art*. He even hints that the **status** of machine art might be decided in the gallery or concert hall rather than the lab – i.e. if people start treating AI-generated works as art, that social reality may trump any theoretical objections. This aligns with an **institutional theory of art** (art is what the art world accepts) and with a **pragmatic view of creativity** (creativity is as creativity does, in practice). Coeckelbergh’s openness to being “surprised” by machine creativityfile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7 underscores that we should allow our aesthetic judgments to evolve. Boden’s view, conversely, would measure machine outputs against existing human standards (the machine’s painting should be as aesthetically good as a human’s). Coeckelbergh might respond that machines could introduce new aesthetics altogether, challenging us to broaden what we consider valuable or beautiful. In short, Boden focuses on **outcome quality** as judged by current human criteria, whereas Coeckelbergh focuses on **the process of judgment** itself and how it might adapt in a human–machine collaborative future.

### **Human vs. Machine Agency**

Finally, the two thinkers diverge on the perceived **boundary between human and machine agency** in creativity. Boden’s analysis effectively **minimizes the gap** between human creators and AI “creators.” By highlighting ordinary mental processes and showing how a program can emulate them, she portrays machine agency in creativity as a logical extension of human technique. She even uses examples of programs that *autonomously* generate results to argue that the machine can *originate* ideas (at least in a limited sense). Boden acknowledges that current AI is not as generally creative as humans, but she sees no principled barrier: with more advanced algorithms, greater computational power, or better simulations of brain processes, machines could approach human-level creativity. Importantly, she frames the question of *“is the machine really creative or just a tool?”* as largely semantic. If the machine’s output is creative, then the machine can be credited with creativity – we need not insist on some metaphysical free will or soul. In her view, *agency* in creativity can be partial or distributed: a computer can contribute creative moves that its human programmer did not explicitly design, thereby acting as an agent of creativity. Boden’s stance here is in line with *AI as an autonomous system*, at least to the extent that it surprises its creators. Her theoretical leanings (like the possibility of evolutionary algorithms yielding unpredictable novelty) support the idea that machines can have a form of agency (they can “surprise even their programmers”file-hulxpjw3ssqyf7ftsjx1ev).

Coeckelbergh’s perspective on agency is more nuanced and relational. Rather than simply asking “can the machine be an autonomous creative agent?”, he asks us to consider *how agency is shared or perceived*. He explicitly brings in scenarios of **human–machine co-creation**file-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7, which suggest that agency in art can be a **distributed property**. For example, if a human artist uses an AI tool that generates parts of a painting, the creative agency is partly human (choosing and guiding the tool) and partly machine (producing novel patterns). Coeckelbergh invites reflection on *human–technology relations*: technology might act as an extension of the artist’s mind, or even as a collaborator that introduces its own twists. In these cases, the boundary of agency is blurred – creativity emerges from the *interaction*. This is a fundamentally different way of conceptualizing agency compared to Boden’s more individualistic model (where either a human or a machine, considered separately, has the creative process). Coeckelbergh’s dialectic suggests that insisting on a strict division (“either the human is the real artist or the machine is”) might be an oversimplification. Instead, he leans towards a view of **hybrid agency**: art creation can be an **ensemble act**, involving multiple agents (human and non-human) in a network. This aligns with philosophical anthropology insights that our tools become part of our cognitive and creative process.

Another axis of contrast on agency is **intentionality and meaning**. Boden is relatively silent on whether a machine “understands” or “intends” its creations – for her, having intentional states is not a prerequisite for being creative (after all, we consider algorithmic processes in human cognition creative even if we aren’t conscious of them). Coeckelbergh, conversely, gives weight to the idea of *intentionality* in art. He asks implicitly: Can we say a machine *meant* to create this art? Does it *about* something or express something? Traditional aesthetics often ties art to the artist’s intention or the expressive act. Coeckelbergh doesn’t offer a simple answer but highlights that **if we require intentional agency, machines as currently designed lack this (they do not form intentions or interpretations of what they create)**. This could be a point against machine agency: a machine painting has no inner story or feeling it wishes to convey. However, Coeckelbergh also questions whether demanding human-like intentionality is necessary to appreciate machine creations. Perhaps meaning can emerge *after* the fact through our interpretation (a very postmodern notion where the author’s intent is not paramount). Thus, Coeckelbergh keeps the boundary question open: he acknowledges a **difference** – machines today operate without lived experience or self-reflective intention – yet he also imagines that art might arise from machines in new ways that don’t map neatly onto human agency.

The **sharp contrast** can be encapsulated thus: **Boden flattens the human–machine distinction** by showing functional equivalences (a program can do in principle what a mind does), whereas **Coeckelbergh explores the human–machine relation** as something that could redefine creativity (the presence of machines could change how we view authorship and agency in art). Boden’s world is one where machines join humans as creators by meeting human-defined criteria; Coeckelbergh’s is one where the very roles of “creator” and “tool” are in flux, and we must philosophically navigate this new landscape.

## **Conclusion**

Margaret Boden and Mark Coeckelbergh offer two philosophically rich, yet contrasting, contributions to the debate on AI and creativity. Boden’s essay is characterized by **structural precision and explanatory ambition**: she dissects creativity into types and mechanisms, arguing that what makes humans creative can be understood scientifically and thus *potentially built* into machines. Her argument proceeds from clear premises about novelty and cognition, and she employs a dialectical move against the romantic view of creativity, insisting that *nothing about creativity fundamentally forbids automation*. In doing so, Boden blurs the line between human and machine, treating creativity as a general phenomenon of intelligence rather than a sacred human privilege.

Coeckelbergh’s paper, by contrast, is marked by **conceptual clarity and philosophical rigor** in interrogating the question itself. He does not start with a fixed definition of creativity or art, but rather *constructs the question* through sub-questions and an analysis of criteria. His dialectical method engages multiple viewpoints – from classical aesthetics to contemporary AI debates – and reveals tensions between them (process vs outcome, appearance vs reality, human vs non-human). Coeckelbergh’s greatest contribution is perhaps his **structural reframing**: he shifts the focus from “Can machines match humans?” to “What do we consider art and creativity, and could those concepts expand?”. He highlights that the **sharpest contrast** might not be between human and machine, but between different *conceptions of creativity*. Ultimately, Coeckelbergh suggests a philosophical humility: the meeting of human and machine in art may surprise us and teach us new ways to think about creativity itselffile-p9qsx8ebbkd4ijuh22pbl7.

The sharpest axis of contrast between Boden and Coeckelbergh lies in **their treatment of the nature of creativity and how it ties to agency**. Boden treats creativity as *algorithmically tractable*, rooted in mental mechanics and measurable surprise – a view that effectively extends human agency to machines if they perform suitably. Coeckelbergh treats creativity as *conceptually and experientially emergent*, something that might not be fully capturable by checklists and that challenges the clear-cut agency of an individual. In terms of **aesthetic judgment**, Boden assumes a stable standard of value (against which machines can be evaluated), whereas Coeckelbergh sees aesthetic judgment as part of the evolving story – the way we judge machine art could itself redefine what counts as art.

Both authors operate at the intersection of aesthetics, technology, and mind, but they play different roles: Boden is the *demystifier*, bringing creativity into the realm of cognitive science and AI engineering, whereas Coeckelbergh is the *interrogator*, examining our assumptions and urging a reconceptualization in light of new technological possibilities. Together, their works form a dialogue – one optimistic that AI can participate in creativity on human terms, the other cautious and curious about how AI might change the terms. This comparative analysis, by reconstructing their arguments, underscores how philosophy can both guide and question the advancement of AI in creative domains, ensuring we understand not just *whether* machines can create art, but *what it means* for something to be a creator or a work of art in the first place.

## 1. **철학적 개입의 양상: 주장이라기보다 정위(定位)의 시도**

Boden은 *“Creativity and Artificial Intelligence”*에서 명시적으로 어떤 강한 철학적 결론을 도출하지 않습니다. 오히려 그녀의 주된 목적은 다음과 같이 구조화된 **논쟁 지형을 제시**하는 데 있습니다.

- **어떤 방식으로 창의성의 개념이 구성되어 왔는가?** (e.g., combinational, exploratory, transformational creativity)
- **어떤 메커니즘들이 컴퓨터에 의해 구현 가능한가?**
- **‘진짜 창의성’이라는 철학적 개념은 어떤 의미로 이해되어야 하는가?**

그 결과, Boden은 다음과 같은 입장을 취합니다:

> “The question of whether a computer could be really creative involves issues—about autonomy, intentionality, and consciousness—that we are far from understanding well enough to settle philosophically.” (요약적 진술)
> 

즉, 그녀는 *논쟁적 결론을 유보하면서*, 현재의 과학적 성과들과 개념적 틀들을 소개하는 데 초점을 둡니다. 이런 점에서 그녀는 **정념적 철학자라기보다, 개념적 지도 제작자 혹은 해설자**의 입장을 취하고 있습니다.

---

## 2. **AI와 철학의 관계: 해석보다 정합적 모델링**

Coeckelbergh는 현상의 의미를 **해석적-현상학적 관점에서** 재구성하려는 반면, Boden은 인간 정신을 **기능적, 구성적 측면에서 모델링할 수 있다는 전제**를 수용하고 그 연장선상에서 AI가 어느 정도까지 창의성을 구현할 수 있는지를 따집니다. 하지만 그녀는 다음과 같은 **철학적 한계 인식**도 동시에 제시합니다:

- *Autonomy*와 *intentionality*의 본질은 아직 과학적으로 해명되지 않았다.
- *Machine consciousness*에 대한 논의는 여전히 이론적으로 불안정하다.
- *Creativity*를 설명하는 객관적 기준이 존재한다고 보기 어렵다.

따라서 그녀는 다음과 같이 말합니다:

> "To ask whether a computer could ‘really’ be creative is to ask whether any of the concepts considered in section 4 is forever barred to computers… The answer is clear in only one case, that of autonomy. The others must remain open."
> 

이와 같이, 그녀는 개념들을 **구획**하면서도, 철학적 결론에 대해서는 명시적인 **불가지론(agnosticism)**을 견지합니다.

## 3. **철학적 논증이 아닌 과학적-구성적 가능성의 스펙트럼 제시**

마지막으로 Boden의 글은 철학적 개념을 직접적으로 해석하거나 규범적으로 규정하려는 시도보다, **컴퓨터가 수행한 작업의 종류와 그 구조적 특성을 제시하고, 그것이 철학적 개념들과 어떤 관계를 맺는지를 ‘간접적으로’ 탐색**하는 데에 가깝습니다. 예를 들어, 그녀는 evolutionary programming, GAs, Sims의 예시 등을 통해 다음과 같은 방식으로 논의합니다:

- 컴퓨터는 입력값과 환경적 조건에 따라 예기치 않은 결과를 낼 수 있다.
- 그것은 인간이 기대하지 않았다는 점에서 ‘surprising’할 수 있다.
- 그런 사례들은 창의성의 외형적 조건은 만족시키지만, 내적인 의도성이나 자율성은 결여할 수 있다.

그 결과, 그녀는 **기술적 가능성과 철학적 본질 규정 사이의 간극**을 강조하게 됩니다.

## 종합 평가

따라서 Coeckelbergh가 **개념 분석과 해석학적 자기반성**을 통해 철학적 언어 자체를 재조정하려는 데 반해, Boden은 **자신이 속한 인지과학적 틀 내에서의 설명 가능성과 그 한계들을 명시적으로 조망**하고자 합니다. 요약하자면:

| 항목 | Boden | Coeckelbergh |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 개입 방식 | 설명적, 지도 제작 | 해석적, 개념 전복 |
| 철학적 태도 | 불가지론적 실증주의 | 관계론적 현상학 |
| 논증 방식 | 구조적 구획 + 사례 정리 | 개념 분석 + 규범 반성 |
| 목적 | 가능성 탐색 | 개념 확장 / 질문의 재구성 |

결론적으로, **Boden은 주장보다는 상황 정리자**, **Coeckelbergh는 정리보다는 질문 재구성자**의 위치에 서 있습니다. 이 차이는 두 논문이 AI와 예술, 창의성을 다루는 방식에서 **철학적 행위의 양식** 자체가 달라짐을 보여주는 흥미로운 사례라 할 수 있습니다.
