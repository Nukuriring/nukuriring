# Comparing Theories of Love: Frankfurt, Velleman, and Kolodny

## Harry Frankfurt: Love as Volitional Necessity and Caring

**Guiding Puzzle:** Frankfurt is motivated by questions about what gives our lives meaning and direction – **“How should we live, and what do we care about?”**[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=this%20short%20book%2C%20Frankfurt%20explores,of%20love%20like%20romantic%20love). He observes that love seems to impose its own *necessities* on us, binding our will much like moral duties or pragmatic needs do[cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/necessity-volition-and-love/autonomy-necessity-and-love/5696160C9BA0DBB70B379E326DA7029E#:~:text=One%20category%20of%20such%20actions,addition%20the%20necessities%20of%20love). The puzzle is how love, which is not chosen by deliberate decision, can nonetheless command our will and define what is important to us. Frankfurt seeks to explain the nature of love as a distinctive motivational force in our lives.

**Central Thesis:** Frankfurt’s core commitment is that **love is a special kind of caring** that is **ineluctably personal and entails “volitional necessity.”** In other words, love involves caring about someone (or something) so deeply that the lover *cannot help but* regard the beloved’s interests as supremely important – **the lover’s will is constrained by this caring**[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63). Importantly, love is **“most centrally, a disinterested concern”** for the beloved’s well-being[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=love%20is%20a%20particular%20mode,of%20a%20person%20to%20love). This means the lover values the beloved *for the beloved’s own sake*, not as a means to some self-interested end[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,the%20person%20who%20is%20loved). For Frankfurt, what we love constitutes what we *must* care about, thereby conferring importance and guiding our practical reasoning[cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/necessity-volition-and-love/autonomy-necessity-and-love/5696160C9BA0DBB70B379E326DA7029E#:~:text=One%20category%20of%20such%20actions,addition%20the%20necessities%20of%20love).

**Rejected Views:** Frankfurt pointedly **rejects the idea that love responds to rational *criteria* or reasons**. Against any view that we love someone *because* of their attractive qualities or moral merit, Frankfurt argues that love is not grounded in an antecedent appraisal of value[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be). In fact, he suggests it’s the other way around: **we do not love things because we find them valuable; rather, they become especially valuable to us because we love them**. He also distinguishes love from mere desires or liking – for example, loving a person is not the same as enjoying chocolate ice cream[cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/necessity-volition-and-love/autonomy-necessity-and-love/5696160C9BA0DBB70B379E326DA7029E#:~:text=I%20shall%20not%20attempt%20to,the%20piano%20music%20of%20Chopin). By rejecting **reason-based or instrumental** accounts of love, Frankfurt sets his view apart from theories (like Velleman’s or any “quality-based” theory) that seek justification for love. Love, in Frankfurt’s view, is **not under our direct voluntary control**[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=love,reader%20a%20better%20understanding%20of)[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63) and thus cannot be commanded or willed into existence by reasons or decisions.

**Key Features of Frankfurt’s Account:** Frankfurt analyzes love by identifying four essential features that distinguish love as a species of caring[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=proposes%20that%20there%20are%20%E2%80%9Cfour,End%20Page%201):

- **Disinterested Concern:** Love consists of a **“disinterested concern for the well-being or flourishing”** of the beloved[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=love,End%20Page%201). The lover genuinely wishes the beloved well *for the beloved’s sake*. If one only valued the beloved for selfish reasons or instrumental gain, that would not count as genuine love[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20states%20that%20love%20is,to%20push%20for%20this%20policy). (Frankfurt notes that in cases like romantic/sexual love, additional elements like lust or infatuation may be present, but those are *“distracting elements”* not essential to love’s nature[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20himself%20recognises%20that%20the,infatuation%2C%20lust%2C%20obsession%20and%20possessiveness).)
- **Ineluctably Personal:** Love is unavoidably particular – it fixes on *irreplaceable* individuals. Frankfurt argues that love “is ineluctably personal,” meaning we love someone *for who they uniquely are*, not as an interchangeable instance of a type[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=). From the lover’s perspective, the beloved has distinctive features or an identity that *no one else* can replicate[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20thinks%20that%20when%20we,recommend%20Frankfurt%E2%80%99s%20essay%20%E2%80%98On%20Caring%E2%80%99). (No matter how similar another person might be, you cannot simply swap in a “replacement” for your beloved – love resists such substitutability.)
- **Identification:** The lover **identifies with the beloved**. Frankfurt writes: *“A lover identifies himself with what he loves. In virtue of this identification, protecting the interests of his beloved is necessarily among the lover’s own interests. The interests of his beloved are not actually other than his at all. They are his interests too.”*[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20writes). In other words, the lover internalizes the beloved’s needs and goals – the boundary between self-interest and concern for the other blurs. Loving someone means your identity and volitions expand to include caring for them as *part of yourself*. This explains why a lover will often prioritize the beloved’s good without needing to weigh it against his own: the beloved’s good *is* his own good, due to this identification[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20writes).
- **Volitional Necessity (Non-Voluntary):** Love is **“not under our direct and immediate voluntary control.”**[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=love,reader%20a%20better%20understanding%20of) We cannot simply choose to start or stop loving at will[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20underlines%20that%20love%20is,loving%20our%20beloved%20at%20will). Instead, love “is determined by conditions that are outside our immediate voluntary control”[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,outside%20our%20immediate%20voluntary%20control%E2%80%9D) – it befalls us or grows out of our care, rather than being a calculated decision. Because we cannot help whom (or what) we come to love, we find ourselves **“more or less helplessly driven by the necessities that love entails”**[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63). These “necessities of love” mean that there are certain actions or commitments we *must* undertake once love grips us – the lover, in Frankfurt’s terms, has “no choice” but to pursue the beloved’s welfare, given the love. This *volitional necessity* is self-imposed (by one’s own will, since one’s will is now aligned with the beloved’s good) yet also involuntary (since we did not choose to impose that binding care on ourselves)[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63). Frankfurt emphasizes that although love constrains us, it does not alienate us from our will – the constraints of love *are* our own will, just shaped by an enduring care[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20thinks%20that%20though%20we,our%20free%20will%2C%20I%20recommend). In this way, love defines the **“boundaries of the self”**: what we love sets limits on what we can seriously consider doing[cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/necessity-volition-and-love/autonomy-necessity-and-love/5696160C9BA0DBB70B379E326DA7029E#:~:text=One%20category%20of%20such%20actions,addition%20the%20necessities%20of%20love) (certain betrayals or harms to our beloved become *unthinkable* to us, given our will is invested in them).

**Argument Structure:** Step by step, Frankfurt’s account proceeds roughly as follows:

1. **Caring and Practical Identity:** He begins by observing that what a person *cares about* gives form and structure to her life. Caring provides a framework of ends and priorities within which we exercise practical reason[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=The%20Reasons%20of%20Love%20is,purest%20form%20of%20love%20is). Without caring about anything, we would be adrift. From this starting point, Frankfurt suggests that among our cares, **love is a paramount mode of caring** – indeed “the most important form of caring”[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=love%20is%20a%20particular%20mode,of%20a%20person%20to%20love). Love, then, is central to answering the question of how one should live, because it fixes what is non-negotiably important to the lover.
2. **Defining Love via Its Essential Features:** Frankfurt then analyzes love by its conceptual necessities (the four features above). He contrasts love with superficial forms of attraction or desire, to home in on love’s distinctive character as *disinterested* (not ego-driven) and *particular* (focused on this individual)[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,the%20person%20who%20is%20loved)[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20thinks%20that%20when%20we,recommend%20Frankfurt%E2%80%99s%20essay%20%E2%80%98On%20Caring%E2%80%99). Through examples, he illustrates disinterested concern (e.g. a politician aiding others at no benefit to himself[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20states%20that%20love%20is,to%20push%20for%20this%20policy)) and the non-substitutability of the beloved (the lover cannot simply trade one person for another, because love sees the beloved as unique[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20thinks%20that%20when%20we,recommend%20Frankfurt%E2%80%99s%20essay%20%E2%80%98On%20Caring%E2%80%99)). These features address puzzles like why love persists even if the beloved changes, and why “fungibility” is anathema to love – points that will later be explicitly discussed by Kolodny as well.
3. **Love’s Volitional Necessity:** Next, Frankfurt explains how love binds the will. Because the lover identifies with the beloved, the lover is **internally compelled** to act for the beloved’s sake[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20writes). Frankfurt frames this as a kind of necessity: just as we have necessities of prudence (we *must* eat to live) or morality (we feel we *must* keep our obligations), there are **“necessities of love”** that stem from our own heart[cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/necessity-volition-and-love/autonomy-necessity-and-love/5696160C9BA0DBB70B379E326DA7029E#:~:text=One%20category%20of%20such%20actions,addition%20the%20necessities%20of%20love). He argues that this does not undermine autonomy, since these necessities express our deepest volitions rather than external coercion[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20thinks%20that%20though%20we,our%20free%20will%2C%20I%20recommend). However, it does mean love isn’t subject to immediate rational revision – one cannot talk oneself out of love by argument, nor simply decide to stop loving because it’s imprudent or immoral. We are, in Frankfurt’s words, *helpless* in the grip of love’s demand on us[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63).
4. **Love and Reasons:** Frankfurt acknowledges a potential tension: if love is not based on reasons, how do we understand the *rationality* of actions done out of love? His answer is that love itself provides the *reasons* or motives for the lover. The lover doesn’t need an *external* justification to care for their child; the fact that they **love** the child is enough for the child’s needs to appear supremely important. In Frankfurt’s terms, love bypasses the need for independent principles: **the beloved’s importance is “imported” into the lover’s will by love itself**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be). (Philosopher Niko Kolodny summarizes Frankfurt’s view here: Frankfurt “contends that love is a structure of desires… that is not a response to some antecedent reason”[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be). Love’s desires do not *answer to* reasons; they *create* reasons.) This means Frankfurt deliberately **sidesteps the search for an external justification** of why we love one person and not another – love is a **“brute fact”** of our volitional life, which then generates commitments. By rejecting the requirement of prior reasons, Frankfurt avoids the substitutability problem (he would say we *cannot* simply love a substitute with equal qualities, because our love was never based on a comparison of qualities in the first place).
5. **Solution and Vision:** Ultimately, Frankfurt concludes that **love is what gives meaning and order to an individual’s life**. In his book *The Reasons of Love*, he even suggests that the answer to “how should we live?” is: devote yourself to loving something worthy and important, because love will focus your will and define your identity[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=this%20short%20book%2C%20Frankfurt%20explores,of%20love%20like%20romantic%20love)[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=love%20is%20a%20particular%20mode,of%20a%20person%20to%20love). He famously argues that the “purest” form of love is a kind of self-love – the love that motivates us to love *anything at all* (a desire for purposes to commit to)[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=In%20this%20chapter%2C%20Frankfurt%20explains,of%20a%20person%20to%20love). But in everyday terms, it is our love for particular people, causes, or things that grounds our practical decisions. He thus paints a picture in which love is **foundational in our practical reasoning**: it is not judged by reason, but rather our reasons flow from what we love. Love, for Frankfurt, is irreducible – a basic *explanatory principle* of why we care about what we care about, and thereby a bedrock of ethics and moral psychology.

## J. David Velleman: Love as Moral Emotion and Value Recognition

**Guiding Puzzle:** Velleman’s inquiry is driven by a classic tension in ethics: **the conflict between love’s partiality and morality’s impartiality**[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=Love%20as%20a%20Moral%20Emotion,of%20Use%2C%20available%20at)[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=point%20of%20view%20is%20impartial,differently%2C%20whether%20or%20not%20they). We tend to think that morality demands equal respect or concern for all persons, while love by its nature singles out specific individuals for special favor. How can one reconcile *being a loving partner or parent*, who will prioritize a beloved’s needs, with *being a moral agent*, who acknowledges the equal worth of all people? Philosophers have assumed that **“love and morality are generally assumed to differ in spirit”**, as Velleman notes[journals.uchicago.edu](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdfplus/10.1086/233898#:~:text=Love%20as%20a%20Moral%20Emotion,favors%20no%20particular%20individual%2C%20whereas). The guiding question is whether this apparent conflict is real or merely superficial. **Must we abandon impartial moral principles to accommodate love, or misunderstand love’s nature to make it fit morality?** Velleman aims to resolve this puzzle by rethinking what love fundamentally is.

**Central Thesis:** Velleman’s central claim is that **love is a “moral emotion,” specifically an arresting awareness of the intrinsic value of a person**[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,interested%20designs%20on%20him). In his view, love is not primarily a desire or a pursuit of intimacy, but an attitude of *valuing* the beloved for who they are. It is closely related to Kantian **respect for persons**: both are responses to the *dignity* or worth of a person’s rational nature[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=capacity%20for%20respect,with%20respect%20but%20also%20some%02times). However, love goes beyond cold respect. Velleman describes love as an emotional response that **“arrests” us – it catches and holds our attention on the value of the beloved – and motivates us to openness and care**[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,interested%20designs%20on%20him). Crucially, love **“differs from Kantian respect in that its primary motivational force is to suspend our emotional self-protection from the person rather than our self-interested designs on him.”**[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=THE%20PARTIALITY%20OF%20LOVE%20I,which%20insists%20that%20people%20are) In other words, respecting someone (morally) prevents us from exploiting or manipulating them (it checks our selfish impulses), whereas **loving someone makes us let down our guard and become emotionally vulnerable or attentive to them**. Love invites the person into our heart, as it were, by **removing the barriers of indifference or fear**, allowing us truly to see *and respond to* their inherent value.

Another way to put Velleman’s thesis: **to love someone is to appreciate them as an end in themselves** (never merely as means) in a deeply felt way. This thesis implies that love, at its core, aligns with the moral principle of valuing persons, suggesting that love and moral respect spring from the same recognition of the other’s worth[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=capacity%20for%20respect,with%20respect%20but%20also%20some%02times). Thus, rather than seeing love as an irrational bias, Velleman sees it as rooted in *reason-recognition*: love **“appreciates the value”** of the beloved’s personhood[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=capacity%20for%20respect,with%20respect%20but%20also%20some%02times). This is why he provocatively calls love a **“moral emotion”** – it’s an emotion essentially tied to moral value.

**Competing Views Rejected:** Velleman distances himself from both the view that **morality must be revised to accommodate love’s partiality** and the view that **love is simply a composite of desires or behaviors found in close relationships**.

- First, he opposes those who say *morality* should become more partial to approve of favoritism (some ethicists had argued that maybe our moral theory should give a special place to personal relationships). Velleman thinks this approach gets things backward: *“The way to bring love into convergence with morality is not to stop thinking of morality as impartial but to rethink the partiality of love.”*[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=greater%20partiality%20in%20our%20conception,to%20be%20more%20rather%20than). He insists that we do **not** have to water down impartial moral principles; instead, we should examine whether love truly demands the sort of partial **bias** we assume it does. In short, he rejects the premise that love and impartial respect are inherently at odds – a premise he calls a source of *“righteous absurdity”* if taken too far[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=into%20convergence%20with%20morality%20is,tend%20to%20arise%20in%20loving).
- Second, Velleman criticizes standard analyses of love that focus on the **symptoms of love (the desires and actions it prompts)** rather than its essence. Many philosophers, he says, effectively list things like wanting to be with the beloved, wanting the beloved’s happiness, preferring the beloved over others, etc., and treat that as the definition of love. Velleman claims that is *describing typical lovers’ behavior*, but not pinning down what the *emotion itself* is[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=avoid%20absurdity%2C%20but%20I%20won%E2%80%99t,with%20the%20spirit%20of%20morality). *“Once we distinguish love from the likings and longings that usually go with it,”* he argues, *“we will give up the assumption that the emotion is partial in a sense that puts it in conflict with the spirit of morality.”*[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=since%20I%20think%20that%20moral,with%20the%20spirit%20of%20morality). Here he is rejecting the view (held by Frankfurt and others) that love just *is* a pattern of desires/concerns. Instead, he posits love as a more fundamental attitude – *appreciation of value* – which can lead to various desires but shouldn’t be conflated with them. By stripping away those contingent desires, Velleman aims to show that **love itself need not involve unfair favoritism**.
- Additionally, Velleman implicitly challenges Frankfurt’s no-reason view. If love is a response to the beloved’s inherent value, then love **does** have a basis in reasons (the “reason” being the precious value of a person). He doesn’t frame it as a straightforward argument like “I love you because of feature X” (since that would face the substitutability problem), but he does ground love in the *recognition* of the beloved’s *personhood*. This is in contrast to Frankfurt, who sees love as creating value rather than recognizing it. Velleman’s Kantian orientation thus **rejects the idea that love is utterly arational or value-creating**; for him, love sees what is already there – the immense value in the beloved – much as respect does[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=capacity%20for%20respect,with%20respect%20but%20also%20some%02times).

**Proposed Solution & Argument:** Velleman’s positive account unfolds in a series of logical steps:

1. **Distinguish Love from Its Usual Effects:** Velleman begins by separating the *core emotion of love* from the common *byproducts* of love. For example, a lover often *desires* intimacy, prefers the beloved’s company, feels affection, etc., but these are *expressions* of love in typical contexts, not the very essence of love. He gives thought experiments or cases to illustrate this. One striking example he discusses is the relationship between a dedicated teacher and a student: true love in that context might *not* seek personal closeness or romantic interaction (in fact, that could be inappropriate). A teacher can **care deeply** for a student’s growth and well-being, which Velleman would consider a form of love, yet maintain professional distance[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=teacher%2C%20but%20that%20when%20it,treat%20students%20otherwise%20than%20as). The teacher’s love manifests not by hugging the student or spending off-hours together, but by, say, painstakingly helping the student succeed – or even by *“keeping one’s distance”* if that is in the student’s best interest[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=true%20love%20in%20this%20context%3B,THE%20PARTIALITY%20OF%20LOVE). This shows that love doesn’t always coincide with the stereotypical *longings*. By such examples, Velleman reinforces that **love proper = the attitude of valuing the person,** whereas the desires for interaction or exclusivity are contingent and context-dependent. (Thus a parent’s love may involve cuddling their child, a friend’s love involves hanging out, a teacher’s love involves mentorship – different behaviors, same underlying emotion of profound concern and appreciation.)
2. **Love as Value Perception:** Having isolated the phenomenon, Velleman then defines it: **love is a mode of seeing or appreciating the worth of the beloved**. He invokes Iris Murdoch’s idea that loving attention is a kind of *really looking* at another person[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=This%20hypothesis%20would%20explain%20why,other%20person%20is%20among%20the). When you love someone, you *truly see* them in their full reality and humanity. This clear vision reveals the other as **valuable in himself**. Velleman ties this to Kant’s notion of the value of persons (each person’s rational nature has a dignity beyond price). He hypothesizes that in love **“one’s eyes are opened”** to the reality of the other, such that one is struck by their value as a person[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable). This emotional openness is “arresting”: it stops us from being indifferent or self-centered; we cannot look away from the fact *this person matters*. In Velleman’s memorable formulation, **“love is an arresting awareness of value in a person”**[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable).
    
    He carefully differentiates this from Kantian *respect*. Respect is also rooted in recognizing a person’s value, but respect operates by **constraining the agent’s own will** (e.g. “I must not treat this person as a mere means, because I respect their humanity”). Love, by contrast, operates by **dissolving the boundaries** between persons emotionally: it *“suspends our emotional self-protection”*, meaning we allow the other’s joys and sorrows to affect us as if they were our own[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable). So both respect and love treat the other as an end, but respect is more about refraining from harm/exploitation, whereas love actively embraces the other’s good as part of one’s own good. Despite this difference, the *object* of the attitude is the same: the other’s intrinsic value or true self.
    
3. **Reconceiving Partiality:** With love defined in this way, Velleman addresses the **partiality problem** head-on. If love is appreciating someone’s value as a person, shouldn’t we then love *everyone*, since everyone has this value? How can love involve choosing just a few to love? He acknowledges: *“if love is a way of valuing persons, then in loving some people but not others, we must value some people but not others”*, which seems to conflict with the moral truth that *all people are equally valuable*[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=self,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable). This is the **tension**: love’s selectivity versus the impartial value of persons.
    
    Velleman’s nuanced answer is that **love’s partiality is not a judgment that the beloved is *more* valuable than others, but a reflection of the fact that we are only capable of loving (i.e., fully appreciating) certain people to whom life brings us close**. All persons may be worthy of love in principle, but practical and psychological constraints mean we only come to see *some* people in that intimate light. Love doesn’t deny that others have equal worth; it’s just an attitude we enter into with particular individuals under suitable conditions (familiarity, personal interaction, etc.). Velleman suggests that the appearance of “discriminating love” is partly a matter of perspective: we *feel* special to our beloved and want to be valued “above others”[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=How%20We%20Want%20to%20Be,to%20conflict%20with%20impartial%20morality), but this does not mean the lover thinks other people have no worth – only that the lover’s attention is non-fungibly fixed on *this* person.
    
    He also points out a subtle shift in how the love-vs-morality problem is often presented: when we worry that impartial morality might undermine love, we imagine, as lovers, having to sacrifice our beloved’s interest for a stranger. But when we worry love undermines morality, we imagine, as those *desiring to be loved*, that if our loved ones were too moral, we wouldn’t be treated as special[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=Notice%2C%20then%2C%20that%20when%20philosophers,threatens%20to%20interfere%20with%20our). The first is a lover’s perspective (feeling conflict between duty and love), the second is the beloved’s perspective (fearing not to be special). Velleman uses this analysis to argue that the conflict is not as irreconcilable as it seems: morally, one can acknowledge everyone’s equal worth *and* still have a special relationship where one’s attention is particularly on the beloved. The beloved is “special” to the lover *because* the lover actually sees that person’s full value (something the lover doesn’t manage to do with everyone). This doesn’t falsify the fact that others have equal value; it’s just that love is a unique *encounter* with value that, by its nature, can’t be spread thin over all humanity at once.
    
4. **Love’s Motivation and Morality:** Because love is seeing the other’s value, it naturally motivates beneficent action – *but* Velleman stresses that **the desire to benefit the beloved or to be with them is a secondary consequence, not the defining feature**. For example, if truly loving a student means keeping professional distance for their own good, the loving teacher will do so[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=true%20love%20in%20this%20context%3B,THE%20PARTIALITY%20OF%20LOVE). In general, love will often motivate us to promote the beloved’s happiness (just as respect motivates helping others in need), but love can also require restraint or sacrifice if that actually honors the beloved’s true interests or autonomy. In this sense, Velleman aligns love with moral principles: a lover moved by the *value* of their beloved will not lie, cheat, or steal *for* the beloved in ways that undermine the beloved’s own moral agency or value. Love wouldn’t want the beloved to become morally corrupt or for the lover to do unjust things on the beloved’s behalf, because that ultimately conflicts with seeing the beloved (and others) as persons of worth. Thus, when love and morality *seem* to conflict, Velleman might argue the conflict is with those *“likings and longings”* (like possessiveness or preference) that people often mistake for love itself[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=avoid%20absurdity%2C%20but%20I%20won%E2%80%99t,with%20the%20spirit%20of%20morality). True love, as the appreciation of personhood, is **compatible with impartial respect** – it is basically *respect with affection*. So a loving person, in Velleman’s ideal, will actually behave ethically: they value their beloved as an irreplaceable person, and they also continue to respect the humanity of others.
5. **Conclusion – Love Unified with Moral Vision:** Velleman’s account thus resolves the puzzle by **integrating love into the moral framework**. Love is not an unruly exception to morality, but rather a **fulfillment of moral perception**. It *“rethinks the partiality of love”* and finds that love, properly understood, does not demand privileging the beloved’s value over all others **in principle** – it demands intense attention to *this* person’s value *in practice*. His broader vision is a moral-psychological one: he portrays humans as having the capacity to **value persons in two related modes** – **respect** (impersonal, universal) and **love** (personal, particular)[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=capacity%20for%20respect,with%20respect%20but%20also%20some%02times). Both modes recognize the same deep value; love simply does so in a way that forges a personal bond. By emphasizing this, Velleman bites a bullet of his own: if taken literally, his theory would mean *any* person is equally worthy of anyone’s love, and differences in whom we love are circumstantial. This is a striking implication (love becomes, in principle, *indiscriminate* in who deserves it – a radical contrast to Frankfurt and Kolodny). Velleman accepts that conceptually and then explains why, psychologically, love will always be limited to particular relationships. In the end, he achieves a view on which **the demands of morality (to acknowledge each person’s worth) and the impulse of love (to devote oneself to a beloved) are two facets of valuing persons**, rather than antagonistic forces. Love is thus rehabilitated as **not blind favoritism, but a clear-sighted appreciation of another’s true value**, which naturally flourishes in intimate contexts.

## Niko Kolodny: Love as Valuing a Relationship

**Guiding Puzzle:** Kolodny squarely confronts the question: **Are there reasons for love, and if so, what could justify loving one particular person over another?** The philosophical tension here arises from the **“reasons problem” of love**: we normally feel our love for someone is *appropriate* in a way that mere whims are not, yet it’s puzzling to say what reasons one has to love a specific person (especially since saying “I love you because of your qualities X, Y, Z” seems to cheapen or misidentify love). On one hand, as Kolodny notes, it *seems* we ought to be able to give some reason why we love our beloved (beyond “just because I do”), otherwise love appears arbitrary. On the other hand, any candidate reason focusing on the beloved’s *attributes* runs into problems of fungibility and fickleness. **Kolodny’s motivating puzzle is exactly this impasse**: either **love has reasons**, in which case it risks being conditional and transferable, or **love has no reasons**, in which case it becomes a mysterious brute impulse, insulated from rational critique. He finds neither extreme satisfying and seeks a middle path that accounts for love’s selectivity **without** undermining its authenticity.

**Central Thesis:** Kolodny’s theory, which he calls the **“relationship theory” of love**, holds that **one’s reason for loving a person is one’s *relationship* with that person**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their). In a slogan: *we love people because of the relationships we have with them*. More precisely, the **history of interpersonal interactions and mutual dependence** you share with someone can itself be the justificatory reason that makes loving that person fitting. For example, the reason I (appropriately) love my best friend is not (or not only) that she has such-and-such virtues, but that *she is my friend* – we have years of trust and shared experiences that constitute a friendship. That *relationship* is a fact about us, and it confers value on the person *relative to me* that no outsider has. Thus, love is understood as a kind of **valuation of a relationship**: to love someone is to cherish the relationship (as an ongoing pattern of concern, trust, mutual support, etc.) one has with them, and to see that relationship as a source of reasons to act and feel in certain loving ways[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their). Importantly, valuing the relationship and loving the person are not in competition – valuing the relationship **just is** the way one loves that person for who they are *to you*. Kolodny’s thesis thereby grounds love in **particular, historical, *relational* facts** rather than in the beloved’s general qualities or in pure volition. It’s an answer to *“Why do you love this person?”* – Kolodny’s answer: *“Because she is my wife/sister/friend/etc. – because of the relationship we have.”*[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their).

**Views Rejected:** Kolodny arrives at this thesis by **dismantling two opposing approaches**: (a) **Quality-based theories** (love for reasons of the beloved’s attributes) and (b) **No-reason theories** (like Frankfurt’s, where love is a-baseless commitment).

- *Against quality-based (nonrelational) reasons:* Kolodny systematically examines what he calls the “quality theory” – the idea that we love people for their attractive qualities (beauty, virtue, charm, etc.). He identifies several deep problems with this view, including **Constancy**, **Non-substitutability**, and **Amnesia**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=remaining%20problems%3A%20constancy%2C%20nonsubstitutability%2C%20and,love%20for%20my%20wife%20of)[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=her%20looks%2C%20wit%2C%20and%20self,responsive%20to%20its%20reasons%2C%20there%02140).
    - **Constancy:** If love were justified by certain qualities, then if the beloved lost those qualities, the reason for love would disappear, and one’s love should rationally end[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=remaining%20problems%3A%20constancy%2C%20nonsubstitutability%2C%20and,love%20for%20my%20wife%20of). But genuine love is often *constant* through changes – *“Love is not love which alters when it alteration finds,”* as Shakespeare said[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=as%20she%20loses%20those%20qualities,love%20for%20my%20wife%20of). We consider a love that evaporates as soon as the beloved becomes ill or less attractive to be shallow or even not love at all. Thus, a purely quality-based view makes love too fragile and *“fickle”*[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=remaining%20problems%3A%20constancy%2C%20nonsubstitutability%2C%20and,responsive%20to%20its%20own%20reasons) to match our intuitions.
    - **Non-substitutability (Irreplaceability):** If the reason I love Jane is that she has qualities A, B, C, then anyone else with equally good A, B, C qualities should be just as lovable to me. The quality-based theory implies that if someone “with the same qualities” came along, I should be indifferent between them and Jane – I have *as much reason* to love the similar person as to love Jane[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,that%20cite%20nonrelational%20fea%02tures%20as)[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=her%20looks%2C%20wit%2C%20and%20self,responsive%20to%20its%20reasons%2C%20there%02140). This clashes with the intuition that our love is *particular*: we don’t see our beloved as just an exemplar of certain traits, but as *uniquely* significant. A love that *“would accept just as well any Doppelgänger”* (Kolodny’s phrase) doesn’t ring true as love[www2.bartleby.com](https://www2.bartleby.com/essay/Love-As-Valuing-A-Relationship-By-Niko-85D8DB33EA0D34BB#:~:text=Love%20As%20Valuing%20A%20Relationship,appropriate%20by%20relationships%2C%20or). In other words, loving *this* person seems to involve a commitment that no one else can simply replace, even if they have similar virtues[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=her%20looks%2C%20wit%2C%20and%20self,responsive%20to%20its%20reasons%2C%20there%02140).
    - **Amnesia:** Kolodny asks, what if a lover suffered memory loss and forgot all the shared history with the beloved? Would the beloved’s abstract qualities alone suffice to sustain love? Intuitively, much of what fuels love is the *history* (the memories, the things gone through together). If all of that were wiped away, a lover might no longer feel the same love, even if the person before them still has the same qualities they always admired. This suggests that specific *relational history* is crucial to love, not just the beloved’s traits in a vacuum[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=remaining%20problems%3A%20constancy%2C%20nonsubstitutability%2C%20and,love%20for%20my%20wife%20of)[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,that%20cite%20nonrelational%20fea%02tures%20as).
    
    From these and other considerations, Kolodny concludes that citing **nonrelational features of the person “gives the wrong object”** for love. Love isn’t fundamentally about those properties – or else it wouldn’t survive changes, nor would it resist substitution[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=remaining%20problems%3A%20constancy%2C%20nonsubstitutability%2C%20and,love%20for%20my%20wife%20of)[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=her%20looks%2C%20wit%2C%20and%20self,responsive%20to%20its%20reasons%2C%20there%02140).
    
    He even directly addresses a sophisticated version of the quality view: **J. David Velleman’s Kantian proposal that we love people for their “bare personhood” or rational nature**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=ingeniously%20argued%20proposal%2C%20the%20reason,similar%20person%20as%20a%20replacement). This is a nonrelational feature at the opposite extreme (the universal feature of being a person). Kolodny argues that this too fails, for a similar reason: every person has that feature, so it cannot explain why *this* person is loved and not another[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=ingeniously%20argued%20proposal%2C%20the%20reason,similar%20person%20as%20a%20replacement). If “personhood” were my reason for loving Jane, I should, absurdly, be ready to love anyone with equal personhood just as much – which would make love completely indiscriminate and undermine its meaningful particularity[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=ingeniously%20argued%20proposal%2C%20the%20reason,similar%20person%20as%20a%20replacement). Thus, Kolodny finds *all* such nonrelational reasons (from mundane qualities to Kantian dignity) inadequate as the basis for love’s specific focus.
    
- *Against no-reason (volitional) theories:* On the other side, Kolodny considers the view (exemplified by Frankfurt) that **love is fundamentally arational – it does not respond to reasons at all** but is just a contingent commitment or “volitional necessity.” Frankfurt, as Kolodny summarizes, says love is a **“structure of desires… that is not a response to some antecedent reason.”**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be). Kolodny has two main objections to this *no-reasons* stance:
    1. **Love’s Distinctiveness:** If love had no reasons, how do we distinguish loving attitudes from other motives that produce similar behavior? Kolodny points out that **without reasons, we can’t explain what makes, say, a parent’s urge to care for their child (out of love) different from a stranger’s urge to help the same child out of sympathy, duty, or even a random whim**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be). If a loving parent and a dutiful babysitter both rush to comfort a crying child, the outward action is the same. What makes the parent’s motivation *love*? Intuitively, it’s that the parent *sees the child as “my child” and thus especially to be cared for*. That *seeing-as* suggests a reason (the relationship) that the dutiful babysitter lacks. Kolodny argues that **love must have a “distinctive reason” in light of which the lover’s actions make sense**, otherwise love as a *state* cannot be conceptually distinguished from other motives[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Besides%20having%20other%20problems%2C%20however%2C,these%20reasons%20could%20possibly%20be). He writes, *“If her lover’s desire is to be distinguished from these other desires, it must be distinguished in terms of the distinctive reasons for it… If there are distinctive reasons for the desires constitutive of love, then there are distinctive reasons for love.”*[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Besides%20having%20other%20problems%2C%20however%2C,these%20reasons%20could%20possibly%20be). This cleverly turns Frankfurt’s point on its head: Frankfurt said love’s desires aren’t based on reasons; Kolodny responds that if that were true, we could never point to any rationale that separates a lover’s devotion from any other form of concern. But since we *do* think love is a unique stance, there *must* be some reason-structure to it (even if not a traditional evaluative reason like a trait).
    2. **Normativity of Love:** Additionally, Kolodny is concerned that if love is beyond reasons, we lose the ability to say love can ever be *appropriate or inappropriate*. Yet we commonly feel that some loves are fitting (loving one’s kind parents) and some are strange or misguided (e.g. loving a serial killer, or continuing to love someone who has horribly betrayed you). A no-reason view suggests any object of love is as good as any other – but that seems incorrect; we do judge and reflect on our loving attachments. By reintroducing reasons, Kolodny wants to allow that love can be evaluated: maybe not by outsiders’ standards, but by whether the purported *reason (relationship)* really holds value or not (a deep genuine relationship vs. a fantasy, for example).

Having shown the need for reasons but the failure of generic person-based reasons, Kolodny proposes his **relationship account** as the solution.

**Proposed Solution – Relationship Theory:**

1. **Reason = Relationship:** Kolodny’s key move is to say *the reason you love someone is precisely the relationship you have with them.* For instance, *“the fact that Jane is one’s daughter is a reason for loving her”*, and importantly *“not a reason for loving a substitute with identical nonrelational features.”*[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their). This satisfies the **non-substitutability** requirement: only **Jane is one’s daughter**, not any look-alike or similar person. It also explains **constancy**: even if Jane’s qualities change, as long as the parent-child relationship remains (and retains value), the love continues to have reason. And it fits **everyday justification**: if someone asks *“Why do you love her?”* it is perfectly apt to respond, *“She’s my mother”* or *“We’ve been friends since childhood.”* Such answers, Kolodny argues, are not placeholders or excuses; they genuinely point to why this love makes sense[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their). The relationship is an ongoing source of value for the people in it – a value that outsiders do not have equal access to.
2. **Avoiding the Prior Pitfalls:** The relationship theory elegantly avoids the earlier problems:
    - **No fungibility:** Because relationships are inherently particular, the lover cannot simply swap one person for another. *“The fact that Jane is one’s daughter, sister, friend, etc., is a reason for loving her, but not a reason for loving a substitute with identical nonrelational features.”*[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their). This directly addresses substitutability: a stranger could have all of Jane’s nice qualities, but *that stranger is not my* friend/spouse/etc.*. So the lover has no reason to transfer love to the stranger. Love’s focus on *one’s own* relationship insulates it from the “anyone with those traits” critique. Each love has an essentially agent-relative foundation.
    - **Constancy through change:** Because what one values is the relationship, minor changes in personal qualities don’t undermine love. A devoted friend will love you despite your aging or changing interests because the friendship – the shared history and bond – remains, and that is what they deeply value. (Of course, if the changes are so severe that the relationship itself deteriorates – say one party ceases all contact or betrays the other – then it may no longer be rational to love, but that is because the relationship reason has been undermined, not because of mere change in qualities. Kolodny allows that extreme events like **cruelty or betrayal** *within* the relationship can indeed justify love’s cessation[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=The%20sonnet%2C%20no%20doubt%2C%20overstates,properly%20lapse%20if%20she%20becomes), since they amount to the loss or corruption of the relationship.)
    - **No love on command & Amnesia:** Kolodny’s view naturally explains why we cannot just decide to love a stranger out of the blue: we have no existing relationship with them that could constitute a reason. Love typically grows as a relationship develops; at the start, we may be attracted by qualities (which gives *reasons to initiate* a relationship, perhaps), but the full-fledged love that endures is sustained by the relationship that has been built[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=that%20often%20give%20rise%20to,Insofar%20as%20my)[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=may%20serve%20as%20reasons%20for,a%20fickle%20attitude%2C%20however%2C%20hardly). Conversely, in an amnesia scenario – if one forgets the entire relationship – one indeed might cease to love, which is what we’d expect if the relationship was the reason. (Kolodny likely would say that’s unfortunate, but it fits the idea that the *knowledge* of the relationship history is what grounds the emotions of love. Without memory, the relationship to the lover is, subjectively, gone.)
3. **Addressing Objections:** Kolodny anticipates two major objections to the relationship theory:
    - **“Wrong Object” Worry:** Critics might say, “We don’t love relationships, we love people! Isn’t Kolodny saying you’re really loving the relationship itself rather than the person?” Kolodny clarifies that loving someone *because* of a relationship does **not** mean you literally direct love toward an abstract entity (the friendship or marriage) instead of toward the person. The person **is still the object of love**, but the *reason* you find it appropriate to love that person (rather than any other) is that this person stands in a valued relationship with you[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=For%20two%20reasons%2C%20however%2C%20this,again%20appears%20that%20they%20have). In simpler terms, the relationship is the **context** that makes the person uniquely dear to you. So you are loving the person **as your friend**, **as your sister**, etc. This is analogous to how one might value a *role*: one might have special affection for “my teacher” that one doesn’t have for other equally good teachers – it’s not that one loves the abstract role of *teacher*, but one loves the person under the description *“the one who is my teacher.”* Kolodny argues this preserves the person as the focus while explaining why that person is singled out by love[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=nonstarter,of%20one%27s%20friend%20or%20lover).
    - **Circularity (“Love as its own reason”) Worry:** Another challenge is that for certain types of love, notably romantic love or friendship, *the relationship itself is partly constituted by love*. Saying “the relationship is the reason for love” can sound circular if the relationship *includes* the love. For example, to say “I love my spouse because she’s my spouse” might seem uninformative since being spouses implies love (at least ideally). Kolodny is aware that in cases like friendship and romance, we “don’t love relationships; we love people,” yet those relationships are in place largely *because* of mutual love[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=some%20connection%20that%20she%20has,of%20one%27s%20friend%20or%20lover). He addresses this by examining the timeline and aspects of relationships. In early stages, qualities or attraction may spark love (he doesn’t deny that *initial* love can be motivated by nonrelational factors); however, once the relationship (friendship, marriage) is established, it takes on a life and value of its own, which then sustains and justifies the love going forward[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=by%20love,Sections%206%20and%207). In other words, there is an evolving dynamic: initial love helps create the relationship, and then the relationship provides new reasons to deepen and continue the love. There is no vicious circle because at any given point, one’s reason for continuing to love is the existing bond, not the *future* love. Also, Kolodny’s theory fits better for cases like familial love (parent/child, siblings) where the relationship often pre-exists any choice – you literally love your sister *because she’s your sister*, and that’s that. In such cases, it’s clear the relationship isn’t constituted by prior love (you didn’t choose your sister or gradually form that bond by courting her; it was given, and love naturally grows from it). Those are paradigm cases that give the theory intuitive force.
4. **Comparing to Alternatives:** In the latter part of his essay (section 8), Kolodny compares his relationship theory with Velleman’s and others[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=objections%3A%20that%20it%20gives%20love,implications%20of%20the%20relationship%20theory). He argues that Velleman’s Kantian view (love as responding to personhood) cannot explain *partiality* without implicitly invoking relationships. If love is just about seeing someone’s humanity, why love one spouse more than a stranger? Kolodny would say Velleman ends up smuggling in the idea that *this* person’s humanity is specially salient to me because of our personal connection – which is essentially Kolodny’s point. Similarly, Frankfurt’s view that love needs no reasons, Kolodny believes, misses the fact that *relationships themselves are reason-giving* structures in our practical life. By articulating this, Kolodny aims to capture the **“distinctive reasons for love that views such as Frankfurt’s ignore at their peril.”**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=reasons%20for%20love,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their).
5. **Implications:** Kolodny’s solution offers a broader vision of love as **embedded in a network of personal relationships that themselves carry normative weight**. The overarching implication is that **love is neither capricious nor universally owed to everyone**; it is rationally anchored in the meaningful relationships that people actually have. This has a appealing upshot: it justifies **partiality** in a principled way. It’s *right* and reasonable for me to show special concern for *my* family and friends precisely because they *stand in special relations to me* that I have strong reason to honor and preserve. Love, then, is not a bias in favor of the beloved’s independent merits; it’s a fitting response to the relational value that arises from sharing a life with someone. In Kolodny’s broader moral psychology, reasons can be **agent-relative** – some facts (like “she is my friend”) provide reasons to one agent that do not equally apply to others[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their). This undermines a strict impartialist view of reasons and supports a more relational understanding of ethical life.
    
    There are “bullets” Kolodny knowingly bites: for instance, his theory suggests that **without a prior or ongoing relationship, love cannot be justified**. This means phenomena like “love at first sight” or idolizing a distant celebrity would not count as fully justified love (they might be *protolove* or infatuation awaiting a real relationship to latch onto). Additionally, if a relationship is utterly destroyed (say by betrayal), Kolodny’s view implies the reason to love may disappear, even if one’s heart still feels love – thus one *ought* (normatively) to stop loving in such cases. Some may find this too rationalistic about an emotion like love. But Kolodny accepts that love has a normative dimension: there are better or worse reasons to continue loving, and a relationship that has become hollow or toxic might no longer merit love. These are the costs of treating love in the space of reasons. In return, we get a compelling explanation of love’s **particularity** and its harmony with our ethical intuitions about partial relationships.
    

## Comparative Analysis of the Theories

Having reconstructed each philosopher’s account, we can now compare their approaches, highlighting where they diverge, conflict, or even coincide. Each theory offers a distinct answer to **what love is** and **why (or whether) it is justified**, leading to important differences regarding **partiality, moral conflict, the source of love’s value, and the “disinterested” nature of love**.

### Divergent Foundations: Reasons vs. Volition

A fundamental split is on whether love is grounded in *reasons* at all. **Frankfurt** stands on one side: he asserts that love is not born from rational assessment; it’s a **volitional commitment without prior justification**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be). For Frankfurt, asking “why do you love this person?” has no answer in terms of independent criteria – love just fixes your priorities because it does. In contrast, **Velleman** and **Kolodny** both maintain that love *can* be explained or justified by reference to something: Velleman by the inherent **value of the person**, Kolodny by the **relationship** one has. Thus:

- **Frankfurt:** Love has **no external justification**; it is a basic stance of caring that creates its own reasons. He would likely say that trying to ground love in properties or relations is a mistake – love is **“ineluctably personal”** and arises from the lover’s will, not from calculating reasons[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=)[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be). The cost of this view is that it renders love somewhat **immune to rational critique**. If someone loves an unworthy object, Frankfurt can only describe how that love will shape their will, not judge it as based on bad reasons (since there are none). Frankfurt thus accepts love’s *potential arbitrariness*: love can be “blind” or directed at things reason would not endorse, yet it still defines what matters to the lover.
- **Velleman:** Love is **very much about reasons**, but of a peculiar kind – *moral* or *value-based reasons*. Velleman’s love arises in response to seeing the **“value in a person”**, specifically their rational nature or humanity[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=capacity%20for%20respect,with%20respect%20but%20also%20some%02times). In his view, love is **rationally intelligible** as an appropriate response to something objectively valuable (persons). This gives love a kind of moral high ground; however, it faces the challenge: if the value is objective and universal, what explains love’s selectivity? Velleman’s answer is that while everyone is equally worthy of love, we are only in a position to properly appreciate (and therefore love) a limited number of people (those we know personally, typically). This view implies that **in principle one has reason to love anyone** (since every person has the required value), which *conceptually* universalizes love even though one’s actual love life is particular[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=self,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable). The **incompatibility with Frankfurt** here is stark: Frankfurt would say we do **not** love strangers just because they’re persons – love needs a personal involvement and cannot be commanded by abstract value. Kolodny explicitly sides with Frankfurt on this point, arguing that Velleman’s universal reason doesn’t capture real love’s focus[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=ingeniously%20argued%20proposal%2C%20the%20reason,similar%20person%20as%20a%20replacement).
- **Kolodny:** Love is also about reasons, but **agent-relative reasons**. Unlike Velleman’s *impersonal value* reason, Kolodny’s reason is **essentially personal: “She is *my* ___ (friend, sister, etc.)”**[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their). This makes love justified *relative to the lover’s standpoint*, not from an objective view from nowhere. Kolodny’s stance allows us to **rationally prefer our nearest and dearest** without claiming they are objectively the best people – it’s the relationship that matters. Compared to Velleman, Kolodny better preserves the intuition that love doesn’t generalize: I have *zero* reason to love your friend in the way I love mine, if I lack that relationship. This agent-relativity is incompatible with Velleman’s Kantian symmetry (where in principle your friend is as worthy as mine). Frankfurt would find Kolodny’s view closer in spirit (since it emphasizes the personal point of view), but Frankfurt would object to calling these “reasons” at all. From Frankfurt’s lens, Kolodny is smuggling in love’s own product (the relationship, which is built by love and care) as if it were an independent reason – potentially a circularity. ### Partiality and Moral Conflict

One of the most prominent points of divergence is how each theory handles **love’s partiality** – the fact that love favors one individual – in relation to moral ideals of impartiality:

- **Frankfurt** accepts *partiality* as an inherent feature of love and largely **independent of moral duty**. He categorizes love-based demands as a distinct realm of “necessities” that can conflict with moral obligation[cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/necessity-volition-and-love/autonomy-necessity-and-love/5696160C9BA0DBB70B379E326DA7029E#:~:text=One%20category%20of%20such%20actions,addition%20the%20necessities%20of%20love)】. If a loved one’s needs clash with a stranger’s needs, Frankfurt’s theory doesn’t provide an internal resolution – love might simply *motivate* the lover to favor the beloved, moral rules notwithstanding. He does not see this as a philosophical failure; it is a reflection that love and morality are **different sources of reasons** (indeed, in his view love isn’t accountable to moral reasoning at all). So, **moral conflict** can occur (e.g. lying to protect a loved one) and Frankfurt would likely say that such “bullets” sometimes just have to be bitten: love can lead us to do things that *from an impartial standpoint* are wrong, yet from the perspective of the loving self are unavoidable. Frankfurt’s interest is not in morally evaluating this, but in **describing how love operates on the will**. The cost of this position is that it offers little guidance for when love demands something immoral – Frankfurtian lovers might simply act out of love and leave moral theory to catch up. In practice, Frankfurt acknowledges that we often *feel* it’s not a genuine option to betray our beloved, even if abstract morality says we should treat everyone equall[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63)】. He takes that phenomenology seriously, giving love a kind of autonomy from moral calculation.
- **Velleman** strives to **eliminate the conflict** between love’s partiality and morality’s impartiality by *redefining love* in line with moral value. On his account, properly understood, love **is not a biased favoring of the beloved’s trivial interests over others**; it is an acute awareness of the beloved’s true worth. Because *everyone* has this worth, love in principle **affirms an impartial moral truth** (each person is an end-in-themselves). The appearance of partiality is explained by our limited capacity to engage emotionally with everyone equally, not by a denial of others’ valu[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=self,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable)】. In effect, Velleman says the **“spirit” of love is impartial**, even if the practice of love is particula[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=greater%20partiality%20in%20our%20conception,to%20be%20more%20rather%20than)[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=self,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable)】. He notes that moral philosophers often talk about the conflict as if the lover must choose between love and duty, but he points out this is often a matter of perspective shifting: as *lovers* we fear morality telling us to abandon our beloved; as *those who desire to be loved* we fear a morality that says we’re not specia[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=Notice%2C%20then%2C%20that%20when%20philosophers,in%20our%20capacity%20as%20aspiring)】. His solution is that a lover who truly sees the beloved’s humanity will not cease to acknowledge the humanity of others. For instance, a Vellemanian lover would not cheat *for* his beloved in a way that harms others, because that would be failing to regard those others (and ultimately the beloved too, as a moral being) with respect. In theory, then, **love aligned with personhood-value won’t ask you to do something that treats outsiders as “less human.”** Love may demand special attention to the beloved (you spend your evening comforting your friend rather than a stranger), but this attention doesn’t violate any moral duty as long as no one’s rights are trampled. Velleman bites a subtle bullet here: if morality did demand a significant sacrifice of the beloved’s interest (say, choosing to save five strangers over one’s spouse), his view might imply that the truly moral person finds a way to accept that without “betraying” love – a highly contentious claim. He hints that such dilemmas are often set up by a misconstrual of love; yet the worry remains that **extreme cases** pit impartial good against love. Velleman’s ideal perhaps is that a fully rational agent would love everyone insofar as they engage with them as persons, and thus never face a stark love-versus-duty choice – a utopian resolution that some critics find psychologically unrealistic.
- **Kolodny** unabashedly **embraces partiality as justified**: loving particular people is rational *because* of particular relationships. In his framework, **partiality is not a moral vice but a natural outgrowth of the reasons relationships provide**. If I devote more resources to my child’s education than to a stranger’s, that is not an embarrassing anomaly needing moral justification – it’s directly justified by the parent–child relationship. Kolodny thus provides a *normative permission* (even obligation) to favor loved ones that Frankfurt simply assumes and Velleman tries to transcend. However, Kolodny does have to consider moral limits. His theory implies that while **agent-centered reasons** (from relationships) are genuine, they don’t automatically trump all other reasons. For example, I have reason to save my friend from harm due to our friendship, but I also have moral reason to save five other people from greater harm. Kolodny doesn’t fully spell out how to weigh these, but one could combine his view with a pluralist morality: there are relationship-based reasons *and* impartial humanitarian reasons, and part of practical wisdom is balancing them. At least, Kolodny legitimizes that **it wouldn’t be irrational** to save one’s friend even in some cases where an impersonal calculus disagrees – because that friendship is a source of value for the agen[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their)】. Unlike Velleman, Kolodny is comfortable with the idea that *from the agent’s standpoint* their loved one really does take priority due to the relationship. This resonates with common moral intuitions (many people feel it’s not only natural but sometimes right to favor loved ones). The **tension**, however, is that Kolodny’s reasons are agent-relative: a third party can’t endorse your partial act by the same reason (your relationship means nothing to a stranger). Morality traditionally seeks reasons that are shareable or at least defensible to all. Kolodny’s vision thus pushes toward a more relativized morality where each person has special duties to their own. The **cost** is that it potentially fractures the unitary idea of moral truth (what’s right for me given my relationships might not be what’s right for you). Still, many find this an acceptable cost to preserve the legitimacy of love’s partial commitments.

In summary, **Frankfurt** lets love and morality stand apart (potential conflict accepted), **Velleman** revises love to erase the conflict (making love itself quasi-impartial in motivation), and **Kolodny** revises our understanding of reasons to legitimize partiality (thus accommodating love within a broader rational framework, conflict mitigated by contextual reasons). Each must “bite a bullet”: Frankfurt allows that a loving person might morally err yet still be following the highest law of their heart; Velleman risks portraying an ideal love so high-minded it may seem too detached from the passionate favoritism of real human love; Kolodny concedes that what’s rational for one agent (loving their own) can’t be demanded of another, complicating interpersonal moral assessment.

### The Source of Love’s Value: Created vs. Perceived vs. Relational

Another key divergence lies in **what each theory sees as the source of the beloved’s special value or importance** in love, which we can call the issue of **value asymmetry** (why the beloved matters so much more to the lover than others do):

- For **Frankfurt**, the beloved’s supreme value to the lover is in a sense **created by the act of loving**. We *invest* the beloved with importance by caring about them. Frankfurt explicitly denies that we love someone *because* they are antecedently valuable in some objective or general way. Rather, **“the fact that we love them is what makes them especially valuable to us.”** Love is a *value-conferring* stance: by loving X, X becomes a central, non-fungible value in my life. This view highlights how love can make even an objectively ordinary thing (a tattered old blanket, a pet rock, etc.) immeasurably precious to someone. It also underscores the deeply personal nature of value on Frankfurt’s account – values are indexed to our will. The **asymmetry** here is entirely from the lover’s side: the beloved might not be valuable to others at all, but to the lover, because of love, they are of irreplaceable value. This helps explain phenomena like loving bad or flawed people; love isn’t sensitive to objective merits. However, a consequence is that Frankfurt can’t easily explain why it might be *good* to love what is truly good (except that it will structure your life). He leaves the notion of **“worthy” loves** mostly to the side, whereas Velleman and Kolodny try to capture why some loves seem more fitting than others.
- For **Velleman**, the beloved’s value is **independent and pre-existent** – it lies in the beloved’s personhood (their capacity for rational agency, autonomy, etc.). Love is a **value-discerning** or **value-responsive** emotion: it latches onto the *real moral value* of the person. The lover sees the beloved as *precious, beyond price*, which for Velleman is no projection – it’s the truth about persons (in Kantian terms, each person has dignity[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=capacity%20for%20respect,with%20respect%20but%20also%20some%02times)】. Thus, the *special* value of the beloved in love isn’t actually a comparative value (“more valuable than others”), but an absolute value (“of unquantifiable value”) recognized in this individual. The **asymmetry** arises because while everyone has this value, only toward some do we open our eyes and heart enough to fully appreciate it. So Velleman might say: *In loving my friend, I finally see her value properly.* I don’t thereby deny others’ value, but I’m not in a position to see all others with the same clarity and emotional immediacy. In a way, love for Velleman is **perception of the universal in the particular** – I experience the wonder of personhood through **this** person in front of me. This gives love a noble grounding: it’s tied to what is objectively worth valuing (persons). It also means love has an inherent *moral dimension*: to love someone is, at root, to value them in the way morality says everyone should be valued (as ends in themselves). The **tension** or cost here is that Velleman’s account struggles with the *particularity* of love’s value. If all persons are equally valuable, what *explains* the focus on one? Velleman would say it’s a matter of *which relationships and interactions allow that value to shine forth to us*, not that the one we love is *more valuable* than others. Some critics think this flattens the experience of love – after all, lovers typically feel *“My beloved is more important to me than anyone else.”* Velleman’s view translates that to *“My beloved reveals the important value of persons to me in a unique way.”* It’s a refined thought, perhaps too refined for the throes of passion, but it’s philosophically elegant.
- For **Kolodny**, the beloved’s value is **relative to the relationship**. It’s not that the beloved has superior personal qualities or a higher moral status; it’s that *the relationship itself* carries value for the people in it. The beloved matters to the lover in a way no one else does **because no one else stands in that same relation**. So the asymmetry is straightforward: *“She is my beloved (my friend, my sister, etc.), and that makes all the difference.”* The rest of the world might see your friend as just one person among many, but to you, the friendship endows her with a special significance. This is an **agent-relative value** – true from the lover’s perspective, not necessarily from an outsider’s. Kolodny’s approach thus legitimizes a kind of **plurality of values**: each relationship is a source of value for those involved, generating potentially distinct “circles” of value in the world. There is no single ranking of who is most valuable; value is distributed in a network of relationships. One upshot is that Kolodny can explain why even an **unexceptional person can be deeply loved**: it’s not about being the best or most virtuous, it’s about what you’ve been through together. This view resonates with how people actually talk about love (“He’s my dad; of course I love him,” even if Dad is an average Joe). It also handles **value asymmetry in a contented way**: your beloved is *most important to you*, and that doesn’t need to be justified by her being objectively better – it’s justified by the *relationship*. The **philosophical cost** is that this value is in a sense **opaque to outsiders**. Someone not sharing the relationship might think, “Why all the fuss about this person?” and there’s no non-relational answer – which might be fine (we don’t owe the same care to everyone), but it means Kolodny’s reasons are inherently local. Additionally, if a relationship turns sour, the value might dissipate, which can sound harsh: it implies that if the relational reason is gone, the person might *no longer merit love*. Some might find that too quick – don’t we sometimes continue to love even when the relationship is broken? Kolodny would likely say that lingering love is running on the inertia of past reasons, or is misaligned with current reasons (a tragic but understandable situation). In effect, Kolodny’s beloved has **value to the lover** because of the *shared history and bond*, and that is sufficient in his normative universe.

### “Disinterested” Concern and Selfhood in Love

All three theories agree that genuine love involves **caring for the beloved for the beloved’s own sake** – in that sense, they all see love as *disinterested* (not driven by the lover’s selfish needs). However, they unpack this idea with different emphases:

- Frankfurt explicitly defines love as a **“disinterested concern” for the beloved’s well-being*[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=love,End%20Page%201)】. He means that in loving someone, you do not care for them in order to gain something or because it benefits you; you care *about them, period*. In Frankfurt’s framework, this disinterest is so central that if you only value someone instrumentally (say, you “love” a wealthy relative hoping for inheritance), it’s not love at all. Interestingly, Frankfurt also notes that in romantic or passionate love, sexual desire or possessiveness may accompany love but are not the essence – they are “distracting elements” that love proper (as disinterested concern) could do withou[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20himself%20recognises%20that%20the,infatuation%2C%20lust%2C%20obsession%20and%20possessiveness)】. So Frankfurt’s pure love is quite altruistic in spirit. At the same time, because of **identification**, the lover sees the beloved’s good as his own goo[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20writes)】. This creates a nuanced picture: love is not self-interested, yet it radically redraws the self to include the other. The lover gets satisfaction from the beloved’s happiness *because* he is invested in the beloved, not as a separate payoff but as a direct extension of his own flourishing. Thus, while love isn’t about self-benefit, it becomes a part of the lover’s identity and life meaning. Frankfurt thereby harmonizes disinterested love with a kind of *self-fulfillment*: in loving disinterestedly, you actually define a larger, more meaningful self (one connected to what you love).
- Velleman also conceives of love as fundamentally **non-instrumental**. In his terms, love appreciates the beloved as an end in herself, never as a mere means – which is the Kantian hallmark of respect and moral recognition. He even suggests that in love we *refrain from selfish emotional defenses*, exposing ourselves to the beloved’s influenc[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,which%20insists%20that%20people%20are)】. This implies a deep willingness to respond to the beloved’s needs without egoistic filtering. Velleman’s loving agent is not trying to *get* anything out of the beloved; indeed, love often inspires acts of care or sacrifice that cost the lover. Because love is seeing the other’s value, it naturally entails *valuing the other’s interests*. However, one interesting aspect of Velleman’s view is that love isn’t exactly about *promoting* the beloved’s interests in a calculative way either – it’s more about being emotionally present to who the beloved is. He gives the example that sometimes “true love can manifest itself in an inclination to keep one’s distance” if that is what respect for the person (in their role as student, say) demand[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=teacher%2C%20but%20that%20when%20it,treat%20students%20otherwise%20than%20as)】. This indicates that, for Velleman, **love’s concern is for the person as an autonomous being**, not just for the person’s feelings or wants at every moment. So his love is disinterested in the sense of **devotion to the person’s humanity**. The lover’s own gratification (being loved back, say) is not the driving factor – in fact, Velleman emphasizes one cannot demand love in return as a trade. A potential tension is that Velleman’s portrayal can seem a bit *austere*: love comes off almost as a moral stance (like a saintly recognition of value) rather than the messy, possessive, needy thing it sometimes is. He is intentionally filtering out those “needy” aspects as not essential to love. This means disinterestedness is taken very far – arguably to the point that *ideal love might not even seek personal intimacy if it weren’t appropriate*. Many would agree true love isn’t selfish, but they might still expect love to involve *wanting* some closeness or reciprocation. Velleman might reply that wanting closeness is fine, but it should stem from appreciating the person, not from using them to satisfy loneliness; and if closeness would harm them, true love restrains itself. In short, Velleman’s love is *for the beloved’s sake entirely*, to the point of self-restraint and self-sacrifice, aligning perfectly with moral altruism.
- Kolodny’s view implicitly requires disinterest as well: if the reason to love is the relationship, it follows that you value the relationship (and thus the person in that relationship) intrinsically, not for ulterior motives. When a parent loves a child because “she’s my daughter,” it’s not because the daughter is useful or because loving her yields a reward – it’s because the parent-child bond is inherently valued. Kolodny doesn’t explicitly wax on about altruism, but the idea of **valuing a relationship** includes caring about the participants in that relationship for what they mean to each other. One might ask: is valuing a relationship subtly more self-involved than valuing a person? After all, a relationship is something that includes *oneself*. Could Kolodny’s account be interpreted as: “I love you because of *our* relationship (which is partially about me)” and thus be less purely other-directed? Kolodny would likely respond that the value of a relationship is not an egoistic value – it’s not like valuing “my comfort” or “my money.” A relationship is a **shared** good, a bond *between* two people, and valuing it means you value that shared life and *thereby* value the other person who is essentially part of it. It’s true that the reason is relative to the lover, but it’s not a selfish reason; it’s a **personal reason**. In practice, a Kolodny-style lover acts very much like a disinterested lover: you care about your friend’s happiness because she’s your friend, not because you get something (except the warmth of friendship itself). So, all things considered, Kolodny’s theory supports the idea that love motivates concern for the other for their own sake. It just locates the *source* of that concern in the history and connection you share. If anything, Kolodny allows that love often aligns with *mutual* interest – lovers typically benefit from loving relationships (happiness, support, etc.), but those benefits are not the reason you love. They are by-products of a valued relationship, not the goal. Thus, he remains in line with the classic notion of love’s **“partial altruism”**: love aims at the beloved’s good, and in a well-functioning relationship, each aims at the other’s good (and that very dynamic can be good for each).

### Theoretical Costs and “Bullets to Bite”

Each theory, as we’ve seen, buys a coherent account of love at the price of certain counter-intuitive implications – the “bullets” each must bite:

- **Frankfurt’s Costs:** By denying that love rests on reasons or objective values, Frankfurt must accept that **love can be directed at practically anything**, and we have no philosophical basis (within his view) to say a given love is mistaken *qua* love. This means Frankfurtian love can be **amoral or even immoral**: a terrorist’s love for his cause, a criminal’s love for her partner in crime – these are love in Frankfurt’s sense if they have the structure of disinterested concern and volitional necessity. Frankfurt can of course personally lament someone loving an unworthy object (he does talk about “investing ourselves wisely[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20thinks%20that%20though%20we,our%20free%20will%2C%20I%20recommend)】), but in his theory, “wise” simply means conducive to the lover’s own coherent volitional life, not aligned with external standards. Another cost is that **Frankfurt’s love is not directly criticizable by reason**: if a person asks “should I love this person or not?”, Frankfurt would say that’s not a question reason can decide. You either find yourself loving, or not. This leaves a bit of a **normative vacuum**: can we ever *argue* someone into or out of love? Frankfurt would say no – at best, you can try to influence what they come to care about, but there’s no reasoning about love’s object. Some find this too pessimistic or too permissive, because it means, for instance, you can’t really *fault* someone for not loving their parent or for loving a scoundrel (except in terms of practical fallout). Frankfurt also bites the bullet that **love can conflict with morality** and sometimes *will* override it in the lover’s motivational hierarchy. This raises worries about ethical egoism or favoritism that he doesn’t resolve, except by saying love is outside morality’s jurisdiction. Yet, despite these costs, Frankfurt’s theory gains the benefit of describing love as we often *feel* it – not a choice, not a calculation, but a powerful necessity that defines our personal world of importanc[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63)】. He’s willing to live with the messiness (love isn’t always praiseworthy or rational), because for him the alternative is worse: a lifeless world where only impersonal reasons reign and nothing is **“dear”** to us. The **bullet Frankfurt happily bites** is that *from the outside*, love may appear arbitrary or unjustified – but from the inside, love is self-justifying and indeed essential for a meaningful lif[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=this%20short%20book%2C%20Frankfurt%20explores,of%20love%20like%20romantic%20love)】.
- **Velleman’s Costs:** Velleman’s Kantian approach, while elegant, **risks losing grip on the particular passion of love**. Critics charge that if love is basically the same appreciation we should have for all persons, then it fails to explain the heat, the exclusivity, the *specialness* of loving *one* person in a way that deeply affects us. Velleman counters this by pointing to the emotional **“arrest”** and openness that occurs only in lov[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,which%20insists%20that%20people%20are)】. Still, one might say he downplays the role of attraction, personal history, and even *fondness*. His conception of love is arguably **very intellectualized and moralized** – one might wonder, does it account for romantic love’s erotic dimension or a parent’s fierce possessiveness? Velleman would likely respond that those elements (sexual desire, possessiveness) are *concomitants* of love but not love itself (similar to Frankfurt’s strategy of separating love from “distracting elements”). Another bullet is that **Velleman’s theory implies we could (in principle) love anyone**, and perhaps even that we *should* love everyone, if only we knew them properly. This arguably dilutes the idea of love as *selective*. While he has an explanation for selectivity (you can’t emotionally engage with everyone), some find that explanation a bit rationalizing – after all, we don’t usually think “I can’t love everyone because of limited time”; we think “I love these people because they are *mine* in some sense.” Velleman’s view might struggle with loves that don’t seem morally wholesome: can someone *love* a wicked person? He might say yes, you can love the person’s true self or potential (their humanity) even if their actions are evil – akin to “hate the sin, love the sinner.” But if love is tied to seeing the person’s value, extreme badness might cloud that perception or make the person’s moral self unattractive. This raises a puzzle: is it *appropriate* to love an evil person? Velleman could answer that it’s always appropriate to love the fundamental humanity in someone, even if we condemn their deeds – a very Christian sounding “love the sinner” ethos. That’s a demanding bullet to bite, and many would find it hard to accept. Finally, Velleman’s theory almost implies a certain **inevitable respect in love** – that true love will never violate the beloved’s autonomy or rights. Some may find this idealized: real love, they might say, can include jealousy or controlling impulses (which are bad, but often present). Velleman sets the bar that if you’re truly appreciating the person’s value, you *won’t* do those things – effectively ruling that such flawed love isn’t love at all. While this keeps love morally pristine, it might be too ideal to characterize all the loves we actually talk about. In sum, Velleman must accept that his account is somewhat **normative or ideal** – describing love as it *ought* to be when fully in line with moral value, rather than love as it often is. The payoff for biting these bullets is a theory where love is **fully ennobled**: it carries no whiff of irrationality or arbitrariness; it’s a central moral emotion that complements reason and enriches our moral lives by giving us a personal window into the value of persons.
- **Kolodny’s Costs:** Kolodny’s relationship theory, while intuitively appealing, faces the challenge of **circularity and genesis**. One bullet is the scenario of how love begins: early in a relationship, you don’t yet have the rich history that Kolodny says is the reason for love. So is *falling in love* (before the relationship is firmly established) done without reasons? Kolodny might say the initial spark can be explained by attraction to qualities (so he doesn’t deny qualities play a role in *starting* relationships), but that the justification for *persisting* and deepening love transitions to the relationship itself. This two-stage story is plausible but a bit complex – it means before the relationship, maybe love isn’t fully justified (except as an attempt to *create* a relationship). He effectively might bite the bullet that **love at first sight isn’t rationally justified** – it’s a passion in search of reasons that only forming a relationship can supply. Some romantics would balk at that, insisting love at first sight *can* be authentic love. Kolodny would likely categorize that as infatuation or an inchoate love that needs history to back it up. Another bullet is **loving the unworthy friend or relative**: what if your brother is a jerk? Do you still have reason to love him just because he’s your brother? Kolodny’s theory suggests yes, you *have* a reason (family relationship), but perhaps an attenuated one if the relationship is poor. You might have conflicting reasons: the familial tie gives *some* reason to love or at least try to love, while the brother’s awful behavior gives reason to emotionally distance. If the negativity is extreme (abuse, cruelty), Kolodny would likely say the relationship quality is destroyed enough that love is no longer required – you have *pro tanto* reason from the blood tie, but stronger reason not to love from the betrayal. Sorting this out can be tricky, but it’s in principle doable. What’s notable is Kolodny’s willingness to say **there are normative facts about when love is appropriate**. This means he can face cases where someone feels love that perhaps they *ought not* – e.g., someone remains in love with an abusive partner. Kolodny can empathetically say “I understand you *feel* love, but given that the mutual relationship is gone or harmful, you **lack good reason** to love them; ideally, you would cease to, however hard that is.” This is a bullet because it casts a value judgment on someone’s heartfelt emotion, calling it *unwarranted*. Some will feel this intrudes rationality too far into the domain of love (which often seems anarchic). Nonetheless, Kolodny embraces that to make sense of love in normative terms. Another potential cost is that **Kolodny’s view is somewhat conservative**: it privileges existing relationships. It suggests that *who* you should love largely depends on accidents of birth and circumstance (who happens to be your family, who you happened to meet and befriend). That fits many of our moral intuitions (we do feel special duties to those close), but it could be seen as lacking an aspirational aspect – e.g., can love break boundaries and extend beyond one’s circle? Kolodny would say you can form new relationships (adopted family, found friendships) and then those become reasons, but until you do, you don’t have love obligations. This might be criticized for not encouraging extending love to outsiders. Yet, Kolodny might counter that humans *do* expand their circles by gradually building relationships; you can’t short-circuit that with abstract love for strangers (that’s more like impersonal benevolence, not the personal love he’s analyzing). In short, Kolodny must accept that love is in a sense **parochial** – each person’s loves are justified for them but not universal – and that might conflict with a more universalist ethical sentiment. The reward, however, is a theory that **vindicates our deepest attachments as rational**. It means you don’t have to apologize to philosophical reason for loving your partner “just because she’s *my* partner” – that is a perfectly good reason in Kolodny’s worl[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their)】. It also provides a handle on **love’s cessation**: if someone asks “Was I wrong to stop loving X after they changed so much?”, Kolodny can answer, “No, because the relationship that gave you reason to love no longer exists.” That sort of answer is harder to come by in Frankfurt (who’d say you can’t *choose* anyway) or Velleman (who might say something perplexing like “perhaps you realized they lacked the value you thought”). Kolodny straightforwardly says love is subject to the vagaries of relationships – a strength and a weakness, philosophically.

### Common Ground and Contrasts

It’s worth noting that despite their differences, all three accounts share some **common insights** about love. All agree that love is **particular** (they just explain particularity differently), that love involves **caring deeply for the other’s welfare**, and that love is in an important sense **unconditional or not entirely up to us** (Frankfurt and Kolodny emphasize the unchosen aspect, Velleman too acknowledges love is something that “happens” when we truly see the other). They also each acknowledge, implicitly or explicitly, that **love has a kind of logic or normative structure** – be it internal to the will, embedded in moral value, or grounded in reasons. None of them think love is just inexplicable nonsense; they all believe philosophy can give a coherent account of it, even if they disagree on what that account is.

In contrasting them succinctly: **Frankfurt** sees love as **volitional necessity** – the lover’s will is **captured** by the beloved, without prior reason, and thereby the beloved becomes central to the lover’s practical worl[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63)】. **Velleman** sees love as **moral perception** – the lover’s heart is **captivated** by the inherent worth of the beloved, revealing an *impersonal value in a personal form[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable)】. **Kolodny** sees love as **valuation of a relationship** – the lover’s reason to care is **conferred** by the intimate relation itself, making the beloved *irreplaceably significant* to that love[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their)】.

## The Deeper Problem: Love, Reason, and the Meaning of Persons

Despite their divergent theories, Frankfurt, Velleman, and Kolodny are all wrestling with a **common deep concern**: how to understand love in a way that makes sense of its powerful role in our lives, while addressing the tensions it raises with our ideals of rational agency, morality, and identity. In essence, each is attempting to answer a version of **“What is the philosophical significance of love?”** – and in doing so, each illuminates a broader vision of human nature:

- **Frankfurt’s Overarching Concern:** Frankfurt is fundamentally concerned with **what gives our lives meaning and coherence**. The deep problem he addresses is that of **volitional orientation**: as free beings, we face the question “What should I care about? What ends should guide me?” Pure reason doesn’t answer that – one could be completely rational yet completely indifferent, which Frankfurt sees as a recipe for a hollow life. Love, for Frankfurt, is the answer to this existential void: it **“establishes the boundaries of our will”** and thus defines who we ar[jstor.org](https://www.jstor.org/stable/23685969#:~:text=Volitional%20Rationality%20and%20the%20Necessities,which%20the%20person%20is)[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=,63)】. The puzzle of **freedom vs. necessity** is central here – we appear to freely choose, yet love imposes a necessity on us. Frankfurt’s resolution is that this necessity, coming from love, is actually what *free* and authentic agency looks like: **“the constraints that love brings… are our own [will]”*[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=Frankfurt%20thinks%20that%20though%20we,our%20free%20will%2C%20I%20recommend)】. He offers a **metaphysical and psychological vision** in which persons are essentially beings who *care*, and caring deeply (loving) is what provides *reasons for action, projects to pursue, and an identity to live out[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=The%20Reasons%20of%20Love%20is,purest%20form%20of%20love%20is)[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be)】. He’s addressing a problem as old as Augustine and Kierkegaard: the heart needs something to cling to. By his account, love is both the source of value and the answer to the question of what one should will. This ties into broader themes of **autonomy and identity** – Frankfurt sees love (even love we do not choose) as integral to **“how we should live”*[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=this%20short%20book%2C%20Frankfurt%20explores,of%20love%20like%20romantic%20love)】. In his broader moral psychology, love is more important than abstract moral duty for giving life substance. He even famously argues that **self-love – the love that leads one to love anything at all – is the most primordial form of love*[jprstudies.org](https://www.jprstudies.org/2020/11/review-the-reasons-of-love-by-harry-g-frankfurt/#:~:text=In%20this%20chapter%2C%20Frankfurt%20explains,of%20a%20person%20to%20love)】. That suggests his ultimate concern is that each person finds *something* to love, in order to have a self at all. The **deep problem** is thus: *without love, can we even have values or meaning?* Frankfurt’s answer is no – love is the bedrock. In a way, Frankfurt’s philosophy of love is part of a **larger vision where meaning, will, and love are intertwined**: he offers a somewhat deflationary view of moral obligation and an inflationary view of personal love. The picture that emerges is one where **the personal is primary** – what and whom we love defines our practical world, and morality must accommodate that fact or risk irrelevance to human motivation.
- **Velleman’s Overarching Concern:** Velleman is grappling with the **ethical integration of love**: the deep problem of **“how can we accommodate the claims of love within a moral framework that prizes impartial respect and equality?”*[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=Love%20as%20a%20Moral%20Emotion,The%20University%20of%20Chicago%20Press)】. He reframes this as a question of whether love and morality, which seem to speak different languages (one of partial affection, the other of universal principle), might actually be harmonized by a more profound understanding. Velleman’s conceptualization of the problem is very much in terms of **value and recognition**: he sees love as a test case for a larger issue – how to acknowledge the full value of persons in a world where our attention and emotion are limited. The broader moral vision he offers is **Kantian but humanized**: he takes Kant’s austere doctrine that persons must be treated as ends, and he shows how **this doctrine comes alive in human psychology through love and emotion*[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=capacity%20for%20respect,with%20respect%20but%20also%20some%02times)】. In other words, love is the lived experience of recognizing another’s humanity. This feeds into a **metaphysical view of persons**: implicitly, Velleman suggests that what we love in the beloved is the **“true self”** of the person – not their transient traits, but their core capacity for rational, moral agency (their soul, one might poetically say). Thus, the deep problem is also **what it is to value a person as a person**. By tying love to that value, Velleman shows a way that **the partiality of love doesn’t undermine the equality of persons**: each person might be the center of someone’s loving world, precisely because each person is supremely valuable. His broader psychological vision is somewhat optimistic about human nature: it implies that if we really come to know others in the right way, love will naturally arise (because we will see their value clearly). This aligns with thinkers like Iris Murdoch (whom he cites) in the idea that *attention* to reality (here, the reality of another’s personhood) naturally breeds lov[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20can%20foreshadow%20my%20conclusion,minded)】. Moreover, by calling love a “moral emotion,” Velleman situates love in the same category as **moral sentiments like empathy, sympathy, respect** – emotions that have a built-in rational structure because they respond to real features of the moral world. The overarching concern is thus how **to reconcile head and heart**: Velleman reconciles them by finding a shared object (the value of persons) and describing love as essentially *rational sentiment*. This contributes to a larger project of moral philosophy: showing that our emotions are not just wild forces but can be aligned with ethical truth. In Velleman’s ideal, love is part of what makes morality **psychologically feasible and rich** – morality isn’t just duty, it’s also found in the tender eye contact between mother and child, or the profound appreciation between close friends. Love gives *content* to the Kantian respect, preventing it from being an empty formality. The **deep problem being answered** could be framed as: *How can humans be partial creatures with loved ones and still be moral creatures who acknowledge everyone’s worth?* Velleman answers by elevating love into the moral domain and showing that loving one is not necessarily negating the value of others.
- **Kolodny’s Overarching Concern:** Kolodny is fundamentally addressing the **normative puzzle of partiality**: the deep problem is **“on what basis can we rationally favor ‘our own’ in a world where others might have equal or greater merits?”**. This is part of a larger inquiry in moral philosophy and the philosophy of emotion about **reasons for partiality** – for friendships, family ties, patriotism, etc. Kolodny zeroes in on love as the most personal form of partiality and seeks to **vindicate it in terms of reasons**. By doing so, he’s tackling a broader metaphysical issue about the **nature of reasons and values**: can there be reasons that are neither purely subjective (just a whim) nor objective-and-impersonal (true for everyone)? His answer – the existence of **relationship-dependent reasons** – sketches a middle ground in meta-ethics. It points to a universe where some values are **relative to social context** but still real and normatively binding. The overarching philosophical vision Kolodny offers is one where **human life is understood as inherently relational**: we are not isolated atoms who then decide whom to love; we are born into and continuously form relationships, and these relationships themselves define what matters to us and what we ought to do. This aligns with a broader movement in ethics that emphasizes **the importance of family, community, and history** in the formation of values (contrasting with highly individualist or abstract views). Kolodny’s deep problem can also be seen as the question of **what makes love fitting**. Philosophers worry about fittingness of emotions (“Is this fear justified, is this anger appropriate?”); Kolodny applies that to love: *what makes it fitting to love one person and not another?* His answer (the relationship) ties love to facts about the world (I actually have a relationship with this person) rather than just internal feelings. This is a move to **objectify the context of love**: love isn’t just in my head; it’s an appropriate response to an interpersonal reality. The broader moral implication is that **our duties and values depend on the narratives of our lives**. Who you should care for depends on who you have become through your relationships. In Kolodny’s moral vision, there’s a tapestry of intersecting circles of concern – each person rightfully tends their own garden of relationships, and that’s how value and care propagate in society. Far from being an unfortunate deviation from impartial moral truth, this structure of **“each caring for their own”** is beautiful and rational in his view, preventing a cold moral uniformity and acknowledging the richness of human bonds. The deep issue of **“love’s selective nature”** is thus solved by linking love to the **selective histories we inevitably have**. Kolodny’s contribution is to make explicit something often felt: that *who someone is to us* is a valid reason for love. In doing so, he addresses the philosophical anxiety that love might be irrational or unjustified. His answer builds a bridge between **personal life and ethical theory**, suggesting that ethical theory must expand to include relational facts as reason-giving.

**Unifying Reflection:** At the most overarching level, all three theories are grappling with how love fits into the **human quest for meaning and moral understanding**. Each sees love as attempting to solve a profound human problem:

- For Frankfurt, the problem is **meaninglessness or directionlessness**: love solves it by providing focus and importance.
- For Velleman, the problem is **ethical alienation or coldness**: love solves it by imbuing our recognition of others’ value with warmth and motivation.
- For Kolodny, the problem is **justification of favoritism**: love (through relationships) solves it by showing that our loyalties have a rational basis in the connections that constitute our lives.

All three ultimately contribute to an even more fundamental question: **“What is the place of love in the universe of value?”** Is love a *fundamental value* in its own right (Frankfurt tends to say yes – the loves of our life are the source of valu[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=Such%20problems%20lead%20other%20philosophers,lover%27s%20desire%20is%20to%20be)】), or is love a *response to* value (Velleman – love responds to the value of person[philarchive.org](https://philarchive.org/archive/VELLAA#:~:text=I%20have%20suggested%20that%20love,that%20people%20are%20equally%20valuable)】), or is love a *preserver and amplifier of* value within relationships (Kolodny – love upholds the value found in meaningful relationship[mit.edu](https://www.mit.edu/~shaslang/mprg/KolodnyLVR.pdf#:~:text=person%20is%20one%27s%20relationship%20to,as%20Frankfurt%27s%20ignore%20at%20their)】)? Each answers in their way, but all treat love as deeply significant, not an illusion or mere appetite.

In conclusion, **Frankfurt, Velleman, and Kolodny each illuminate a different facet of love’s complex nature**: Frankfurt highlights love’s role in shaping the will and conferring importance, Velleman highlights love as the moral recognition of persons, and Kolodny highlights love’s foundation in the fabric of relationships. They disagree on the mechanics and justification of love, yet they converge in treating love as a key to understanding human motivation, ethical life, and what it is that makes us profoundly connected to others. Each theory, with its dialectical reconstruction and resolution of puzzles, contributes to a richer philosophical portrait of love – a portrait that acknowledges love’s *passion* (Frankfurt’s necessity), its *principle* (Velleman’s respect-like appreciation), and its *particularity* (Kolodny’s relationships), thereby addressing the overarching problem of how love can be at once so irrational-seeming in its partiality and yet so central to what we treasure about being human.
