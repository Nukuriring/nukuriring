Some words are open

Some words live in my throat

Seeking like gypsies over my tongue
To explode through my lips

Some words
bedevil me.

# Building on this opening stanza, the middle stanza explores ‘words’ in more detail. I will explore the interpretation of ‘words’ as poet’s own language. Some words are ‘open’ like a diamond on glass windows (diamond can cut glass, so black people can cut *through* the glass barrier and make themselves seen). As the diamond scrapes along the window, it screeches and ‘sings’, further drawing attention to itself, aided by the sunlight glinting on it.

# 

# Note the elemental properties to Lorde’s poem: just as that coal comes from the inside of the earth, so the diamond is augmented by the sunlight.

# 

# But some words are like ‘stapled wagers’: compromised because the author has allowed their work to be diluted or censored so they can make it into print, ‘wagering’ on a greater degree of success if they don’t upset the apple-cart too much. This book is like a tooth that’s been pulled out by an incompetent dentist, corrupt and rotten.

# 

# Some words, by contrast, live in the poet’s throat and multiply like adders: poisonous and dangerous, like the bite of a venomous snake. Yet other words want to get out (like ‘gypsies’ they are travellers, seeking to move, never staying still for long), and are desperate to be spoken by the poet’s tongue, like young birds hatching from an egg. Yet other words ‘bedevil’ or torment the poet.

In “Coal, Audre Lorde weaves together various themes and metaphors to present a multifaceted exploration of blackness, language, and identity. The poem celebrates the power of language and its ability to shape and redefine the self. Lorde asserts her blackness as a source of strength and resilience, drawing parallels between her identity and the enduring nature of coal.

Through the imagery of coal burning and immolating, the poet acknowledges the pain and sacrifice inherent in her existence. The kisses given to her lover are likened to acts of self-immolation, suggesting the intense passion and fire within her. Lorde recognizes the complexities of blackness, acknowledging the destructive and transformative qualities it possesses.

Within the poem, Lorde also reflects on the limitations and constraints placed upon black individuals. She references the blind dead blowflies on a carcass, symbolizing the oppressive forces that seek to silence and confine black voices. Despite this, the poet finds solace in the stillness that emerges before silence, seeing it as a potent and transformative space from which her voice can arise.

Overall, "Coal" by Audre Lorde explores themes of identity, language, and the resilience of blackness. The poem presents a complex and nuanced understanding of black identity, embracing both the challenges and the strength that it entails. Lorde's words evoke a sense of empowerment and celebration, asserting the richness and power of black voices and experiences.

By intertwining the imagery of coal, fire, and language, Lorde invites readers to reflect on their own identities, the power of language, and the transformative potential that lies within embracing and celebrating one's authentic self.
