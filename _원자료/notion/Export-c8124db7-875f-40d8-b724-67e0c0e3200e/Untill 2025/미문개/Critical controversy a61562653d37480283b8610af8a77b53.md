# Critical controversy

허클베리 핀의 결말에 대한 비평적 논쟁

Leo Marx claimed, in his *Mr. Eliot, Mr. Trilling, and Huckleberry Finn,* that the ending of Twain’s *Huckleberry Finn* undermines the whole theme of the novel. The ending undermines “Jim’s yearning for freedom” and even makes it the “object of” utter “nonsense”. 

Julius Lester, in *Morality and Adeventures of Huckleberry Finn,* expands the point made by Leo Marx. She claims that Jim was a “plaything, an excuse for “the *adventure of it,”* to be used as it suits fancies of white folk”. Jim had never been treated as a human being througout the novel. Julius Lester suggests this fact as an evidence that Twain did not really care about Jim’s life.

David L. Smith, in *Huck, Jim, and American Racial Disocourse*, defends the novel from the charges pressed by precedent critics. He claims It is an anti-racist novel which adpots the strategy of subversion to attack on “fiction” of race. Twain’s use of N-word exposes the absurdity and the “mismatch between racial abstractions and real human beings.”

Jane Smiley, in *Say it Ain’t so, Huck: Second Thoughts on Mark Twain’s “Masterpiece”*, disagrees with the critics who recognizes the novel as “Masterpiece”. Reading the novel as adopting a startegy of subversion mirrors the reasoning Whites usually endorse when they need to convince themselves that they are not raicist. The novle itself is a sort of whit-washing of Afro-American lives. 

Tony Morrison suggests the subtle and nuanced view in *Introduction to Adventures of Hucklberry Finn.* He concedes that this nove does creat some uneasiness to the readers. However, the fact that this novel raises the uneasiness or the controversy or the argument is reason why this novel is a distinguished classic. It is clear that this novel should not be dismissed.

Alan Gribben who publishes the new edition of the novel, replaced every N-word to slaves. Michiko Kakutani criticizes this editorial decision. She clamis that attempts to sanitize the classcal work is a denial of harsh historical reality and takes the opportunity away from the student to understand the context. There is a “patronizing Big Brother aspect” in this censorship, telling readers that they should be protected from offensive, inappropiate literatures.

As Korean, I find myself to be alineated from the heat of the controversy. Twain’s use of N-word does not really give me any “feelings” or “uneasiness”, because I have never thought of myself to be involved in such racial problem. I assume that is why I do not see any appeal from claims that the N-word, or *Huckleberry Finn* should be censored or banned from the public education curriculum. Still, this critical controversy allows me to give some thought about the question: “what attitude should we have in adpoting so-called classcial works?”. I am not in a position to give a definite answer right here, but I believe it is clear that we should take adpotation as a ‘coversation’ between the work and contemporary readers, rather than one-sided projection.

    Leo Marx claimed in his essay "Mr. Eliot, Mr. Trilling, and Huckleberry Finn" that the ending of Twain's novel undermines the whole theme by making Jim's yearning for freedom the object of utter nonsense. Julius Lester expands on Marx's point in Morality and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, suggesting that Jim is treated as a plaything and excuse for adventure, rather than as a human being. Lester argues that Twain did not really care about Jim's life.
    
    In Huck, Jim, and American Racial Discourse, David L. Smith defends the novel against these charges. He claims that Huckleberry Finn is an anti-racist novel that adopts a strategy of subversion to attack the fiction of race. Twain's use of the N-word exposes the absurdity and mismatch between racial abstractions and real human beings.
    
    Jane Smiley, in "Say It Ain't So, Huck: Second Thoughts on Mark Twain's 'Masterpiece,'" disagrees with those who recognize the novel as a "masterpiece." Reading the novel as adopting a strategy of subversion mirrors the reasoning that whites often use to convince themselves that they are not racist. Smiley argues that the novel is a sort of whitewashing of African American lives.
    
    Toni Morrison offers a more nuanced view in her introduction to Adventures of Huckleberry Finn. While acknowledging that the novel creates uneasiness for readers, she argues that this very uneasiness and controversy is what makes it a distinguished classic. Morrison emphasizes that the novel should not be dismissed.
    
    Alan Gribben published a new edition of the novel, replacing every N-word with "slave." Michiko Kakutani criticizes this editorial decision, arguing that attempts to sanitize classical works deny the harsh historical reality and take away the opportunity for students to understand the context. She notes a "patronizing Big Brother aspect" to this censorship, which tells readers that they should be protected from offensive and inappropriate literature.
    
    As a Korean, I find myself alienated from the heat of this controversy. Twain's use of the N-word does not evoke any feelings of uneasiness for me, as I have never thought of myself as involved in racial problems. However, I believe that this critical controversy allows us to reflect on the question of how we should approach classical works. Rather than projecting our own biases onto these works, we should view them as a conversation between the work and contemporary readers.
