# Emily Dickinson

339

I like a look of Agony,
Because I know it's true—
Men do not sham Convulsion,
Nor simulate, a Throe—

The Eyes glaze once—and that is Death—
Impossible to feign
The Beads upon the Forehead
By homely Anguish strung.

This poem explores the speaker's fascination with the authenticity of human emotion, particularly the expression of pain and agony. The speaker notes that true agony cannot be faked or pretended, and expresses admiration for its genuine display. The final lines of the poem suggest that the speaker finds beauty in the physical signs of agony, such as the beads of sweat on a forehead, even as they acknowledge the sadness and finality of death.

I think I will recall this poem at moments when I become skeptical about the pain felt by the people I love. When I am too overwhelmed to acknowledge that I love them and they love me, and cannot admit that this love makes me vulnerable, I wonder how I can face the truth and acknowledge the love. In such moments, I believe that what allows me to see the truth of love is their pain, which I simply cannot deny.

448

I died for Beauty – but was scarce
Adjusted in the Tomb
When One who died for Truth, was lain
In an adjoining Room –

He questioned softly "Why I failed"?
"For Beauty", I replied –
"And I – for Truth – Themself are One –
We Brethren, are", He said –

And so, as Kinsmen, met a Night –
We talked between the Rooms –
Until the Moss had reached our lips –
And covered up – our names –

In this poem, the speaker reflects on the commonality she shares with another person who has died, but whose death was motivated by a different ideal: Truth. The speaker's death was for Beauty, while the other person's death was for Truth. However, the two are united in death and become "kinsmen" who share a common fate.

I think I will recall this poem at moments when I confront the mortality and vulnerability of myself in the midst of pursuing my ideal. I might become deeply frustrated and depressed by the fact that my ideal means almost nothing in the face of the dreary mortal destiny of human beings. Yet, at the same time, I might be relieved, for I can keep talking about my idea with my 'kinsmen' until the 'Moss had reached our lips’.

1773

My life closed twice before its close—
It yet remains to see
If Immortality unveil
A third event to me,

So huge, so hopeless to conceive,
As these that twice befell.
Parting is all we know of heaven,
And all we need of hell.

In this poem, the speaker reflects on two major losses or changes that have occurred in her life, which she describes as her "life clos[ing] twice before its close". She wonders if there will be a third event, perhaps even more significant than the first two, that will occur before the end of her life. The final two lines of the poem suggest that the pain of separation and loss is the most we can comprehend of heaven and the most we need to understand of hell.

I think I will recall this poem at moments when I need to acknowledge the inevitability of the death of my loved ones. I know that my parents will pass away someday, and all of my loved ones will pass away too. I am also aware that I, myself, will pass away at some point. It will undoubtedly be a painful and sad experience, but when the time comes, I will need to find a way to accept its inevitability.
