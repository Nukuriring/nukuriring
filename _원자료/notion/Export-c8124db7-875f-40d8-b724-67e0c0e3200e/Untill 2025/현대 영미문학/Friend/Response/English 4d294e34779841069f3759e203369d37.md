If Quine is correct, then there cannot be such a thing as "true communication" between beings with different communication systems. In the context of the novel, and animals, we could say the following about the translation indeterminacy thesis: there is no objective fact about deciding what dogs, cats, and parrots meant. The meaning we derive from them is only an interpretation of animal behavior based on human convenience and utility.

I don't remember exactly where, but Wittgenstein once said, "A dog can be angry or sad, but it cannot hope. To hope requires grammar." He probably believed that hope is a mental state that can only be possessed by beings who are already fully skilled in the concept of "hope" and its application conditions in language. If we stay on this train of thought, we may have to say that dogs cannot mourn or wait either. They are not participants in the language games that use concepts such as mourning and waiting, and therefore their actions cannot be described accurately with vocabulary such as "wait" or "mourn."

Quine's thesis of translation indeterminacy and Wittgenstein's remarks seem to completely block the possibility of understanding animals, especially dogs in the context of this novel. At best, it seems that when we appear to understand animals, we are only engaging in a analogus projection from the human perspective. Is it really true that humans cannot understand animals, and animals cannot understand humans?

Returning to the context of the novel, the first-person narrator "I" does not easily assert what Apollo thinks. She examines Apollo's reactions as if exploring an unknown language of an undiscovered tribe. Therefore, she is always full of doubts. She wonders if Apollo likes the massage or is enduring it, estimates his emotions by inferring about his memory (125), and observes his reactions and actions from all angles, sometimes judging what he might be thinking. She imagines the difficulties of living as a dog and wonders how mysterious humans might feel to them (132). She constantly negotiates with Apollo's life, intersecting and rubbing her life against his, willingly accepting the difficulties that arise in the process. She stammers while guessing what Apollo's life might look like, experimenting cautiously but persistently with what might happen when her life intersects with his. She recites Rilke's poetry to Apollo and observes his reactions, carefully observing how this exchange affects her own life and Apollo's.

She realizes that she views Apollo from a human perspective, but she does not monopolize the interpretive power over Apollo's emotions, thoughts, and reactions. She acknowledges that Apollo is like a puzzle to her. Her approach to Apollo is similar to how the instruments of an orchestra tune to one another, playing her own notes and listening to Apollo's reactions. Sometimes the notes clash and the possibility of correct tuning seems elusive, but she does not give up on adjusting her and Apollo's frequency.

This relationship between Apollo and ‘I’ seems to exemplify the possibility that Quine and Wittgenstein's arguments do not capture. They touch each other's lives as peers. or I might say “companiable life”
