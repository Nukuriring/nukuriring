My research aims to reveal the connections between the philosophical skepticism of so-called “Problem of the other” and Early modern way of Rhetorical self-fashioning through lens of Othello. For this purpose I need to bring out the precise concept of the “Skepticism on The Other Mind” and “Early modern way of Rhetorical Self-Fashioning”. I mainly aim to include the work of Stanley Cavell, Joel Altman, Stephen Greenblatt, additional to relatively auxiliary references of Paul Kottman, David Schalkwyk, and others. 

Cavell – The Stake of the Other
Cavell’s The Stake of the Other provides the concept of “Skepticism” required for my research. Notably, he brings out his notion of understanding the genre of tragedy “as a kind of epistemological problem, or as the outcome of the problem of knowledge- of the dominance of modern philosophical thought by it”(126). He basically identifies Othello as substantiating the humane anxiety toward a possibility that could be stated as following: “the integrity of my (human, finite) existence may depend on the fact and the idea of another being’s existence, and on the possibility of Proving that existence, an existence conceived from my very dependence and incompleteness, hence conceived as perfect, and conceived as producing me”.

Cavell points out that Othello presents the “the logic, the emotion and the scene of skepticism epimoized”. As Cavell specifies the Skepticism with what he calls “the best case argument”, which involves such statements of “If I know anything, I know this”, Othello indeed brings out the logic of typical mode of skepticism (This point will be more articulated in the reading of Cavell’s other book: “The Claim of Reason”). Futheremore, Cavell designates the feeling of ‘Discovery, or astonishment” as a typical, paradigmatic emotion accompnied with Skepticism. This emotion contains the sense of ‘I-thoguht-I-knew-it-but-I-did-not.’ It is not an astonishment of “knowledge”. It is rather astonishment of “effort to stave the knowledge off”. Finally, the scene of Skepticism can be found in Othello’s language and actions which “continuosly outstrips reality, dissolves it in trance or dream or in the beauty of ugliness of his incantatory imagination; im which he visualizes the possiblities that reason, unaided, cannot rule out.”

Cavell proceeds by pointing out the textual evidences which shows Othello’s conceiving his integrity or purity to be dependent on Desdemona’s existence: “To say he loses Desdemona’s power to confirm his image of himself is to say that he loses his old power of imagination. And this is to say that he loses his grasp of his own nature”. Thus he further claims that Othello’s nature, or life is clearly staked upon Desdemona’s virginity. 

With this claim, Cavell poignantly points out that Othello must be denying his knowledge on Desdemona. By denial, Cavell merely means that “we … not assume that we know this woman(Desdemona) better than this man(Othello) knows her. - making Othello some kind of exotic, gorgeous, superstitious lunkhead”. At least to some extent, Othello knows Iago’s statement as false. - Then, apparently, we should say that Othello wants “to believe Iago, to be trying, against his knowledge, to believe him. Othello’s eager insistence on Iago’s honesty, his eager salking of his thirst for knowledge with that poision, is not a sign of his stupidity … but of his devouring need of it”. 

Then, how could the idea of “Desdemona as an adulterous whore” is more convenient to him than the idea of her chaste? Why Othello fears acknowledging Desdemona’s faithfulness? Cavell suggests that the “exhibition of wedding sheets” in the last murder scene provides us with some clue. - Cavell seems to claim that the exhibition of wedding sheets “can only refer to the practice of proving purity by staning”. However, whichever answers provided toward these practice of proving, Othello cannot accept neither of it. Othellos’ torture of logic would be like this: “Either I shed her blood and scarred her or I did not. If I did not she was not a virgin and this is a stain upon me. If I did then she is no longer a virgin and this is stain upon me. Either way, I am contaminated” - What haunts the Othello is not the answer towards the question of Desdemona’s chastity, but the question itself. “Her virginity, her intactness, her perfection, had been gladly foregone by her for him… It is the sacrifice he could not accept, for then he was not himself perfect”. 

Cavell concludes that Othello is “rendered impotent and murderous by aroused, or by having aroused, female sexuality”. Being horrfied by human sexuality, Cavell suggests, is the posibility that no human being can be free of. Human sexuality is the field in which the fantasy of finitude, of its accpetance and its repetitious overcoming is worked out. 

This much said, Othello can be understood as a skeptic about Desdemona’s existence. He certainly knows Desdemona “exists, is flesh and blood; is separate from him; is the other”. But this much knowledge is what brings Othllo into madness, as this knowledge realizes the posbility of imperfect, blood-flesh made, partial and dependent existence which his own life or nature is staked on. According to Cavell, “his professions of skepticism over her faithfulness are a cover story for a deeper conviction; a terrible doubt covering a yet more terrible certainty, an unstatable certainty”. And the “cause of his skepticism is an attempt to convert the human condition into an intellectual difficulty, a riddle”.

Cavell believes Othello’s stoning Desdemona is “the failure to acknowledge a best case of the other … and the death of our capacity to acknowledge as such, the turning of our hearts of stone”.
