· Epistemic (=Primary) Intension as the A Priori Element of Meaning

- The epistemic intension of a linguistic expression or a concept is what we know a priori when we know its meaning. Hence this must be knowledge we have regardless of what our world is like.

- Depending on what our world is actually like, the epistemic intension of a linguistic expression or a concept will determine its extension.

(예) “The shortest spy”의 인식적 내포: <가장 작은 스파이>

w1: 스미스를 외연으로 산출

w2: 존스를 외연으로 산출

Since we do not have complete knowledge of our world, we can consider various possibilities regarding what our world is actually like while being ignorant.

- In so doing, we consider each possible circumstance of our world (=each possible world) as an actual world.

- Hence the epistemic intension of a linguistic expression or a concept can be understood as a function from possible worlds considered as actual to an extension.

· Subjunctive (=Secondary) Intension as Not Necessarily A Priori Element of Meaning

- Even if we have complete knowledge of our world, we can still consider various counterfactual circumstances of our world while imagining what the world could have been like or might have been like. In so doing, we consider each possible circumstance of world (= each possible world) as a purely counterfactual world.

- Kripke and Kaplan have persuasively argued that, unlike definite descriptions, the intensions of proper names, natural kind terms, and indexicals cannot be understood as their epistemic intensions. For it seems that these expressions have the same extensions regardless of what our world might have been like, that is, regardless of different possible circumstances of our world (=each possible world) considered as purely counterfactual worlds.

(e.g.) (1) “The most transparent liquid is not transparent” (False)

(2) “The most transparent liquid could have been non-transparent” (False)

(3) “The best soccer player is Michael Jordan” (False)

(4) “The best soccer player could have been Michael Jordan” (True)

(1*) “George Orwell is a singer” (False)

(2*) “George Orwell could have been a singer” (True)

(3*) “George Orwell is Franz Kafka” (False)

(4*) “George Orwell could have been Franz Kafka” (False)

(1**) “Water has an odor” (False)

(2**) “Water could have an odor” (True)

(3**) “Water is XYZ” (False)

(4**) “Water could have been XYZ” (False)

(1***) “He is a singer” [pointing to Prof. Kang] (False)

(2***) “He could have been a singer” [pointing to Prof. Kang] (True)

(3***) “He is Saul Kripke” [pointing to Prof. Kang] (False)

(4***) “He could have been Saul Kripke” [pointing to Prof. Kang] (False)

- One might conclude from Kripke and Kaplan’s arguments that these linguistic expressions do not have (epistemic) intensions and have just extensions. [ the Millian Theory]

- But it is also possible to think that these expressions have two different types of intensions, namely epistemic and subjunctive intensions, and that the latter types of intensions determine the extensions of these expressions depending on what our world could have been like, not depending on what our world is actually like.

- If so, the subjunctive intension of a linguistic expression or a concept can be understood as a function from possible worlds considered as counterfactual to an extension.

· Williamson’s Anti-Luminosity Argument: Settings, Definitions, and Principles

- Settings

* case  = centered possible world <w, a, t>

* condition C = event or state of affair = a set of (centered) possible worlds in which C obtains

* t0, t1, . . . , tn = a series of times at one millisecond intervals from dawn to noon.

* i = the case at ti (o  i  n)

- Definition of ‘in a position to know’

(PK) S is in a position to know p = no obstacle must block one’s path to knowing p.

- From this definition, Williamson supposes that the following holds:

(PK-K) If one is in a position to know p, and if one has done what one is in a position to do to decide whether p is true, then one does know p.

- Definition of luminosity

A condition C is luminous iff (L) holds

(L) For every case , if in  C obtains, then in  one is in a position to know that C obtains.

- Margin-for-Error Principle (MAR)

(MAR) If in i one knows that a condition C obtains, then in i+1 the condition C also obtains.

- Williamson’s Argument for the Margin-for-Error Principle

“Consider a time ti between to and tn, and suppose that at ti one knows that one feels cold. Thus one is at least reasonably confident (=believes) that one feels cold, for otherwise one would not know. Moreover, this confidence must be reliably based, for otherwise one would still not know that one feels cold. Now at ti+1 one is almost equally confident that one feels cold, by the description of the case. So if one does not feel cold at ti+1, then one’s confidence (=belief) at ti that one feels cold is not reliably based, for one’s almost equal confidence on a similar basis a millisecond alter that one felt cold is mistaken.” (p.97; bolds are added)

· Williamson’s Argument that Knowledge Must be “Realible” [≒Safe]

- “Within the limits just explained, we can nevertheless see how a reliability condition [≒safety condition] on knowledge is consonant with the role of knowledge in the causal explanation of action, as described in sections 2.4 and 3.4. Knowledge is superior to mere true belief because, being more robust in the face of new evidence, it better facilitates action at a temporal distance. Other things being equal, given rational sensitivity to new evidence, present knowledge makes future true belief more likely than mere present true belief does.” (p.101)

· Williamson’s Anti-Luminosity Argument against Chalmers’s Theory of Phenomenal Beliefs?

- According to Chalmers, there is a category of phenomenal beliefs, which he calls “direct phenomenal beliefs”, that are infallible or incorrigible in that they cannot be false when we have them.

- So when a subject S instantiates a phenomenal property F by having a certain experience and believes <I have F>, S’s instantiation of F must be “luminous” in Williamson’s sense. That is, S must not only believe but also know <I have F>.

- Since Williamson argues that there cannot be such “luminous” states of affairs, either Williamson’s argument is wrong or Chalmer’s acquaintance theory of phenomenal beliefs is mistaken.

· The Constitutive Relation between Direct Phenomenal Beliefs and Phenomenal Properties

- According to Chalmers, the content of a direct phenomenal belief is partly constituted by the phenomenal quality itself “in that the content will mirror the quality (picking out instances of the quality in all epistemic possibilities), and in that across a wide range of nearby conceptually possible cases in which the underlying quality is varied while background properties are held constant, the content will co-vary to mirror the quality.” (p.235)

- If we accept that such a constitutive relation holds between direct phenomenal beliefs and phenomenal properties, then it seems that we can resist Williamson’s argument.

· Chalmers’s Possible Response to Williamson’s Anti-Luminosity Argument

- Even if Chalmers accepts Williamson’s requirement that knowledge must be safe, he can reject Willaimson’s further claim that the safety condition implies the Margin-for-Error Principle (MAR).

(MAR) If in i one knows that a condition C obtains, then in i+1 the condition C also obtains.

- Argument

(Step 1) According to Williamson, there must be a time tk such that, in k one feels cold but in k+1 one does not feel cold.

(Step 2) If Chamers’s acquaintance theory of phenomenal belief is right, then in k one’s belief will have the content <I feel cold>, while in k+1 one’s belief will have the content <I don’t feel cold>, for the contents of direct phenomenal beliefs are constituted by phenomenal qualities so that two phenomenal beliefs cannot share the same content when they are about different phenomenal properties.

(Step 3) But then k+1 would be the case in which one no longer has the belief <I feel cold>. If so, Williamson is wrong in supposing that one will be “almost equally confident” that one feels cold in k+1.

(Step 4) Hence k+1 is not one of those “nearby possible worlds” in which one believes <I feel cold> and this belief is false, for one no longer has this belief in k+1.

(Step 5) Therefore, we can insist that in k one does know <I feel cold> although it is no longer the case that in k+1 one feels cold. Hence we must reject (MAR).

- In the thought experiment given by Williamson, it is likely that one will not have linguistic expressions corresponding to the different feelings one has in k and k+1. If asked, one might simply answer “I feel this” in both k and k+1.

- But we should note that, in Chalmers’s theory of phenomenal beliefs, the two utterances of ‘this’ in k and k+1 will correspond to different concepts whose content are constituted by one’s feeling cold and by one’s not feeling cold, respectively.
