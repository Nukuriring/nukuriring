Rationalist and Rational Agency Theories of Self-Knowledge

· Rationalist Theories (Shoemaker, Gallois)

- The capacity for self-knowledge is an essential nature of rational thinkers, in that anyone

who is a rational thinker must be capable of knowing his/her own mental states in virtue of

his/her rational capacity.

· Rational Acency Theories (Burge, Moran, Boyle, Bilgrami, Coliva)

- At least some of our mental states, in particular intentional states such as beliefs, desires, and

intentions, express our rational agency in the sense that they are not merely things that occur

in us but things we do to which we are committed and for which we are responsible.

Burge on the Distinction between (Epistemic) Justification, Warrant, and Entitlement

Usual Distinction between Justification and Warrant

- After Gettier’s counter examples to the analysis of knowledge as true justified belief, many philosophers use the concept of warrant for “whatever precisely it is, which together with truth makes the difference between knowledge and mere true belief.” (Plantinga (1993), Warrant: The Current Debate; p.3)

그러나 버지의 보증 개념은 이러한 일반적 보증 개념이 아니다!

(1) 일반적 보증 개념: 보증된 참인 믿음 = 지식

(2) 버지의 보증 개념: 보증된 참인 믿음은 여전히 지식이 아닐 수 있다.

Burge on the Distinction among Warrant, Justification and Entitlement

- But Burge uses the notion of warrant in a different way, according to which true warranted belief can still fall short of knowledge.

- Burge uses the notion of warrant as an umbrella term that covers justification and entitlement. Justification is warrant through a reason, whereas entitlement is warrant without a reason. Burge thinks that both justification and entitlement are evidential. (There is no such thing as non-evidential warrant).

(1) S’s belief that p is warranted iff S has a good and reliable route to the truth of S’s belief that p.

(2) S’s belief that p is justified  iff S’s belief that p is warranted through a reason.

※ a reason = a propositional content that can serve as an answer to ‘why’ questions.

(3) S’s belief that p is entitled  iff S’s belief that p is warranted without a reason.

- Burge’s main motives for introducing the idea of entitlement are (1) to explain how non-human animals and children can have knowledge without attributing to them capacities for reasoning, and (ii) to explain our perceptual knowledge without supposing that our perceptual experience has propositional or conceptual content. (※ Burge thinks that our perceptual experience has only non-conceptual content).

- According to Burge, we are (defeasibly) entitled to have our perceptual beliefs as long as the following two conditions are met: (i) the corresponding perceptual experience is anti-individually individuated and (ii) it is reliably veridical in the normal environment.

- Bur Burge does not restrict his notion of entitlement to perceptual beliefs. According to him, for example, we are entitled to have our judgments (or beliefs) about our thoughts as long as we are capable of critical reasoning (plus the condition that the contents of our thoughts that are the subject matter of these judgments are anti-individually individuated). This entitlement has a priori basis. For we know by a priori reflection that our critical reasoning is reasonable, although the person who is entitled to have beliefs about his/her thoughts need not be aware of this a priori knowledge.

- (Step 3) In order to be subject to the rational norms necessarily associated with critical reasoning, we must be able to make judgments about our thoughts (= judgments about the contents of our intentional states, our intentional states, reasons, and reasoning).

- (Step 4) Given the reasonability of the whole process of critical reasoning, our judgments about our own thoughts must be a non-observational, non-empirical form of knowledge.

* (Step 4-1) Given the reasonability of the whole process of critical reasoning, we must be epistemically entitled to our judgments about our own intentional states.

* (Step 4-2) Given the reasonability of the whole process of critical reasoning, our judgments about our own intentional states must be knowledge and not merely (epistemically) entitled judgments.

* (Step 4-3) Given the specific role of knowledge of our thoughts in critical reasoning, it must be non-observational and non-empirical.

The reasoning involves not only Smith’s judgments about reasons such as r, s, and not-p, which are propositions or thought contents, but also his judgments about his propositional attitudes (=intentional mental states) such as his belief that p, his belief that r, and his belief that s.

“Critical reasoning involves an ability to assess truth, falsity, evidential support, entailment, and non-

entailment among propositions or thought contents. It also involves an ability to assess the truth

and reasonability of reasoning – hence attitudes. This is not to say that critical reasoning must focus

on attitudes, as opposed to their subject matter. Normally we reason not about ourselves but about

the world or about practical goods. But to be fully a critical reasoner, one must be able to – and

sometimes actually – identify, distinguish, evaluate propositions as asserted, denied, hypothesized,

or merely considered. Such abilities and activities are central to argumentation.” (p.74.)

- An example of critical reasoning that does not involve judgments about one’s propositional

attitudes.

It is raining now. Whenever it is raining, the humidity in my room is going up. Therefore,

the humidity in my room is going up now.

- Suppose Smith went through the above reasoning. Suppose further that, rather than blindly

reasoning in accordance with the logical rule of modus ponens, He recognizes that the fact that

it is raining and the general fact that, whenever it is raining, the humidity in his room is going

up, together provide the reason for the fact that the humidity in his room is going up now, so

that he can answer to the question “Why is the humidity in your room is going up?”. Hence he

is engaged in critical reasoning here.

“When one carries out a proof, one checks the steps of the reasoning, making sure that the inferences are valid. Any activity of proof requires some conception of validity, which requires an ability to think of the propositions in a proof as constituting reasons for what follows from them. Indeed, it is arguable that use of therefore in reasoning – deductive or otherwise – constitutes an exercise of this meta-cognitive ability.” (p.74)

- But the above critical reasoning involves only Smith’s judgments about the world and does not involve any of his judgments about his beliefs. Of course, Smith believes that it is raining now, believes that whenever it is raining, the humidity in his room is going up, and comes to believe that the humidity in his room is going up now as a result of the above critical reasoning. But the  critical reasoning itself does not involve any of Smith’s judgments about his beliefs.

· Burge’s Argument for (Step 4-1)

- (Step 4-1-1) Critical reasoning is reasoning in which norms of reaon apply to how our thoughts (=intentional states) should be affected partly on the basis of reasoning that derives from judgments about our thoughts.

- (Step 4-1-2) But if our reflection (= judgment) about our intentional states are not epistemically entitled, then the rational connection between the thoughts reflected upon and the reflection would be broken

- (Step 4-1-3) If so, no norms of reason would apply to how our thoughts(=intentional states) should be affected based on reasoning, which implies that the reasoning cannot be critical reasoning.

- (Step 4-1-4) Therefore, we must be entitled to our judgments about our thoughts(=intentional states) in order for them to form an integral part of our critical reasoning.

※ When Burge claims that critical reasoning is “reasonable”, he has in mind that it is a good and reliable route to truth. This is why we are epistemically entitled to our judgments about our thoughts as long as they are an integral part of critical reasoning. Burge thinks that we can know by a priori reflection that critical reasoning is “reasonable” in this sense.

· Burge’s Argument for (Step 4-2)

- (Step 4-2-1) Critical reasoning can be “reasonable” (= a good and reliable route to truth) only if  judgments about our thoughts are knowledge, that is, only if they are (i) normally true and (ii) connected to truth in a non-accidental and knowledge-yielding way.

- (Step 4-2-2) Therefore, judgments about our thoughts must be knowledge if they form an integral part of critical reasoning.

· Burge’s Argument for (Step 4-3)

- (Step 4-3-1) If our judgments about our thoughts in critical reasoning were observational or empirical knowledge, then our epistemic warrant for them would rest on the exstence of a pattern of veridical but brute, contingent, and non-rational relations between our thoughts and our judgments about our thoughts.

- (Step 4-3-2) But this understanding of our epistemic warrant for our judgments about our thoughts incompatible with the role of these judgments in critical reasoning.

(Step 4-3-2-1) Rational evaluation of attitudes commonly applies to and within a perspective or point of view.

(Step 4-3-2-2) Let us suppose that all one’s knowledge of one’s thoughts fit the simple observational model.

(Step 4-3-2-3) Then one’s entitlement to instances of such knowledge would always rest on purely contingent and non-rational relations between any given judgment about one’s thoughts and the thougths themselves, which are the subject matter of the judgment.

(Step 4-3-2-4) If so, brute error would be possible in any given case.

※ One makes brute errors in one’s judgments iff

the judgments were in error (=false) even if (i) they were rational (=had epistmeic warrants) and (ii) one’s perception and ordinary understanding were functioning optimally.

(Step 4-3-2-5) If so, normative evaluation of reasonability and epistmeic entitlement in critical reasoning – in checking and evaluating one’s reasoning – would apply within the perspective of the judgments, but not immediately within the perspective of the subject matter of the judgments.

(Step 4-3-2-6) In critical reasoning, however, the two perspectives, namely the perspective of the judgments and the perspective of the subject matter of the judgments, are necessarily and rationally connected with each other, so that if there is a reason for judging that there is reason against a certain thought one has, it rationally follows immediately that there is reason for changing or supllementing it, in which the reason applies within the perspective of the subject matter of the judgments.

(Step 4-3-2-7) The supposition that our judgments about our thoughts in critical reasoning were observational or empirical knowledge, and accordingly that our epistemic warrant for them rests on some veridical but brute, contingent, and non-rational relations between our thoughts and our judgments about our attitudes, cannot explain why this necessary and rational connection holds between the perspective of the judgments about our thoughts and the perspective of the subject matter of these judgments.

(e.g.) In the course of critical reasoning, I reasonably conclude that my belief that a given person is guilty rests entirely on unreasonable premises or bad reasoning. Then “it normally follows immediately both for the perspective of the review and for the perspective of the reviewed belief that it is reasonable to give up my belief about guilt or look for new grounds for it.” (p.82).

※ Compare:

(1) In the course of critical reasoning, I reasonably conclude that it will not rain tomorrow. Then it normally follows immediately that it is reasonable to give up the state of affairs that it will rain tomorrow or look for new ground for it. (???)

(2) In the course of critical reasoning, I reasonably conclude that there is no reason at all for my current feeling of pain. Then it normally follows immediately that it is reasonable to give up my feeling of pain or look for new ground for it. (???)

(3) In the course of critical reasoning, I reasonably conclude that there is no reason at all for my current feeling of envy toward Chulsoo. Then it normally follows immediately that it is reasonable to give up my feeling envy (???).

- Burge’s Conclusion

“The reviewing of reasons that is integral to critical reasoning includes the review and the

rivewed attitudes in a single point of view. The simple observational model treats the review

and the system being reviwed as dissociated in a way incompatible with the norms of critical

reasoning. It makes the reviwed system an object of investigation, but not part of the

investigation’s point of view. So the model fails to account for the norms of critical reasoning.”

(p.83)

· Burge’s Additional Arguments for (Step 4-3-2)

(1) Argument from epistemic responsibility

- The simple observational model implies that, in carrying out reviews of one’s reasoning, one

is epistemically responsible not primarily for the thoughts being reviewed but primarily for the

review.

- Accordingly, the model implies that we are in reviewing our reasons only derivatively

responsible for objects of review, not fully and primarily responsible.

(2) Argument from the means of control

- If we knew our attitudes only as empirical objects, then we cannot but face the question of how,

or by what means, to make the reasons (we have discovered for and against our attitudes in

our critical reasoning) effective in view of the contingent relation between the point of view of

the self-knowledge and rational evaluation, on one hand, and the observationally known

attitudes, on the other.

- In critical reasoning, however, such questions of means and control do not arise, since one’s

relation to the known attitudes is rationally immediate: they are part of the perspective of the

review itself.

· Remaining Question

- Burge may have give plausible arguments for his claim that our knowledge of our intentional states must be non-observational and non-empirical.

- But he gives no explanation of how we get such non-observational and non-empirical knowledge of our intentional states.
