Boyle (2009), “Two Kinds of Self-Knowledge”

· Boyle’s Main Question

- How could Moran claim that the kind of self-knowledge he explains in Authority and Estrangement,

namely our knowledge of our own intentional states, is fundamental? If we accept Moran’s account

of self-knowledge, it seems that our knowledge of our own intentional states is merely a different

kind of self-knowledge from our knowledge of our own non-intentional states.

· Boyle’s Answer

- “[T]he power to represent one’s own mental states presupposes the power to know one’s own

deliberative attitudes in the way that Moran specifies.” (Boyle 2009, p.147; emphasis added)

우리가 갖는 어떠한 임의의 믿음 내용 p에 대해서도, “I think that p”라고 말하는 것이 가능해야 한다. (칸트)

· Boyle’s Main Idea in Defense of the Answer

- If order for a person to be able to represent his own mental states of any sorts (including

sensations, perceptions, beliefs, desires, intentions, etc), so that he can think <I am in M> or say

“I am in M” for any M, he must understand that the subject to whom he ascribes those states is

one who has the power to make up her mind so that he must be able to ascribe the belief <I

believe that I am in M> to himself.

(Step 1) In order to genuinely represent (=believe) one’s own mental states, one must understand the content expressed by one’s utterance of the form “I am in M”

(Step 2) In order to understand the content expressed by one’s (declarative) utterance S of any form, including the utterance of the form “I am in M”, one must be able to entertain a certain sort of “Why?”-question about the claim S one makes, a question that asks for grounds that show that S is be true.

(Step 3) Now, if Moran is right about the Transparency Condition, a subject who is capable of holding S true in a way that reflects an appreciation of this sort of “Why?”-questions will also be entitled to ascribe the belief <I believe that S>.

(Step 4) If so, it follows that the kind of self-knowledge that Moran characterizes is fundamental, for the ability to say what one believes in the way Moran specifies (= the ability to find, evaluate reasons for what one believes and to revise one’s beliefs in accordance with such evaluation) is intimately connected with the kinds of representational ability that must be possessed by a subject who can understand the content expressed by one’s utterance S of any form, including the utterance of the form “I am in M”.

(Step 5) Furthermore, in order to understand the content expressed by one’s utterance of the form “I am in M”, in which M is one’s own mental state, one must understand that his use of the term “I” refers to the person who has the power to know one’s own deliberative attitudes in the way that Moran specifies, namely the person whose mind is his to make up.

Byrne (2011), “Transparency, Belief, Intention”

· Byrne’s Main Thesis

- We can develop a unified transparency theory of self-knowledge for all kinds of one’s own mental states, a theory that satifies the following three desiderata:

(1) The account explains why we have privileged access (=epistemic security)to our own mental states.

※ We have (Byrne의 의미에서) privileged access to our own mental states

= Our beliefs about our own mental states are epistemically more secure

= Our beliefs about our own mental states are morely likely to amount to knowledge than our

beliefs about the external world or about other people’s mental states

(2) The account explains why we have peculiar access to our own mental states.

※ We have peculiar access to our own mental states

= We have a distinctive way of knowing about our own mental states that cannot be used to know about the external world or about other people’s mental states.

(3) The account is an economical, not extravagant, theory of self-knolwedge, for it requires only those cognitive capacities that are needed for knowledge of other subject matters.

(No further requirement of capacities for ‘inner sense’ or ‘acquaintance’)

· Transparency Procedure as an Inferential Procedure

- “What exactly is this ‘transparent’ procedure for gaining knowledge of one’s beliefs? Suppose that I examine the evidence and conclude that there will be a third world war. Now what? Evans does not explicitly address this question, but the natural answer is that the next step involves an inference from world to mind: I infer that I believe that there will be a third world war from the single premise that there will be one.” (p.203; italics original)

· The Doxastic Schema and the Puzzle of Transparency

- Byrne agrees with André Gallois that we make a transparent inference about our own mental states in accordance with the following ‘doxastic schema’:

p

-------------------------

I believe that p

지금 비가 오고 있다.

그러므로, 나는 지금 비가 오고 있다고 믿는다.

※ 이한슬 씨 코멘트:

여기서 세계의 사실로서의 <지금 비가 오고 있다>와 나의 심리상태로서의 <나는 지금 비가 오고 있다고 믿는다>는 분명히 서로 다른 범주의 사실들이기는 하지만, 후자의 범주의 사실인 심리 상태의 지향적 내용이 바로 전자의 범주의 사실을 내용으로 갖고 있는 것이기 때문에, 이 점에서 과연 투명성의 역설이 말하는 것처럼 두 범주의 사실이 “완전히 무관한 사실”인지 의문을 제기할 수도 있다.

- The Puzzle of Transparency

(1) “[T]he suggestion that one gains knowledge of one’s beliefs by reasoning in accord with the doxastic schema is plausible on its face.” (p.204)

(2) “Plainly the doxastic schema is neither deductively valid nor inductively strong. . .the transparent inference could hardly be worse, and so the second-order beliefs it yields will not be knowledge.” (p.204)

- In particular, note that an inference in accordance with the doxastic schema apparently yields

knowledge about one’s belief that p even if the premise, p, is false and without any justification.

But “surely an inference from a falsehood – let alone a completely unjustified one! – is not going

to result in knowledge.” (p.XXX)

(e.g.) I will meet a beautiful stranger   [based on the fact that my fortune-teller says so]

I believe that I will meet a beautiful stranger.

전제의 진리치에 관한 Byrne의 논의에 대한 조민수 씨의 해석

여기서 중요한 점은 전제 p가 거짓임에도 결론 <I believe>가 참이라는 점이 아니라, 유일한

전제인 p가 거짓임에도 결론 <I believe>가 참이라는 점이다. 어떤 좋은 추론에서 모든

전제가 거짓이면서도 결론이 늘 참인 것이 도대체 가능한가?

(잠정적 답변) 모든 전제가 거짓이면서도 결론이 참이 경우가 있다. (게티어 사례 중 주머니 동전 사례). 그래서 p가 유일한 전제인데 이 전제가 거짓이라는 점이 결론을 지식으로 만들어주는 것에 특별히 더 위협을 가하는 것 같지는 않다.

· Byrne’s Answer to the Puzzle of Transparency

- An inference in accordance with the doxastic schema is knowledge-conducive because it is

strongly self-verifying and safe.

(1) An inference in accordance with the doxastic schema is strongly self-verifying in the sense that, if one reasons in accordance with the schema and infers that one believes that p from the premise that p, then one’s belief that one believes that p is true because “inference from a premiss[premise] entails belief in that premiss[premise]” (p.206).

(2) Moreover, “such reasoning yields beliefs that are safe in the sense that they could not easily have been false.” (pp.206-207)

Epistemic Safety and Sensitivity

Two modal conditions for knowledge, safety and sensitivity, are widely discussed in contemporary episetmology. Some philosophers have claimed that S’s true belief is knowledge only if it is safe (Sosa, Pritchard, Williamson), while others have claimed that S’s true belief is knowledge only if it is sensitive (Nozick). It is nowadays widely agreed that it is safety that is a necessary condition for knowledge.

Epistemic Safety

- S’s true belief that p is safe [=not with luck]

iff S’s belief that p could not have been easily false.

iff If S were to believe that p, p would not be false.

iff In almost all nearby possible worlds w in which S believes p, p is true.

(e.g.) Suppose Chulsoo believes, without any evidence, on 2021.2.12 that it will rain tomorrow.

Suppose further that it does rain on 2021.2.13, and hence that Chulsoo’s belief happens

to be true. But Chulsoo’s belief is not safe because it could have been easily false.

Epistemic Sensitivity

- S’s true belief that p is sensitive

iff If p were false, S would not believe that p.

iff In almost all nearby possible worlds w in which p is false, S does not believe that p

(e.g.) Suppose Chulsoo believes that he is not a brain in a vat. Although Chulsoo’s belief is true, it is not sensitive because, even If he had been indeed a brain in a vat, he would have still believed that he is not a brain in a vat.

이승수 씨 문제 제기

(1)  “투명성의 퍼즐”은 믿음 도식에 따른 추론의 결론 <I believe p>에 대한 믿음의 정당화와

관련된 문제 제기, 즉 “서로 아예 내용이 다른 두 명제 <p>와 <I believe p>가 도대체 어떻게

정당화 관계를 맺을 수 있단 말인가?”에 관한 퍼즐로 이해되어야 한다.

(2) 반면 “믿음 도식에 따른 추론에서, 결론 <I believe p>를 믿는 것은 전제 <p>가 거짓이어도 지식일 수 있는데, 도대체 거짓인 전제로부터 어떻게 지식이 산출될 수 있는가?”라는 퍼즐은 게티어 문제와 관련된 퍼즐로 정당화와 관련된 “투명성 퍼즐”과 구분되어야 한다.

(3) 안정성(safety) 조건에 호소하는 Alex Byrne의 해결책은 게티어 문제 퍼즐에 대한 답변만 제공하고 있지 (정당화와 관련된 것으로 이해된) 투명성 퍼즐에 대한 답변을 제공하지 못하고 있다.

(4) 그러므로 Byrne의 해결책은 사실 투명성 퍼즐을 해결하지 못하고 있을 뿐 아니라, 아예 이 퍼즐을 다루지도 않고 있다.

번의 입장에서 가능한 답변

(1) “투명성의 퍼즐”은 <p>와 <I believe p>의 내용 연관성과 관련된 퍼즐이지 특별히 둘 사이의 정당화 관계에 대한 퍼즐이 아니다.

※ 번의 이러한 가능한 답변을 강화해주는 박서연 씨의 추가 논증

· Byrne’s Transparency Account of Our Self-Knowledge of Intention

- The basic idea

* It seems that there is a close connection between the intention to  and the belief

that one will . (cf. Anscome (1957)).

* Indeed, philosophers such as Velleman argue that an intention to  is simply a belief that one

will .

* Whether or not these philosophers are right, we may use the close connection between one’s

intention to  and one’s belief that one will  to find a rule of inference in accordance with

which we arrive at knowledge of our own intentions.

- The proposal

We get knowledge of our own intentions by making an inference in accordance with the

following ‘bouletic’ schema:

I will

----------------

I intend to

· Problems for the Bouletic Schema and Byrne’s Solution

- Two problematic cases for the bouletic schema ( Cases of the ‘overgeneration’ problem)

(Case 1) Cases of an estimate of one’s futre

(e.g.) I will fail the exam  [based on the facts that I skipped the lectures and didn’t do the reading]

I intend to fail the exam

(Case 2) Cases of a foreseen but unintended consequences

(e.g.) I will kill the civilians   [based on the fact there are many cilivians living in the factory which

which is the target of the tactical bomber]

I intend to kill the civilians

- Byrne’s Solution

* These cases are those in which one knows what one will do on the basis of evidence but does not ascribe the intention.

* As Anscombe has pointed out, however, in many cases we have knowledge of what we are doing or what we will do “without observation” (Anscombe 1957, p.13). And those present and future actions that can be known without observation are precisely those that one intends to perform.

(e.g.) “I will fail the exam; I’m sick of this school and wants to quit.”

* Therefore, we can propose that the bouletic schema does hold for the cases in which our knowledge of what we will do is “without observation” (Anscombe), i.e. “not resting on evidence” (p.218), although it is defeasible “if one believes that one’s belief that one will  rests on good evidence that one will .” (p.218)

· The Boluetic Schema and Our ‘Privileged’ and ‘Peculiar’ Access to Our Own Intentions

- Privileged access is explained because the bouletic schema is practially strongly self-verifying: normally, if one reasons in accordance with the schema in non-defeated cases, one arrives at a true belief about one’s own intention.

- Peculiar access is explained because getting knowledge about intention in accordance with the bouletic schema is the procedure that works only in one’s own case.

“First, in the case of others, the defeating condition just noted is almost invariably present:

if I believe that Andre will , then I will think that this belief rests on good evidence. And,

second, the step to the conclusion that Andre intends to  will in any case often be

unwarranted – if Andre is going to step on an ant, it is most unlikely that he intends to.”

(p.219)

· Boyle’s Main Theses

(Thesis 1) The nature of belief is such that, normally, when one believes that p one already tacitly knows that one believes that p. One can make this implicit knowledge explicit by reflecting on what one believes.

“This still leaves room for the reflectivist to recognize an important truth underlying the phenomenon of transparency. On his view, the important truth is this: the very same actualization of my cognitive powers that is my believing P is, under another aspect, my tacitly knowing that I believe P. Hence, to pass from believing P to juding I believe P, all I need to do is reflect – i.e. attend to and articulate what I already know.” (p.229)

(Thesis 2) The best way of understanding Moran’s central idea that one can have transparent knowledge of one’s own beliefs because they are expressions of one’s rational agency, i.e. one’s capacity to make up one’s minds, is

(1) not that Moran offers an account of how (1) doxastic self-knowledge and (2) this special way of arriving at beliefs, namely by making up one’s mind, are connected with each other,

(2) but that Moran offers an account of how (normal) doxastic self-knowledge reflects something about what believing is.

※ Moran hasn’t used the term ‘tacit knowledge’ in Authority and Estrangement, and I cannot find textual evidence which suggests that, for Moran, one’s belief that p involves one’s tacit knowledge that one believes that p by the very nature of belief.

※ Boyle’s comments on his interpretation of Moran’s central idea

“I think Moran’s idea – that I can have transparent knowledge of my own beliefs because they are expressions of my rational agency – is best understood as offering this sort of account of our doxastic self-knowledge [= the account that this sort of knowledge reflects something about what believing is]. I do not claim that Moran himself understood his idea in exactly this way, but I have become convinced that his insight takes its best shape in this setting.” (p.235)

(Thesis 3) Byrne’s inferential approach to the transparency of beliefs cannot be right because the ‘inference’ in accordance with the doxastic schema is “mad”. (p.230)

· Boyle’s Interpretation of Moran’s and Byrne’s Transparency Accounts of Knowledge

- “Moran (2001) proposed that the reason why I can normally answer the mind-focused question whether I believe that P ‘transparently’, by reflecting on the world-oriented question whether P, is that my reflection on the latter topic is an exercise of my capacity to ‘make up my mind’, so that my knowledge of my belief concerning P is normally grounded in my capacity actively to determine whether to believe the proposition in question.” (p.124; italics added)

- “Byrne (2011) attacks these claims [made by Boye in defense of Moran] from a direction I did not anticipate. The objectors I was trying to answer generally did not make much of the phenomenon of transparency: They held that the fact that our answers to questions about our own mental states are systematically correlated with our answers to questions about the world at large is, if anything, a by-product of our normal way of knowing our own minds, not the key to an account of this knowledge.” (pp.224-225).

- “According to Byrne, however, precisely this correlation is the basis of our self-knowledge, and he thinks that this holds true across the board. He thus argues that there is good reason to suppose that a uniform account of self-knowledge is possible.” (p.225; italics added)

- “Moreover, he denies that the capacity to make up one’s mind plays an essential role even in the account of our transparent knowledge of our judgment-sensitive attitudes.” (p.225)

· Boyle’s Proposal that Beliefs Already Invoves Tacit Knowledge of Them

- “The reflective approach explains doxastic transparency [. . .] as a matter, not of inferring of a psychological fact from a fact about the world, but of shifting one’s attention from the world with which one is engaged to one’s engagement wth it – an engagement of which one was already tacitly cognizant even when one’s attention was ‘directed outward’.” (p.228)

- “the reflective approach thus does not seek to explain how we acquire doxastic self-knowledge. · Boyle’s Critique of Byrne’s Inferential Approach to Transparency

- In order for an inference to hold, the premises must provide a ground or a reason for the conclusion. But it seems that the premise in the doxastic schema does not provide any such ground or reason.

“To believe that I believe P is to hold it true that I believe P. Being a reflective person, I can ask myself what grounds I have for holding this true. The answer ‘P’ is obviously irrelevant. I am asking what shows that the proposition I believe P is true, and a modicum of rational insight will inform me that, even if it is true that P, this by itself has no tendency to show that I believe it. What would support my conclusion, of course, is the fact that I, the maker of this inference, accept the premise that P. But to represent that as my basis would be presuppose that I already know my own mind on the matter, and that would undermine Byrne’s account.” (p.230; emphasis added)

“A (personal-level) inference is not a mere transition from a stimulus to a response; it is a transition of whose terms I am cognizant, and hose occurrence depends on my – in the normal case: persistenly – taking there to be an intelligible relation between these terms. This is what makes it possible for an inference to leave me with a sustainable belief: I can reflect on why I draw a certain conclusion, and when I do, I can see (what looks to me to be) a reason for it. It is hard to see how the premiss of Byrne’s doxastic schema could supply me with a reaon to draw its conclusion.” (p.231)

※ Boye’s interesting comment on Footnote 8: “Unless the subject thought himself omniscient. But that possibility only reinforces my point: the assumption of omniscience would make this a prima facie reasonable transition, though the assumption itself is deeply unreasonable.” (p.231)

· Comments

- Apart from Boyle’s critique, it should be admitted that an ‘inference’ in accord with the doxastic schema is indeed a very peculiar one.

- In ordinary cases of inference, whether or not the conclusion is true crucially depends on whether or not the premises are true. Also, in order for the conclusion to count as knowledge, it shouldn’t be the case that the premises happen to be true; they must be justified.

- In the case of the doxastic schema, however it is not required that the premise p is true or justified in order for the conclusion <I believe that p> to be knowledge. Even if p is false, and even if p is  unjustified, it is still the case the the conclusion is true and, moreover, amounts to knoweldge!
