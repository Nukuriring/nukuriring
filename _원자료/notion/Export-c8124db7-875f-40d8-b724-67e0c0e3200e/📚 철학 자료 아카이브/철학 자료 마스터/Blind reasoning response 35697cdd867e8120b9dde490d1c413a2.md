Blind but Justifying: Accounting for the Warrant of Inference

How can an inference draw the justified conclusions from justified premises? Boghossian tries to give an answer to the question. He first articulates two conditions for an answer to the question must have: (1) a thinker is justified in believing premises; (2) a thinker’s justification for the conclusions is independent of his justification of the premises. He then turns to one possible answer. Following is a formulated version of the view.

(Simple inferential externalism): (1) + (2) + (3). Implicated patterns of inference are valid. (inference is truth-preserving)

He argues that this cannot be an answer to the question. Because if a thinker is not aware of the reliability of the reliability of the relevant belief-forming method, she is epistemically blame-worthy even though she used the reliable belief-forming method. Boghossian argues that we should consider the state of being epistemically blameless to be a condition for being justified. He now turns to the notion of ‘reflectively transparent justification,’ which he puts in the following form of the formulation:

(Simple Inferential Internalism): (1) + (2) + (3). S is able to know by reflection alone that his premises provide him with a good reason for believing the conclusion.

If S knew that his premises necessitate his conclusions, then we could count S as having a good reason to believe the conclusions. Boghossian gives two possible accounts for how S is able to know this epistemic fact by reflection alone: (i) inferential route and (ii) non-inferential route. He quickly undermines the former option. In this context, we are trying to give an account of how a thinker possibly can be justified in drawing conclusions from his premises via inferences. Therefore, a thinker cannot attain the epistemic fact which enables him to justifiably use inference by ‘inferential route’.

Then he goes for the non-inferential route. The potent candidate for an account of the non-inferential obtainment of a knowledge is introducing the concept of ‘rational insight,’ i.e., we have a special faculty whose exercise is capable of yielding insights into necessary truths. Unfortunately, however, it is quite unintelligible for what kind of relation a thinker holds with the concept when he’s exercising this capacity.

What is more problematic about the notion of rational insight is that even if we accept the notion, it is not clear how it can be helpful for vindicating SII. Rational insight is supposed to explain how a thinker is able to be justified in believing that his inference is valid. To do so, rational insight needs to provide the thinker with the general insight that relevant inference patterns are all valid. For instance,

For all p,q : necessarily : If both p and ‘pq’, then q

If this form of general insight to be helpful for a thinker to be justified in using particular inference, he is required to do following reasoning.

Any inference of the form MPP is valid;

This particular inference is of MPP form;

This particular inference is valid.

However, this reasoning requires the thinker to justifiably use MPP, which is the exactly the thing we are trying to explain. If we are to infer justifiably this particular inference of the form MPP valid, we must be able to infer justifiably according to MPP.

Saying that rational insight provides the thinker with the knowledge of particular inference’s validity does not help. According to the SII, this particular inference is justified just in case the thinker can justifiably believe that his premises provide him with a good reason for drawing the conclusions.

In this case, rational insight provides the thinker with the ability to obtain the knowledge that his premises necessitate the conclusions for a particular case through non-inferential route. However, the thinker is required to infer justifiably according to MPP for this non-inferentially acquired knowledge to make the inference warrant-transferring

The only escape route is to say that appreciating the validity of a particular inference whose premises are known provides a warrant to accept a conclusion directly, rather than inferentially just as we acquire our warrant for the belief that, for example, the sky is blue from recognizing the blueness of the sky. However, we only plausibly assume that warrant is directly given if only, the gap between the content of the observation and the content of the belief is relatively narrow. Say, for example, mere observation of dark and threatening clouds does not justify the belief that it is going to rain because the gap of contents between premises and conclusions are too large to be grounded directly.

Boghossian draws two lessons from above considerations: (i) It must be possible for certain inferences to be blind but justifying. (ii) Accepting SIE to explain warrant-transferring phenomenon will not help.

One possible way is to say that we are justified in using MPP, for instance, because MPP is one of the methods with which we ‘start’ and we have, so far, encountered no incoherence in our overall view to which the best response would have been to reject it or modify it.

This explanation is not convincing because the notion of coherence is empty if the notion of logical consistency and logical consequence is not given already. In other words, the concept of ‘coherence’ presupposes an answer to our question.

Second option is to just say that certain inference patterns are blind but justifying. There is no sense of asking why. No deep answer could be given with regard to that question. Boghossian quickly undermines this option by assuming that the idea of regarding warrant-transferring property as primitive is not plausible.

Boghossian now appeals to concept constitution.

(Appeal to Concept Constitution): If inferring according to a given inference pattern is a precondition for having one of the concepts ingredient to it, a given inference pattern can be employed blamelessly without reflective procedure.

If this is true, in some case, one is not blameworthy for employing a not-justified inference to draw the conclusion because taking that inference as a warrant for believing the conclusion is constitutive of having the concept of the conclusion. In the case of MPP, we can employ MPP blindly if it is constitutive of having the concept conditional that one takes p and ‘pq’ as a reason for believing q.

This line of thought can be formulated as following:

(Meaning-Entitlement Connection or MEC): Any inferential transitions built into the possession conditions for a concept are eo ipso entitling.

However, there seems to be apparent counterexamples for this principle. Some inferential transitions built into the possession condition for (1) an arbitrarily stipulated concept and (2) a racist or abusive concept does not seem so entitling at all. Claiming that further considerations on these kind concepts will defeat the entitling power cannot be an answer, because, in these cases, we do not find any entitling power at all.

If we are to save MEC at all, we need to restrict it to certain concepts which their possession conditions really give out the entitling. For this purpose, any proponent of restricted MEC should give out the systemic way to distinguish whether a particular concept has MEC or not.

Sound scientific concepts are open to question in principle. In other words, on these concepts, we don’t think of their rules corresponding to our possession of concepts as consisting in the proposition saying that concepts are actually instantiated. Rather, we regard their rules for the possession as consisting in conditionalized proposition. If concepts were not stipulated by conditionalized rules, they are structured in such a way that perfectly reasonable questions about their extensions are foreclosed. This sure seems epistemically questionable.

Boghossian makes two claims from this observation: (1) Whenever both conditionalized and unconditionalized versions of a given concept is available, we should choose to use the conditional version; (2) In the case of some concepts, only the unconditionalized version will be available.

He argues that we do not want possession conditions for a concept to foreclose on the possible falsity, and thus, accept the first claim. However, it is clear that for some concepts, we cannot have a conditionalized version of possession conditions. For instance, we cannot conditionalize the possession conditions for the concept of conditional () on the existence of an appropriate truth function. Because we need conditional to conditionalize on anything. It seems clear that some set of basic logical constants would have to be presupposed.

With this in hand, Boghossian tries to answer the initial question: MPP premises provide warrant to the MPP conclusions while being blind because MPP is built into the possession conditions for the conditional, and that is enough for MPP to be entitling for we have no opportunity to construct conditionalized possession conditions for conditional.
