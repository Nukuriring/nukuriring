The Syntactic Distinction between De Dicto and De Re Attitude Sentences

General Idea

Syntactically, de dicto attitude sentence is a sentence in which the singular term is inside the scope of the attitude verb, while de re attitude sentence is an sentence in which a singular term is outside the scope of the attitude verb. 단칭어가 태도 동사 안에 있으면 de dicto 밖에 있으면 de re

Formulations

An attitude sentence S is syntactically de dicto if S contains a singular term inside the scope of the attitude verb.

(e.g.) (1) ‘Smith believes that George Orwell is a writer’ (True)

(2) ‘Smith believes that Eric Blair is a writer’ (False)

An attitude sentence S is syntactically de re if S contains only a pronoun within the scope of an attitude verb that is anaphoric on a singular term which is outside the scope of that verb.

(e.g.) (3) ‘George Orwell is such that Smith believes that he is a writer’

(4) ‘Eric Blair is such that Smith believes that he is a writer’

(5) George Orwell = Eric Blair

As the above examples illustrate, typical attitude attributions are syntactically de dicto attitude sentences.

It is well known that de dicto attitude sentences create Frege’s puzzle, for attitude verbs create opaque contexts so that substitution of one singular term n1 for another singular n2 can bring about the chage in truth-value even if n1 and n2 refer to the same object.

Reporting Smith’s two beliefs in de re attitude sentences cannot capture their difference in truth-values,

for sentences (3) and (4) must have the same truth-value.

Externalism about Mental Content: Introduction

How to Formulate Externalism about Mental Content

Initial formulation:

The content of a mental state is external (or ‘wide’) in that it is determined partly by conditions external to the subject who is in that state.

Question: How we should understand the notion of determination in the formulation?

First proposal: The content of a mental state is determined partly by external conditions iff It is

caused partly by them.

Problem with the first suggestion: It is possible that two mental states are caused by different

2

external conditions but nevertheless have the same mental content, or are the same mental state.

(e.g.) The silent flowing of water in the Han River, rhythmical and poetic, causes Oscar to think that Seoul is a beautiful place. The silent flowing of twater in the Han River, rhythmical and poetic, causes Toscar to think that Seoul is a beautiful place. Their thoughts are caused by different events, but they have exactly the same thought.

(e.g.) Oscar feels pain in the throat while drinking extremely cold water. Toscar feels pain in the throat while drinking extremely cold twater. Their pains are caused by different things, but they feel exactly the same pain.

원인이 다르면 결과도 달라야 하므로 인과적으로 이해하는 것은 어려움

Revised proposal:

The content of a mental state is determined by conditions external to the subject

iff It is not individuated solely on the intrinsic properties of the subject who is in that state. iff It does not supervene on the intrinsic properties of the subject who is in that state.

(Property M supervenes on property P iff Any two individuals x and y who have P must also have M)

The above proposal is widely accepted, so that the determination relation in the thesis of Content Externalism is generally understood in terms of individuation or supervenience relation.

Putnam’s ‘Twin Earth’ Thought Experiment for Meaning Externalism

- Putnam’s thought experiment

(Step 1) There is a planet, the ‘Twin-Earth’, somewhere in the remote region of space, which is exactly like the Earth we inhabit in every respect with only one exception: On the Twin Earth, a certain chemical substance, which happens to have all the superficially observable characteristics of water but have the molecuar structure of XYZ instead of H2O, replaces water everywhere (lakes, rivers, oceans, etc.).

(실제 사례) 다이아몬드(C)와 인조 다이아몬드(ZrO2)

(Step 2) In this situation, the word ‘water’ in English on the Earth refers to water, while the word ‘water’ in the twin English on the Twin Earth refers to difference stuff, which we may call ‘twater’ in order to distinguish it from water. Hence the references of English ‘water’ and the twin English ‘water’ are different. Accordingly, the meanings of English ‘water’ and the twin English ‘water’ must be different, too, for meanings are supposedly what determine references.

(Step 3) Now, suppose that Oscar on the Earth and twin Oscar (=Toscar) on the Twin Earth live in the 1750s, when people had no idea of the underlying molecular structure of substance such as water. Since water and twater look, taste, and feel exactly the same, Oscar and Twin Oscar must be exactly the same, too, with respect to their intrinsic properties when Oscar says, “Water is wet”, and Toscar says, “Water is wet”.

(Step 4) But the meanings of what Oscar means and what Toscar means are differernt, for what Oscar says means that water is wet while what Toscar means is that twater is wet. Hence meanings of their utterances are not individuated solely by (or supervenient on) the intrinc properties of Oscar and Twin Oscar. Therefore, these meanings are external (or ‘wide’).

- When Putnam first introduced this thought experiment in Putnam(1975, he thought that our psychological states are typically ‘narrow’, not ‘wide’, so that he thought that Oscar and Twin Oscar shared their psychologial states.

Burge’s ‘Arthritis’ Thought Experiment for Content Externalism

- Burge’s thought experiment

(Step 1) Let us suppose the following two situations. In the actual situation, Peter thinks that the word ‘arthritis’ means inflammation of the bones in general, while it actually means inflammation of only the bone joints. Feeling pain in his thigh, Peter says to his doctor, “I have arthritis in my thigh.” In this situation, (1) Peter believes that he has arthritis in his thigh, and (2) his belief is false.

(Step 2) Now, let us think of the following counterfactual situation. Nothing has changed with only one exception: In this situation, the word ‘arthritis’ in Peter’s speech community is indeed used to refer to inflammation of bones in general, not just bone joints. We don’t have a word in English that has the same reference, so let us invent a new word, ‘tharthritis’, in order to translate the word ‘arthritis’ used in this counterfactual situation.

(Step 3) We have supposed that nothing has changed with the exception of the above difference.

So Peter feels pain in his thigh and says to his doctor, “I have arthritis in my thigh”. In this situation, (1) Peter believes that he has tharthritis in his thigh, not arthritis, and (2) his belief is true.

(Step 4) But Peter in the actual situation and Peter in the counterfactual situation must be internally exactly the same. For we have supposed that the only difference in these two situations is the difference in Peter’s speech community, and hence nothing has changed with Peter himself.

- With this thought experiment, Burge has forcefully argued that it is not just meanings but also contents of our mental states such as beliefs that are external or ‘wide’, given that they are also not individuated solely by (or supervenient on) the intrinsic properties of the subject who is in those states. Putnam has accepted Burge’s argument.

Distinction between ‘Environmental’ Externalism and ‘Social’ Externalism

It seems that the Putnam-style thought experiments and the Burge-style thought experiments are concerned with different kinds of “conditions external to the subject”.

Putnam  objects and properties in the external world

Burge  the external speech community to which the subject belongs and their language uses.

In order to bring out this difference more clearly, Content Externalism based on the Putnam-style

thought experiments is often called ‘Environmental Externalism’, while Content Externalism based on the Burge-style thought experiments is often called ‘Social Externalism’ (※ Burge prefers the term ‘Anti-individualism’ because he thinks that this term can cover both ‘Environmental’ Externalism and

‘Social’ Externalism).

The Putnam-style thought experiments focus on the meanings and concepts expressed by natural kind terms such as ‘water’, while the Burge-style thought experiments can be set up for whatever expressions that are incompletely understood by the subject.

( 예) ‘elm’(너도밤나무) ‘beech’(느릅나무)

(e.g.) In the actual situation, Peter is a competent speaker of English whose understanding of the word ‘sofa’ is not terribly wrong. But he thinks erroneously that “sufficiently broad (but single-seat) overstuffed armchairs are sofas” (Burge 1979, p.80)”, so that he says to his wife, “that is a nice sofa!”, when he has discovered a nice single-seat armchair in

the department store. In this situation, Peter believes that it is a nice sofa, and his belief is false.

In the counterfactual situation, Peter’s speech community uses the word ‘sofa’ to cover both sofas and single-seat armchairs. Let us translate the word ‘sofa’ in this counterfactual situation as ‘tofa’ in English. In this situation, Peter believes that it is is a nice tofa, and his belief is true.

(e.g.) Peter’s utterance in the actual situation, “The grass is blue”, means that the grass is blue, which is false.

Peter’s utterance in the counterfactual situation, “The grass is blue”, means that the grass is tblue, which is true.

- Therefore, if we can establish Content Externalism based on the Burge-style thought experiments, it follows that virtually all contents of our mental states are wide, in that they must be individuated partly by how the speech community external to the subject uses the words that express the concepts occurring in those contents.

Distinction between ‘Wide Content’ and ‘Narrow Content’?

A majority of philosophers nowadays follow Putnam and Burge in accepting that the contents of

many, if not all, mental states are wide.

But there are many philosophers who think that there are also contents that are ‘narrow’, namely the contents that are individuated solely by (or supervenient on) intrinsic properties of the subject.

Question: What is narrow content?

First proposal: It is a sort of phenomenal content. For example, the narrow content of Oscar’s belief that water is wet can be expressed as: “the stuff that is transparent, colorless, odorless, quenches thirst, fills lakes and rivers, etc. is wet”

※ For the sake of convenience, let us use the expression ‘the watery stuff’ for ‘the stuff that is

transparent, colorless, odorless, quenches thirst, fills lakes and rivers, etc. is wet’

Problems with the first proposal:

(Problem 1) We may use the Burge-style arguments to show that even phenomenal contents are wide, for they are composed of concepts that can be expressed by words used by the subject’s speech community.

(Problem 2) Apart from the Burge-style arguments, it is questionable whether we have anything like phenomenal content when we have beliefs whose contents can be expressed using indexicals and proper names. Since Kripke’s and Kaplan’s forceful arguments, many philosophers accept that indexicals and proper names are directly referential, namely that they have their references directly and do not have Fregean senses. (In fact, many philosophers also accept Kripke’s proposal that natural kind terms do not have Fregean senses, either.)

(e.g.) The narrow content of Oscar’s belief that water is wet.  THE WATERY STUFF IS WET

The narrow content of Oscar’s belief that it is wet.  ???

The narrow content of Oscar’s belief that Richard Feynman is a physicist.  ???

Fodor’s proposal in Fodor (1987), Psychosemantics

Narrow contents are functions from environments to wide contents. Oscar and Toscar, for example, clearly share something, although it is impossible to specify what this is, which when plugged into the Earth environment yield the wide content that water is wet, while yielding the wide the content that twater is wet when plugged into the Twin-Earth environment.

Criticism of Fodor’s proposal (Stalnaker (1989), “On What’s In the Head”; Block (1991), “What

Narrow Content Is Not)

Content must be something that can be true or false. But what Fodor calls ‘narrow content’, i.e. functions from environments to wide contents, cannot have truth-values. Hence they are not contents in any recognizable sense.

(e.g.) No one would think that a neurophysiological structure is the content of a mental state.

Two recent proposals

Narrow content as ‘primary intension’ in two-dimensional semantics? (Chalmers, Jackson)

The phenomenal intentional theory of narrow content? (Horgan & Tienson, Loar, Kriegel)

Self-Knowledge and the Idea of Narrow Content

If there is no such thing as ‘narrow content’, then one way to reconcile self-knowledge with content externalism is blocked, namely the proposal that we have privileged access to the narrow contents of our own mental states even though we don’t have such privileged access to wide contents.

Davidson (1987). “Knowing One’s Own Mind”

Davidson’s ‘Intepretism’ about Mental Content and the Problem of Self-Interpretation

데이빗슨은 의미와 심적 내용에 대한 콰인의 회의적 입장을 공유하고 있다.

그래서 데이빗슨에게 있어서 의미와 심적 내용은 모두 과학적 관점에서 보면 존재하는 존재자가 아니다(의미와 내용 반실재론)

Davidson’s Interpretism: For a subject S to have the belief that p is for that belief to be part of the best (= most coherent, maximally true, etc.) interpretive scheme of S’s total system of propositional attitudes including beliefs, desires, and the rest. Other than this, there is no further fact of the matter about whether S believes that p.

Question: According to Davidson’s Interpretism, one cannot attribute beliefs to a subject S without interpreting S’s behaviors in which one tries to maximize agreement between S’s beliefs and one’s beliefs. But then how can one attribute beliefs to oneself? How is self-interpretation possible? This question arises for Davidson’s philosophy of language and philosophy of mind independently of the issues about Putnam’s and Burge’s content externalism.

Putnam and Davidson on Psychological States

Putnam (1975) claims that no states can satisfy both of the following conditions (I) and (II)

Intentional psychological states are ‘inner’ in the sense that they do not presuppose the existence of any individual other than the subject to whom the state is ascribed.

Intentional psychological states are normally identified and individuated in terms partly of relations to outside objects and events.

On the other hand, Davidson believes that our intentional psychological states satisfy both (I) and (II).

“The thesis of this paper is that there is no reason to suppose that ordinary mental states do not satisfy

both conditions (I) and (II): I think such states are ‘inner’, in the sense of being identical with states of the body, and so identifiable without reference to objects or events outside the body; they are at the same time ‘non-individualist’ in the sense that they can be, and usually are, identified in part by their causal relations to events and objects outside the subject whose states they are.” (p.444)

Davidson’s Distinction between Descriptions of Psychological States and Psychological States Themselves

“It should be clear that it doesn’t follow, simply from the fact that meanings [and psychological states] are identified in part by relations to objects outside the head, that meanings aren’t in the head. To suppose this would be as bad as to argue that because my being sunburned presupposes the existence of the sun, my sunburn isn’t a condition of my skin. My sunburned skin may be indistinguishable from someone else’s skin that achieved its burn by other means (our skins may be identical in ‘the necktie sense’), yet one of us is really sunburned and the other not. This is enough to show that an appreciation of the external factors that enter into our common ways of identifying mental states does not discredit an identity theory of the mental and the physical.” (pp.451-452).

Comments:

(Comment 1) Davidson seems to be right in criticizing Putnam that meanings and mental states themselves are in the head even if we individuate them by objects and events in the world in our descriptions of them. In other words, Davidson seems to be right that, ontologically, externalism about mental content does not imply that mental states are not in the head.

(Comment 2) But the real issue is about our knowledge of our own mental states, not ontological issues. And the traditional idea that we have privileged access to our own mental states involves the idea that we know them as they are described in our ordinary attitude attributions, namely as states with certain specific contents. For example, no one would claim that we have privileged access to our own mental states as they are described in neurophysiological terms, although each mental state is (token) identical with some neurophysiological state according to Davidson’s Anomalous Monism.

Davidson is also aware that he hasn’t yet solved the epistemological problem

“One obstacle to non-evidential knowledge of our own ordinary propositional attitudes has now been removed. For if ordinary beliefs and the other attitudes can be ‘in the head’ even though they are identified as the attitudes they are partly in terms of what is not in the head, then the threat to first person authority cannot come simply from the fact that external factors are relevant to the identification of the attitudes. . . But an apparent difficulty remains” (p.453)

Davidson’s Rejection of the Object-Grasping Model of Self-Knowledge

“There is a picture of the mind which has become so ingrained in our philosophical tradition that it is almost impossible to escape its influence even when its worst faults are recognized and repudiated.I am not now concerned with such (now largely disavowed) objects of the mind as

sense-data, but with their judgmental cousins, the supposed objects of the propositional attitudes, whether thought of as propositions, tokens of propositions, representations, or fragments of

‘mentalese’. The central idea I wish to attack is that these are entities that the mind can ‘entertain’, ‘grasp’, ‘have before it’, or be ‘acquainted’ with.” (pp.453-454)

Comments: How can Davidson’s rejection of the object-grasping model help us in justifying our privileged access to our own mental states? It is clear that Davidson would reject acquaintance or inner-sense theories, but what is his positive theory?

Davidson’s Solution(?)

“When we have freed ourselves from the assumption that thoughts must have mysterious objects, . . .it also opens the way to an explanation of first person authority. The explanation comes with the realization that what a person’s words mean depends in the most basic cases on the kinds of objects and events that have caused the person to hold the words to be applicable; similarly for what the person’s thoughts are about. An interpreter of another’s words and thoughts must depend on scattered information, fortunate training, and imaginative surmise in coming to understand the other. The agent herself, however, is not in a position to wonder whether she is generally using her own words to apply to the right objects and events, since whatever she regularly does apply them to gives her words the meaning they have and her thoughts the contents they have. Of course, in any particular case, she may be wrong in what she believes about the world; what is impossible is that she should be wrong most of the time. The reason is apparent: unless there is a presumption that the speaker knows what she means, i.e., is getting her own language right, there would be nothing for an interpret to interpret.” (pp.455-456; italics and bolds are added)

Comments

(Comment 1) Does Davidson give a kind of transcendental argument that we must have self- knowledge?

(Step 1) We have languages.

(Step 2) In order for have languages, it must be possible for an interpreter to interpret them. (Step 3) In order for an interpreter to interpret language, she must know what she means and

what she thinks when she speaks or thinks using her own language.

(Step 4) Therefore, we must know what we think (as long as they can be expressed in our own language.)

(Comment 2) Even if the above argument is right, Davidson has not shown how we come to know our own thoughts. It seems that he is entirely silent about the mechanism of our acquisition of self-knowledge.

Burge (1988). “Individualism and Self-Knowledge”

Burge’s Strategy for the Compatibility between Externalism and Self-Knowledge

Burge’s Strategy: In general, one can have knowledge without knowing the enabling conditions that make such knowledge possible. Likewise, In the case of self-knowledge, we can know that we are

thinking that p without knowing the enabling conditions that make such self-knowledge possible. “. . .[T]o think that water is a liquid, one need not know the complex conditions that must obtain if one is to think that thought. Such conditions need only be presupposed.” (p.654)

Burge on the Distinction between Perceptual Knowledge and Self-Knowledge

The relation between our perceptions and their objects (= physical entities) are contingent. Also perceptual knowledge is impersonal.

On the other hand, the ‘object’ or the subject matter of one’s (reflective) thought is not contingently related to the thoughts one thinks about it.

“The (reflective) thoughts are self-referential and self-verifying. An error based on a gap between one’s thoughts and the subject matter is simply not possible in these cases.” (p.658)

(e.g.) Descartes comes to think that he thinks that water is wet.

반성적 생각이 자기-지칭적이다 = 반성적 생각을 할 때, 그 반성적 생각은 필연적으로 그 반성적 생각의 대상을 지칭할 수밖에 없다. 그러므로 그 대상은 반드시 존재한다.

반성적 생각의 대상은, 지각의 대상과 다르게 반성적 생각의 부분을 이루고 있다.

우리가 반성적 생각을 할 때, 그러한 생각을 함으로써 우리가 동시에 반드시 그 생각의 부분을 지칭하고 있다는 의미에서 반성적 생각은 자기지칭적이다.

반성적 생각을 할 때, 그 반성적 생각의 대상은 존재하지 않을 수 없다. 그러므로 그 반성적 생각은 참이 될 수밖에 없다.(오류불가능!)

Also, the paradigmatic cases of self-knowledge are essentially personal.

Given these ‘direct’ features of our self-knowledge, it is all the more implausible to think that (basic) self-knowledge requires, as a precondition, knowledge of the conditions that make such knowledge possible.

Burge on the Impossibility of ‘Brute Mistakes’ for Self-Knowledge

Brute mistakes = cognitive mistakes that do not result from any sort of carelessness, malfunction, or irrationality on the subject.

(e.g.) our cognitive mistake in the Müller-Lyer illusion

“When I judge: I am thinking that writing requires concentration, the cognitive content that I am making judgment about is self-referentially fixed by the judgment itself; and the judgment is self- verifying. There is a range of cases of self-knowledge which extend out from this paradigm. I think that, in all cases of authoritative knowledge, brute mistakes are impossible. All errors in matters where people have special authority about themselves are errors which indicate something wrong with the thinker.” (p.658; italics added)

Burge on the ‘Slow Switching’ Scenario (pp.652-653)

The Scenario

Suppose a person has underwent a series of switches between actual earth and actual twin earth so that one remains in each situation long enough to acquire concepts and perceptions appropriate to that situation. Suppose further that these switches have been carried out in such a way that one is not aware that they have been occurring. (e.g. One goes to sleep one night at home and wakes up in twin home in twin bed). In this situation, what would the content of one’s thought when one speaks on the actual twin earth, after decades on such switches, “The water is wet.”

Question: “How can one individuate one’s thoughts when one has not, by empirical methods, discriminated the empirical conditions that determine those thoughts from empirical conditions that would determine other thoughts?” (p.653; italics added)

McKinsey (1991), “Anti-Individualism and Privileged Access”

McKinsey’s Initial Interpretation of Burge’s Compatibilism

According to McKinsey, Burge would maintain that the following three principles are consistent:

Oscar knows a priori that he is thinking that water is wet.

The proposition that Oscar is thinking that water is wet necessarily depends upon E.

※ Burge (1988):

“Let us turn from knowledge of one’s thoughts to individuation of one’s thoughts. Question: How should we understanding the relation of ‘necessary dependence’ in (2)?

McKinsey’s Critique of the Understanding of ‘Necessary Dependence’ as Metaphysical Entailment

One way to understand the ‘necessary dependence’ in (2) is to understand it as metaphysical entailment, so that the following three principles are consistent:

(1) Oscar knows a priori that he is thinking that water is wet.

(2a) The proposition that Oscar is thinking that water is wet metaphysically entails E.

(3) The proposition E cannot be known a priori, but only by empirical investigation.

According to McKinsey, however, this understanding cannot be what Putnam and Burge have in mind. For it would have an absurd consequence that every psychological state is wide (including the state, for example, that one feels a pain).

McKinsey’s Refined Interpretation of Burge’s Compatibilism

Because of the above problem, we must take Burge to maintain that the following three principles are consistent:

(1) Oscar knows a priori that he is thinking that water is wet.

(2b) The proposition that Oscar is thinking that water is wet conceptually (or logically) implies E.

※ A proposition p conceptually implies a proposition q iff there is a correct deduction of q from p, a deduction whose only premises other than p are necessary or conceptual truths that are knowable a priori, and each of whose steps follows from previous lines by a self-evident inference rule of some adequate system of natural deduction. (p.14)

(3) The proposition E cannot be known a priori, but only by empirical investigation.

McKinsey’s Argument that (1), (2b), and (3) Are Inconsistent

From (1) and (2b), it follows that Oscar can know a priori the proposition E. But this is inconsistent with (3).

Brueckner (1992), “What an Anti-Individualist Knows A Priori”

Brueckner’s Main Thesis

McKinsey’s criticism of Burge is based on misunderstanding.

“When it comes to questions about what is conceptually implied by one’s thinking that water is wet (or necessiated by such thinkings in a manner knowable a priori), Burge is rightly cautious. This is because such questions concern the possibility of a Kantian transcendental argument against scepticism proceeding from the assumption of anti-individualism about content.” (p.114)

Questions

Even if Brueckner’s has given a good argument for his main thesis, the question still remains what Burge had in mind when he was talking about the ‘necessary dependence’ between our thoughts and external conditions.

Furthermore, if Burge had in mind some kind of metaphysical necessity, not conceptual or logical necessity, the question remain how he would answer McKinsey’s challenge that it would trivialize the thesis of content externalism because it would make every mental state wide in a trivial sense.

Therefore, regardless of whether McKinsey has misunderstood Burge or not, the question of whether content externalism in an interesting sense is consistent with self-knowledge remains a problem.
