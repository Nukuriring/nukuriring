Burge on the Distinction among Warrant, Justification and Entitlement

- According to Burge, justification and entitlement are two different types of warrant. Justification is warrant through a reason, whereas entitlement is warrant without a reason. Burge thinks that both justification and entitlement are evidential. (There is no such thing as non-evidential warrant).

(1) S’s belief that p is warranted iff S has a good and reliable route to the truth of S’s belief that p.

(2) S’s belief that p is justified  iff S’s belief that p is warranted through a reason.

※ a reason = a propositional content that can serve as an answer to ‘why’ questions.

(3) S’s belief that p is entitled  iff S’s belief that p is warranted without a reason.

- Burge’s main motives for introducing the idea of entitlement are (i) to explain how non-human animals and children can have knowledge without attributing to them capacities for reasoning, and (ii) to explain our perceptual knowledge without supposing that our perceptual experience has propositional or conceptual content. (※ Burge thinks that our perceptual experience has only non-conceptual content).

Wright on the Distinction among Warrant, Justification, and Entitlement (Wright 2004)

- Like Burge, Wright thinks that justification and entitlement are two different types of warrant. Unlike Burge, however, Wright thinks that justification is evidential while entitlement is non-evidential: Justification is warrant that is based on empirical or a priori evidence, whereas entitlement is warrant that is not based on empirical or a priori evidence.

- Wright’s main motive for introducing the idea of entitlement is to answer epistemic skepticism. He claims that we are (defeasibly) entitled to accept (but not believe!) what he calls ‘cornerstone’ propositions.

※ A proposition p is a cornerstone proposition for a domain D iff we could not rationally claim warrant

for belief in any D-proposition unless we are entitled to accept p.

Compatibilism vs. Incompatibilism about Content Externalism and Self-Knowledge

- Two Theses

(EXT) Mental content is individuated partly by some external condition.

(SK) A subject can know without empirical investigation (or ‘a priori’) the content of her thought.

- Compatibilism: “(EXT) and (SK) are compatible with each other.”

- Incompatibilism: “(EXT) and (SK) are incompatible with each other.”

McKinsey’s Original Reductio Ad Absurdum Argument (McKinsey 1991)

(Step 1) Let us suppose that both (EXT) and (SK) are true. Then we get the following:

(1) Oscar knows a priori that he is thinking that water is wet. (from SK)

(2) That Oscar is thinking that water is wet conceptually implies some external condition E. (from EXT)

(Step 2) From (1) and (2), we get the following:

(Absurdum) Oscar can know a priori that the external condition E holds.

(Step 3) But (Absurdum) is clearly an absurd conclusion, for it is incompatible with the following obvious truth:

(3) Oscar cannot a priori that the external condition E holds.

Boghossian’s Reductio Argument (Boghossian 1997)

(Step 1) Let us suppose that both (EXT) and (SK) are true. Then we get the following:

(1*) Oscar knows a priori that he has the concept water. (from SK)

(2*) Oscar knows a priori that, if he has the concept water, then water exists. (from EXT)

(보고시안은 좀 더 복잡하게 정식화:

(2**) Oscar knows a priori that, if he has the concept water, then either water exist or there are community members who have the concept water.)

(Step 2) From (1*) and (2*), we get the following:

(C*) Oscar knows a priori that water exists.

(Step 3) But (C*) is clearly an absurd conclusion, for it is incompatible with the following obvious truth:

(3*) Oscar cannot know a priori that water exists.

Main Challenge for Boghossian’s Revised Reductio Argument

- Why should we accept (2*)? Can we really know (2*) a priori?

“If we can be said to know that water is required for water, we know that only by virtue of our knowledge that ‘water’ does name a natural kind, namely, water. And that, of course, is something that we could only have come to know empirically.” (p.169)

- Boghossian’s consideration of the ‘Dry Earth’ case

“What concept, if any, should a Twin Earth externalist say would be expressed by tokens of the word ‘water’ on this Dry Earth?” (p.170)

- First option

The word ‘water’ expresses a compound concept the watery stuff (=the stuff that is transparent, clear, quenches thirst, . . . ) on Dry Earth, not an atomic concept such as water.

- Difficulty for the first option

How can the word ‘water’ express different kinds of concepts depending on the changes of environments?

- Second option

The word ‘water’ expresses a certain atomic concept on Dry Earth.

- Difficulty for the second option

Given that there is no natural kind denoted by ‘water’ on Dry Earth, there is no fact of the matter what the referent of ‘water’ is. If so, however, then ‘water’ cannot express any atomic concept, for the concept expressed by ‘water’ is supposed to determine its extension but there can be nothing that determines the extension of ‘water’ given that ‘water’ does not have an extension on Dry Earth.

여기서 보고시안이 지적하는 것은, 고유명사에 대한 밀주의자 또는 직접지칭론자가 과연 아무런 대상도 가리키지 않은 고유명사의 의미를 확보할 수 있는냐는 문제 (‘empty  term’의 문제)를 자연종 용어에 대해 제기한 것임.

- Boghossian’s conclusion

We can know a priori that, in the case of Dry Earth in which no watery stuff exists and hence no natural kind denoted by ‘water’ exists, ‘water’ expresses no concept at all so that we cannot have thoughts whose contents involve the concept expressed by ‘water’. It thus follow that, given content externalism, we can know a priori that if we have the concept water then water exists.

The Incompatibilist Argument from Slow Switching Based on Memory (Boghossian 1989)

- Let us suppose that the concept expressed by Oscar’s use of the expression ‘aluminum’ at t1 denotes aluminum and that, because of a slow switching, the concept expressed by Oscar’s use of the expression ‘aluminum’ at a later time, t2, denotes twaluminum. Let us also suppose that, at t1, Oscar believes I am thinking that aluminum is a light metal.

(1) If Oscar has not forgotten anything, then whatever Oscar knew at t1, he knows at t2.

[platitude about memory and knowledge]

(2) Oscar has not forgotten anything.    [stipulation]

(3) Oscar does not know at t2 I was thinking that aluminum was a light metal, for “to know what he thought at t1 he must discover what environment he was in at that time and how long he had been there.” (Boghossian 1989, p.23)    [from the slow switching scenario]

(4) Therefore, Oscar did not know at t1 I am thinking that aluminum is a light metal.

[from (1), (2), and (3)]

Burge’s Response: We Should Reject (3)!

- “[I]f S has forgotten nothing, I see no reason to think that S will not know (in the relevant sense) at time t2 what he knew at t1.” (p.92))

- Burge’s distinction between preservative memory and substantive memory

“The memory need not be about a past event or content at all. It can simply link the past thought to the present, by preserving it. Such cases involve a particular type and function of memory – preservative memory – which preserves propositional contents and attitudes toward them, rather than referring to objects, attitudes, contents, images, or events. The memory content is fixed by the content of the thinking it recalls” (p.93)

- Burge’s distinction between preservation of self-knowledge and discrimination(and identification) of self-knowledge

“Boghossian is asking the wrong question here. . .The knowledge to be preserved did not have the form that Boghossian’s question implies, something like: I am thinking that aluminum is a light metal; and the thought just thought is an aluminum thought as distinct from a twaluminum thought. S did not think a thought that looked on a content from the outside and opened itself to questions of comparative discrimination. . .The right question is whether knowledge first expressed by I am thinking that aluminum is a light metal can be preserved.” (p.96)

Wright’s Distinction between Warrant Closure and Warrant Transmission

- (Warrant Closure; WC)

If S has a warrant for believing p and a warrant for believing p entails q (and S also recognizes that q follows deductively from these beliefs), then S has a warrant for accepting q. (pp.140-141)

- (Warrant Transmission; WT)

If S has a warrant for believing p and a warrant for believing p entails q (and S also recognizes that q follows deductively from these beliefs), then these warrants are transmittable to S’s acceptance of q so that S acquires (perhaps for the first time) a warrant for accepting q from those warrants. (pp.140-141)

- Wright thinks that we have to distinguish between (Warrant Closure) and (Warrant Transmission). He thinks that (Warrant Closure) is a plausible principle but that that there are counter-examples to (Warrant Transmission).

Suppose S is warranted in believing that p and warranted in believing that p entails p. S recognizes that p follows deductively from these two beliefs, and hence S concludes that p. In this case, S has not acquired a warrant in believing the conclusion, p, from the warrants for the belief that p and the belief that p entails p, because in this case “there was warrant for the premises in the first place only because the conclusion was antecedently warranted.” (p.141).

Hence (Warrant Transmission) fails. But (Warrant Closure) holds, for if S is warranted in believing that p and warranted in believing that p entails p, then S is surely warranted in believing that p.

- In order for (Warrant Transmission) to hold, the warrant for the conclusion must come from the warrants for its premises, not the other way around.

Wright’s Basic Idea for Criticizing McKinsey’s Reductio Ad Absurdm Argument

- Wright thinks that the McKinsey case is precisely the one in which (Warrant Transmission) fails, for the warrant for its conclusion, (3), cannot come from the warrants for its premises, (1) and (2).

(1) I believe that water is wet.      p

(2) If I believe that water is wet, then I have had such-and-such encounters with water.  pq

(3) I have had such-and-such encounters with water.     Therefore, q

- Question: Why does (Warrant Transmission) fails in the McKinsey case?

Martin Davies’s Proposal for Why (Warrant Transmission) Fails in the McKinsey Case

- Davies’s general principle (PREP):

Every epistemic project incorporates certain presuppositions which, whatever its upshot, it [=the epistemic project] cannot provide warrant for.

- An instance of (PREP)  Martin Davies’s principle (D)

Warrant cannot be transmitted across a known entailment if the truth of the consequent is a precondition of the subject’s capacity to believe the antecedent.

- According to Davies, (3) is a precondition of (1), so warrant for (1) cannot be transmitted to (3).

- Wright’s question: How should we understand the notion of presupposition or precondition here? It cannot simply mean the necessary condition. For example, the following argument does not seem question-begging at all, so that the warrant for (Premise) is transmittable to (Conclusion), although the conclusion is indeed a necessary condition for the premise to hold.

(Premise) I believe that water is wet.

(Conclusion) Therefore, someone is capable of believing something.

Wright’s Proposal

- Proposal: If it is possible for us to have a warrant for (1) at all, we must be independently warranted in accepting (3). (3) is a precondition for (1) in this sense, and this is why no warrant for (1) is transmittable to (3).

(1) I believe that water is wet.

(2) If I believe that water is wet, then I have had such-and-such encounters with water.

(3) I have had such-and-such encounters with water.

- Wright’s Argument for the Proposal

(Step 1) Let us think of any argument in the following form:

(1) A

(2) A entails B

(3) Therefore, B

(Step 2) Because of the possibility of illusion, there is always a proposition C which

(i) is subjectively indistinguishable from A so that any warrant for A could be an warrant for C;

(ii) is incompatible with A;

(iii) entails B

(Step 3) Therefore, if it is possible for me to have an warrant for A and not merely an warrant for a disjunction A or C, I must be independently warranted in discounting C.

(Step 4) In order for me to be independently warranted in discounting C, “I have to be entitled to discount the negation of B [=B], and therefore entitled to accept B.” (p.155).

entitlement = default, non-evidential warrant that can be defeated only under a special circumstance.

(Step 5) “So it would seem that I must have an appreciable entitlement to affirm B already, independent of the recognition of its entailment by A, if I am to claim to be warranted in accepting A in the first place.” (p.155)

(Step 6) Therefore, my warrant for A is not transmittable to B, for I am independently warranted in accepting B and this warrant for B is a precondition for my being able to have any warrant for A.

Application to the Perceptual Case

- A = <These animals are zebras>

B = <These animals are not mules disguised as zebras>

C = <These animals are mules disguised as zebras>

(1) These animals are zebras.

(2) If these animals are zebras, then they are not mules disguised as zebras.

(3) These animals are not mules disguised as zebras.

- A and C are subjectively indistinguishable, so that any warrant we can have for believing A could also be an warrant for C. [The Possibility of Perceptual Illusion]

- But we are independently warranted in discounting C because, according to Wright, we are entitled to do so in ordinary circumstances.

- Given that we are entitled to discount C, we are also entitled to discount one of its consequences, namely B, and hence are entitled to affirm B.

- Therefore, (i) we have an warrant (in the form of entitlement) for B independently of our warrant for A and, moreover, (ii) this independent warrant for B in the form of entitlement is a precondition for our being able to have any warrant for A at all.

- Therefore, no warrant for (1)(=A) can be transmitted to (3)(=B)

Application of the “Failure of Warrant Transmission” Argument to Putnam’s Proof?

- It seems that we can apply Wright’s argument for the failure of warrant transmission to Putnam’s famous proof that we are not brains-in-a-vat.

A = <I have the concept of what it would be to be a brain-in-a-vat>

B = <I am not a brain-in-a-vat>

C = <The seeming thoughts which I attempt to express by tokenings of ‘brain-in-a-vat’ are content-defective owing the reference failure of the term ‘brain-in-a-vat’ in my language.>

(1) I have the concept of what it would be to be a brain-in-a-vat

(2) No such brain-in-a-vat can have that concept.

(3) Therefore, I am not a brain-in-a-vat.

- However, Wright thinks that there is an importance difference between McKinsey’s paradox and Putnam’s proof, so that we cannot apply his argument to Putnam’s proof.

- According to Wright, no content illusion takes place in Putnam’s scenario. For Putnam assumes that the brains-in-a-vat can genuinely think so that they do have the concept brain-in-a-vat, although this concept is different from our concept brain-in-a-vat. (Let us denote the former by ‘brain-in-a-vat*’.)

- Question: How can this difference show that there is no failure of warrant transmission in Putnam’s proof?

- Wright’s answer: It is a thinkable possibility that one’s token thoughts involving ‘water’ have no content, so we can discount such possibility. In Putnam’s proof, however, it is not a thinkable possibility that one’s token thoughts involving ‘brain-in-a-vat’ token the concept brain-in-a-vat* rather than the concept brain-in-a-vat. Hence we cannot even formulate the disjunction A or C, let alone discount C.
