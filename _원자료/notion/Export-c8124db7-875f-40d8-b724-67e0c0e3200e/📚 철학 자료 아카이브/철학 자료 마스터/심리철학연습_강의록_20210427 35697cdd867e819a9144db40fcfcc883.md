- (Step 3) Now, it is normally the case for the cognitive subject who is a rational agent that, when she is addressing the question of whether something is the case or not, the facts she considers can provide her with the (justifying) reason for believing that something is the case.

- (Step 4) Therefore, it is normally the case for the cognitive subject who is a rational agent that, when she is addressing the question of whether she believes that p, the facts she considers can provide her with the (justifying) reason for believing that she believes that p.

- (Step 5) But it is normally the case for the cognitive subject who is a rational agent, that considerations of facts about X also provide her with a reason for believing that p, in which p is about X.

(e.g.) The fact that it is dark and cloudy outside, as well as the fact that people outside the window are holding umbrellas, provide Younghee with a reason for believing that it is raining.

- (Step 6) Therefore, it turns out that, for a cognitive subject who is a rational agent and who is addressing the question of whether she believes that p, her reason for believing that she believes that p is none other than her reason for believing that p.

- (Step 7) Therefore, we can conclude that it is normally the case for the cognitive subject S who is a rational agent and addressing the question of whether she believes that p, that S will have a reason for believing that she believes that p if S has a reason for believing that p, as long as S’s belief that p is “transparent”.

- (Step 8) Since S is a rational agent, it is normally the case that S can indeed come to know a reason for believing that p, in which p is about X, by considering facts about X.

- (Step 9) Since this reason is also a reason for believeing that she believes that p, S can come to know a reason for believing that she believes that p. Accordingly, S’s belief that she believes that p is based on a reason.

- (Step 10) If S is a cognitive subject who is a rational agent, it is normally be the case that S will believe that something is the case if S is addressing the question of whether something is the case and S has a reason for believing that something is indeed the case.

- (Step 11) Therefore, S will believe that she believes that p.

- (Step 12) By supposition, S believes that p. Hence S’s belief that she believes that p is true.

- (Step 13) Therefore, it follows that (i) S has a reason for beliving that she believes that p, that (ii) S does believe that she believes that p, and (iii) S’s belief that she believes that p is true. Hence S’s belief that she believes that p amounts to knowledge.

- (Step 14) Also, this knowledge is non-observational and non-empirical, for S’s reason for believing that she believes p is just S’s reason for believing that p, and it is normally the case that the rational agent does have a reason for believing that p when she believes that p. Hence the rational agent needs no separate observational or empirical reason for believeing that she believes that p in order to know that she believes that p.

※ Moran is not arguing that, for any p, a rational agent who believes that p thereby knows that she believes that p. · Question

- Why does the Transprency Condition hold for the rational agent? In other words, why is it normally the case that the rational agent not merely attributes her intentional states to herself but avows them?

※ “An avowal is a statement of one’s belief which obeys the Trasparency Condition”. (p.101; italics added)

※ “A statement of one’s belief about X is said to obey the Transparency Condition when the statement is made by consideration of the facts about X itself, and not by either an ‘inward consideration of the facts about X itself, and not by either an ‘inward glance’ or by observation of one’s own behavior.” (p.101; italics added)

· Moran’s Answer:

- The Transparency Condition holds for beliefs of the rational agent because, for the rational agent

treats her own beliefs (and other intentional states) not only as objects of knowledge but also as

the results of exercising her rational agency in her thinkings and actions.

- In exercising her rational agency, the rational agent is seeking to have beliefs (desires, intentions) that she ought to have, namely those beliefs (desires, intentions) that are true (desirable).

- Therefore, those beliefs that the rational agent actually come to have as a result of exercising her rational agency will be what she take as true.

- Therefore, for the rational agent, the question of which beliefs she has is inseparably related to the question of which belifs are true (or the question of which beliefs she ought to have)

- This is why the rational agent can answer the question of whether she believes that p only by considering the question of whether p is true without considering anything else (including her inner states or behaviors), which implies that the Transparency Condition holds, and why the rational agent not only reports but also avows that she believes that p, thereby both attributing the belief that p to herself and endorsing it as true (or being committed to its being true), when she answer the question whether she believes that p.

· Reconstruction of Moran's Central Thesis

- We have self-knowledge, i.e. knowledge of our own intentional states, that has the special features of epistemic immediacy and authority because we are rational agents.

우리가 인식적 직접성과 권위라는 특별한 성질을 갖고 있는 자기지식, 즉 우리 자신의 지향적 상태들에 대한 지식을 갖고 있는 이유는, 우리가 합리적 행위자이기 때문이다.

- Since we are rational agents, we have our own intentional states not only as

(i) objects of our knowledge but also as

(ii) states we arrive at by exercising our rational agency

(iii) that accordingly express (or constitute) our very identity as rational agents,

so that we as rational agents do not merely attribute our own intentional states to us but also avow them. That is, we can report our intentional states about X only by considering facts about X without observing our inner states or behaviors. This avowability shows that we as rational agents not only have our own intentional states but also commit ourselves to their truth or desirability, and thus that we are directly responsible for our own intentional states. The special features of self-knowledge result from these special features of the intentional states of rational agents. ( “Self-Knowledge as Discovery and as Resolution”)

우리가 합리적 행위자이기 때문에, 우리가 갖고 있는 지향적 상태들은

(i) 단지 우리 지식의 대상들일 뿐 아니라,

(ii) 합리적 행위자성이라는 우리 능력을 발휘함으로써 우리가 다다르게 된 상태들로서,

(iii) 이 상태들은 따라서 합리적 행위자로서의 우리 자신의 정체성을 표현(구성)한다.

그러므로, 합리적 행위자로서 우리는 우리 자신의 지향적 상태들을 단지 우리에게 귀속하는 것이 아니라 이들을 표명한다: 즉 우리는 우리 자신의 내적 상태들이나 행동에 대한 관찰 없이 X 그 자체에 관한 사실들만을 고려함으로써 X에 관한 우리의 지향적 상태들을 보고할 수 있다. 이러한 표명가능성은 합리적 행위자로서 우리가 우리 자신의 지향적 상태들을 단지 소유하고 있을 뿐 아니라 그것들의 참 또는 바람직함에 개입하고 있음을 보여주며, 이에 따라 우리가 우리 자신의 지향적 상태들에 대해 직접적으로 책임을 지고 있음을 보여준다.

※ 우리는 우리 자신의 지향적 상태들에 대해 책임을 지고 있고, 이 때 우리가 지고 있는 책임은 특별한 의미에서 직접적인 것 같다.

우리가 우리 자신의 지향적 상태들에 대해 이렇게 직접적인 책임을 지고 있는 이유는, 단지 우리가 우리 자신의 지향적 상태들을 (약한 의미에서) 만들어낸 저자(author)이기 때문만은 아닌 것 같다.

( (예) 내가 그린 그림이 높은 곳에서 떨어져서 어떤 사람에게 부딪혔고 그 결과 그 사람이 안타깝게 사망했다고 해서, 내가 그 사람의 사망에 직접적으로 책임을 지는 것은 아니다. 반면, 내가 <영희를 죽이고 싶다>는 욕구와 <영희를 칼로 찌르면 영희를 죽일 수 있다>는 믿음에 의해 영희를 찔러 죽였다면, 이 때는 나는 영희의 죽음에 직접적으로 책임을 진다.)

· Reconstruction of Moran’s Main Argument for the Central Thesis

- (Step 1) We are rational agents.

- (Step 2) The rational agent is one who can not only know one’s own intentional states but also deliberate about them in such a way that one can rationally form (“make up”), evaluate, and endorse or revise them. That is, the rational agent is one who can take both theoretical stance and deliberative stance toward one’s own intentional states.

“[P]art of what it is to be a rational agent is to be able to subject one’s attitudes to review in a way that makes a difference to what one’s attitude is.” (p.64)

※ We can take both theoretical stance (=third-person perspective) and deliberative stance (=first-person perspective) only toward our own intentional states, while we can take only theoretical stance toward objects in the world, other people, and their mental states. This asymmetry in perspectives is the real source for explaining why there is such a fundamental difference between self-knowledge, in its immediacy and authority, and other kinds of knowledge.

· Moran’s Discussion of Cases in which the Transparency Condition Fails

- (1) Cases of Moore’s Paradox

(M1) “P.    But I don’t believe that P.”

“P is desirable.    But I don’t want to bring about P”

“I will not stop gambling.    But I intend to stop gambling.” (Sartre’s akratic gambler)

(M2) “I believe that P. But P is not true”

- (2) Cases of Akrasia: Sartre’s akratic gambler

“Sartre’s case of the akratic gambler who resolves to stop gambling is in some ways a more helpful example for considering the two stances and the contrasting roles of commitment (of oneself) and theoretical knowledge about oneself. . . In Sartre’s view, his relation to his decision is transformed when it becomes for him an empirical object of consciousness, and he sees it as one of his facticities rather than identifies with it as a transcendence. [. . .] [H]is anxiety is provoked by a disengagement from his resolution, a hedging of his endorsement of it, combined with the simultaneous desire to rely on it like a natural fact. He relates to his resolution as something independent of him, like a machine he has set in motion and which now should carry him along without any further contribution from him. He seeks confidence about his own future behavior at the empirical level, but then realizes that any such theoretical confidence is utterly inadequate on its own to settle his mind, because it can only be totally parasitic on his practical-transcendetal resolution. His problem lies in the fact that if he needs empirical support, that can only be because he feels that his resolution is not strong enough on its own. And he now realizes that the empirical perspective cannot provide any additional strength of its own, for all of it is borrowed from the strengh of the resolution itself. Hence, the resolution itself appears as inadequate (“no more than the memory of an idea, a memory of a feeling”), and at the same time he feels helpless to provide it with any additional strength, because he is seeking it in the wrong place.” (pp.79-80)

- In both the cases of Moore’s Paradox and the case of Sartre’s akratic gambler, the cognitive

agent treats her intentional states such as beliefs and decisions(“resolutions”) purely as objects

of her knowledge and not as the states she has arrived at as a result of exercising his rational

agency, states which constitute herself as a rational agent. Accordingly, the cognitive agent

becomes alienated from her intentional states, her possession of them having nothing to do with

her committment to their being true or desirable.

· Moran against the Presentational View and Expressivism

- According to the Presentational View, the statment of the form “I believe P” is only a guarded way of presenting a claim about p itself and not about one’s belief, and hence should be analyzed as something like: “Probably P”.

- According to Experssivism, the statement of the form “I believe P” is merely a way of expressing one’s belief that P and is not a statement about one’s belief that can be true or false, just as wincing or crying is merely a way of expressing one’s pain and is not a statement about one’s pain that can be true or false.

- According to both views, therefore, self-knowledge is impossible from the start, for there is nothing that can be known regarding one’s own mental states.

- Among many problems, the Presentational View and Expressivim have a difficulty in explaining  why the term ‘belief’ apparently has the same meaning when it is used in the statments about one’s own mental states, which supposedly are incapable of being true or false, and when it is used about other people’s mental states or about one’s own past mental states, which supposedly are capable of being true or false.

· Moran’s Comparsion of Knowledge of One’s Intentional States and Knowledge of One’s Action

- Two different ways of knowing one’s intentional states

(1) Purely theoretical knowledge that one believes that p

One gathers evidence about oneself, from which one concludes that one believes that p.

(2) Deliberative knowledge that one believes that p ( immediate, authoritative)

One deliberates about what one should believe while conducting theoretical inquiry about the world (but not theoretical inquiry about oneself!), [makes up one’s mind(?)], and arrives at the belief that p.

- Two different ways of knowing one’s actions

(1) Purely theoretical knowledge that one will do

One gathers evidence, from which one concludes that one will do .

(2) Pratical knowledge that one will do  ( immediate, authoritative)

One deliberates about what one should do, makes up one’s mind, decides to do , and thus intends to do .

※ Anscombe on Practical Knowledge vs. Contemplative Knowledge

“Can it be that there is something that modern philosophy has blankly misunderstood: namely what ancient and medieval philosophers meant by practical knowledge? Certainly in modern philosophy we have an incorrigibly contemplative conception of knowledge. Knowledge must be something that is judged as such by being in accordance with facts. The facts, reality, are prior, and dictate what is to be said, if it is knowledge. And this is the explanation of the utter darkness in which we found ourselves. For if there are two knowledges – one by observation, the other in intention – then it looks as if there must be two objects of knowledge; but if one says the objects are the same, one looks hopelessly for the different mode of contemplative knowledge in acting, as if there were a very queer and special sort of seeing eye in the middle of acting.” (Anscombe(1957), Intention, p.57)
