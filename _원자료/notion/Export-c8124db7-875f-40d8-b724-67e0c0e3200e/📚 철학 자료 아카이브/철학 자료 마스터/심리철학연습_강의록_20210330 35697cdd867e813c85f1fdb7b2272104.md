· Previous Notes

- What Chalmers means by ‘phenomenal beliefs’ in his paper include both our (1) beliefs about our own mental states and (2) our beliefs about things and properties in the world.

(1)의 믿음들을 서술하는 문장들: “I have a red sensation”, “I feel pain”

(2)의 믿음들을 서술하는 문장들: “This is red”, “This looks red”, “The color of this tomato is red”

- Given that self-knowledge is understood as knowledge of our own mental states, and given that not all phenomenal beliefs are beliefs about our own mental states, it is misleading to think that Chalmers presents an acquaintance theory of self-knowledge in his paper.

- Moreover, what Chalmers means by ‘phenomenal beliefs’ do not include our beliefs about those mental states that do not have any distinctive phenomenology. These “non-phenomenal” mental states seem to include our standing propositional attitudes and possibly occurrent beliefs, intentions, etc. If so, Chalmers’s acquaintance theory of phenomenal beliefs can count as a theory of self-knowledge only for a very limited domain.

- Still, within this limited domain, Chalmers gives probably the most fully developed theory of how we come to have beliefs (and knowledge) about our mental states in this domain and how these beliefs (and knowledge) are special.

Background: Chalmers’s Anti-Physicalism and ‘the Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment’

· Chalmers’s Strong Property Dualism

- Phenomenal properties, or ‘qualia’, are distinct from physical properties in the strong sense that they are not logically (or metaphysically) supervenient on physical properties, although they are naturally supervenient on them.

※ 약한 속성 이원론자 (비환원적 물리주의자): mental properties (including phenomenal properties) are not identical with physical properties, although they are supervenient on physical properties so that any two individuals x and y who shares the same physical properties must share the same mental properties.

※ 강한 속성 이원론자 (반물리주의자): 최소한 현상적 속성들은 물리적 속성들과 동일하지 않을 뿐 아니라 물리적 속성들에 수반하지도 않는다. 즉 물리적 속성들에 있어서 정확히 동일한 두 개체  x와 y가 현상적 속성들에 있어서 다를 수 있다. (‘좀비’의 가능성 인정)

- Chalmer’s “zombie argument” for strong property dualism

It is logically (or metaphysically) possible that two subjects S and S* are physically identical but S is conscious while S* is S’s philosophical zombie, namely a subject who entirely lacks consciousness.

· Chalmers’s Anti-Physicalism

- If physicalism is true, then it must be that phenomenal properties are logically (or metaphysically)

supervenient on physical properties and not just naturally supervenient on physical properties.

- For mere natural supervenience is consistent with the hypothesis that such supervenience holds

in virtue of certain laws of nature that are not purely physical in that they mention both physical

properties and (irreducible) phenomenal properties.

※ 만약 현상적 속성들과 물리적 속성들 간에 자연적 수반만 성립한다면, 물리적 속성들이 발생할 때마다 그 속성들과 현상적 속성들 간에 어떤 상관관계가 법칙적으로 성립하기 때문에, 해당 물리적 속성들을 공유하고 있는 두 개체 x와 y가 해당 현상적 속성들을 공유하게 된다는 가능성을 배제할 수 없다.

- Therefore, the logical (or metaphysical) possibility of zombies shows that physicalism is wrong.

· The Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment

- (Step 1) Suppose Chalmers is right that phenomenal properties are not different from functional and physical properties in the strong sense that the former are not even supervenient on the latter, so that a physically identical twin of a conscious subject S could entirely lack consciousness.

- (Step 2) But it seems that, unlike consciousness, our cognition is entirely governed by   physical laws, so that it can be adequately explained by them.

- (Step 3) If so, however, then our judgments or beliefs about phenomenal properties must also be explainable purely by physical laws.

my judgment that “This is red”

- (Step 4) Therefore, it seems that “consciousness is explanatorily irrelevant to our claims and judgments about consciousness.” (The Conscious Mind, p.177)  The paradox of phenomenal judgment

※ “현상적 판단의 역설”을 정식화하는 다른 방법:

우리의 모든 인지 작용은 완전히 물리적으로 설명될 수 있는 것 같으므로, 우리의 의식 내용에 관한 판단 또는 믿음도 물리적으로 설명될 수 있어야 하는 것 같다. 그러나 그렇다면 의식 자체는 우리의 의식 내용에 관한 판단 또는 믿음을 설명하는데 있어서 아무런 역할을 하지 못한다. 즉 의식은 그것이 존재한다고 하더라도 아무런 설명적 역할을 하지 못한다는 점에서 단순히 부수현상에 지나지 않는 것 같다.

· Chalmers’s Answer to the Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment

- Chalmers reject (Step 2). According to him, some of our cognitions, in particular our phenomenal beliefs, are not entirely governed by physical laws, so that they cannot be adequately explained by them.

- Phenomenal qualities (or “conscious experiences”) are explanatorily relevant to (pure direct) phenomenal beliefs, although they are not causally relevant to them, because they constitute (이루다; 구성하다) the contents of (pure direct) phenomenal beliefs in such a way that, whenever a subject S has a phenomenal quality R, S is in an especially direct relation to R (= “acquainted” with R) so that S cannot be wrong in his/her (pure direct) phenomenal belief about R.

※ 우리가 “대면”하고 있는 현상적 속성들에 대해 믿음을 가질 때 이 믿음들이 오류불가능하다는 특별한 성격은, 대면 관계의 특이성 때문이 아니라 현상적 속성들의 특이성 때문일지도 모른다. 그리고 대면 관계의 특이성은, 그 관계 자체의 특이성에 의해 설명하는 것이 아니라, 이러한 현상적 속성들의 특이성 때문에 우리가 그 속성들을 갖기만 하면 자동적으로 대면 관계가 성립한다는 점을 통해 설명될 수 있을지도 모른다. (대면 관계에 대한 축소적 이해)

· Chalmers on Concept

- There is no shared agreement about what concepts are, and philosophers (as well as psychologists) mean different things by what they call “concepts”.

- Concepts are typically understood as constituents of mental contents. In this understanding, then, concepts are analogous to linguistic meanings.

- But Chalmers uses the notion of concept in a different way, according to which concepts are mental entities that are constituents of beliefs and other propositional attitudes “in a manner loosely analogous to the way in which words are constituents of sentences” (p.223).

- Although Chalmers is not committed to the existence of language of thought, it is convenient to think of what he calls “concepts” as a kind of mental expressions or mental symbols, on a par with linguistic expressions or linguistic symbols.

- Just as linguistic expressions have meanings, what Chalmers calls ‘concepts’ as mental expressions have contents.

- In the two-dimensional semantic framework adopted by Chalmers, every linguistic expression has both epistemic and subjunctive intensions.

- In this framework, concepts as mental expressions also have both epistemic and subjunctive intensions.

- Just as the epistemic and subjunctive intensions of a linguistic expression can be private, so can be the epistemic and subjunctive intensions of a concept.

· Chalmers’ Motivations for Two-Dimensional Semantics

- Defense of the close connection among meaning, a priority, and necessity

- Defense of the legitimacy of a priori modal arguments in philosophy

- Defense of Modal Monism

* According to Chalmers, epistemic and metaphysical modality are fundamentally the same kind of modality, so that both can be explained in terms of the set of same (logically) possible worlds.

* A state of affairs is epistemically possible if it holds at some possible world considered as an actual world.

* A state of affairs is metaphysically possible if it holds at some possible world considered as a merely possible world. (counterfactually, subjunctively)

· The Epistemology of Modality: A Natural Answer

- Cases of modal knowledge

(Case 1) We do not know that god exists. But we apparently know that god could exist.

(Case 2) We do not know that we are brains-in-a-vat. But we apparently know that we could

be brains-in-a-vat.

- Question: How can we know that god could exist, although we don’t know that god exists?

How can we know that we could be brains-in-a-vat, although we don’t know that we are

brains-in-a-vat?

- A natural answer: Because we can imagine, or conceive, that god exists, or that we are brains-in-a-vat.

- An assumption behind the natural answer: Conceivability implies real possibility.

· A Kripkean Challenge for the Natural Answer

- In the cases of a posteriori necessary statement, it seems that conceivability does not imply

possibility!

(e.g.) It seems that we can conceive that the statement ‘water is H2O’ is false. But this statement is necessarily true according to Kripke, so it is not possible for it to be false.

- If Chalmers accepts the Kripkean challenge, he cannot argue for the possibility of zombies from their conceivability.

- Problem: The Kripkean challenge seems to threat a lot of seemingly legitimate philosophical

arguments.

· Chalmers’s Proposal

- Conceivability in ideal cases does imply possibility.

- When it comes to propositions, there are no such things as a posteriori but necessary propositions.

- There are only a posteriori necessary statements, in the sense that the statement S is semantically

associated with an epistemic intension (= primary proposition) p1 and a subjunctive intension

(=secondary proposition) p2 such that p1 is a posteriori and contingently true while p2 is a priori

and necessarily true.

e.g.) the epistemic intension of “water is H2O”  <the watery stuff = H2O> (a posteriori, contingent)

the subjunctive intension of “water is H2O”  <H2O = H2O> (a priori, necessary)

Two Kinds of Explanations for A Posteriori Necessity and A priori Contingency

· A Kripkean Explanation

- A statement S is a posteriori necessarily true = the proposition p expressed by S is not true in every epistemically possible world but true in every metaphysically possible world.

- A statement S is a priori contingently true = the proposition p expressed by S is true in every

epistemically possible world but not true in every metaphysically possible world.

· Chalmers’ Explanation

- A statement S is a posteriori necessarily true = the epistemic proposition p1 expressed by S is not

true in every logically possible world (considered as actual), but the subjunctive proposition p2 expressed by S is true in every logically possible world (considered as counterfactual).

- A statement S is a priori contingently true = the epistemic proposition p1 expressed by S is true in every logically possible world (considered as actual), but the subjunctive proposition p2 expressed by S is not true in every logically possible world (considered as counterfactual).

- There are not two different sets of possible worlds, i.e. a set of epistemically possible worlds and

a set of metaphysically possible worlds. There is only one set of possible worlds considered in two different ways.

Two Kinds of Explanation for the A Posteriori Necessity of ‘Water = H2O’

· Kripke’s Explanation

- Necessity: The term ‘water’ is a rigid designator, referring to H2O in every possible world. Hence the statement ‘water = H2O’ is true in every possible world. Therefore, the statement “water = H2O” is necessarily true.

- A Posteriority: Strictly speaking, it is not conceivable that the statement “water = H2O” is false. But it is conceivable that a different, but qualitatively identical statement, namely the statement “the clear, drinkable liquid which fills rivers and lakes = H2O”, is false. The original statement ‘Water = H2O’ is a posteriori true in the sense that there is another, qualitatively identical statement which is false in some metaphysically possible worlds.

· Chalmers’ Explanation

- Necessity: Both the term ‘water’ and ‘H2O’ has <H2O> as its subjunctive intension. Hence the statement “Water = H2O” expresses the subjunctive proposition <H2O = H2O>. This proposition is true in every logically possible world. Hence the subjective proposition expressed by the statement ‘Water = H2O’ is necessarily true.

- A Posteriority: The term ‘water’ has <the watery stuff> as its epistemic intension while ‘H2O’ has <H2O> as its epistemic intension. Hence the statement “Water = H2O” expresses the epistemic proposition <the watery stuff = H2O>. This proposition is not true in every logically possible world. Hence the primary proposition expressed by the statement “Water = H2O” is not necessarily true. Therefore, the statement “Water = H2O” is not a priori true.

Kripke and Chalmers Against Materialism

· Kripke’s Argument

(Step 1) If physicalism is true, then the statement ‘pain = c-fiber firing’ is true

(Step 2) Both the term ‘pain’ and the term ‘c-fiber firing’ are rigid designators.

(Step 3) Hence the statement “pain = c-fiber firing” must be true in every metaphysically possible

world if it is true.

(Step 4) But it is conceivable that “pain = c-fiber firing” is false.

(Step 5) It cannot be the case that “pain = c-fiber” is conceivably false only in the sense that a different but qualitatively identical statement ‘what feels like pain = c-fiber firing’ is false in some metaphysically possible world. For what feels like pain is pain.

(Step 6) Therefore, there is some metaphysically possible world in which the original statement

“pain = c-fiber firing” is false.

(Step 7) Therefore, the statement “pain = c-fiber firing” is not true in every metaphysically possible

world.

(Step 8) Therefore, the statement “pain = c-fiber firing” is not true.

(Step 9) Therefore, physicalism is false.

· Chalmers’ Argument

(Step 1) If physicalism is true, then the property picked out by the epistemic intension of the term ‘pain’ must be a priori logically supervenient on physical states.

※ epistemic intension of the term ‘pain’ = <pain>

subjunctive intension of the term ‘pain’ = <pain>

(Step 2) So it must not be conceivable that physical states are instantiated but that the property

picked out by the epistemic intension of the term ‘pain’ is not instantiated.

(Step 3) However, it is conceivable that physical states are instantiated but that the property picked out by the primary intension of the term ‘pain’ is not instantiated.

(Step 4) Then, there is a logically possible world w* which is identical with our world wa in terms of physical states but not identical with our world in terms of the property picked out by the epistemic intension of the term ‘pain’.

(Step 5) Therefore, the property picked out by the epistemic intension of the term ‘pain’ is not

a priori logically supervenient on physical states P.

(Step 6) Therefore, physicalism is false.

· The Cognitive Significance of Phenomenal Beliefs Involving Varieties of Phenomenal Concepts

- All three beliefs are not a priori and cognitively significant, which implies that the epistemic intensions of redC, redI, E, and R are all different.

· Mary and Inverted Mary

- Mary acquires phenomenal beliefs redC= R, redI = R, and E=R when she sees the tomato for the first time outside the monochrome room.

- Inverted Mary acquires phenomenal beliefs redC= G, redI = G, and E= G when she sees the tomato for the first time outside the monochrome room.

- So Mary and Inverted Mary have different phenomenal beliefs.

- But Mary and Inverted Mary are physical/function and environmental twins, even after they see the red tomato for the first time. That they have phenomenal beliefs with different contents shows that belief content does not supervene (conceptually) on physical/functional properties.

- Therefore, in the case of the pure phenomenal concepts, we have a situation in which two concepts differ in their epistemic contents despite the subjects being physically identical.

· The Epistemic and Subjunctive Rigidity of Pure Phenomenal Concepts

- As Kripke and Kaplan have forcefully argued, proper names, natural kind terms, and indexicals are subjunctively rigid: they pick out the same referents in every possible world considered as counterfactual.

- Pure phenomenal concepts are not only subjunctively rigid (as Kripke has argued with the example of ‘pain’) but also epistemically rigid. That is, they pick out the same referents in every possible world considered as actual.

- “One might see here some justification for Russell’s claim that we have a special capacity for direct reference to our experience. Contemporary direct-reference theorists hold that Russell’s view was too restrictive, and that we can make direct reference to a much broader class of entities. But the cases they invoke are ‘direct’ only in the weak sense outlined above: the subjunctive content depends on the referent, but the epistemic content of concept does not. In the phenomenal case, the epistemic content itself seems to be constituted by the referent. It is not hard to imagine that some such epistemic requirement on direct reference is what Russell had in mind.” (pp.233-234)

· Chalmers on Direct (Pure) Phenomenal Concepts

- “In particular, I will take it that in cases such as Mary’s, the content of a phenomenal concept and a corresponding phenomenal belief, is partly constituted by an underlying phenomenal quality, in that the content will mirror the quality (picking out instances of the quality in all epistemic possibilities), and in that across a wide range of nearby conceptually possible cases in which the underlying quality is varied while background properties are held constant, the content will co-vary to mirror the quality. Let us call this sort of phenomenal concept a direct phenomenal concept.” (p.235)

- Direct phenomenal concepts and attention

“Not all experiences are accompanied by corresponding direct phenomenal concepts. Many of our experiences appear to pass without our forming any beliefs about them, and without the sort of concept formation that occurs in the Mary case. The clearest cases of direct phenomenal concepts arise when a subject attends to the quality of an experience, and forms a concept wholly based on the attention to the quality, ‘taking up’ the quality into the concept.” (p.235)
