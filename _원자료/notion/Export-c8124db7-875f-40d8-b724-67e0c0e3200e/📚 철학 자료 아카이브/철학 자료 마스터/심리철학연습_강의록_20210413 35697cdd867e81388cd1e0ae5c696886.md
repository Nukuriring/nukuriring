Inner Sense Theories of Self-Knowledge

· Main Theses of Inner Sense Theories of Self-Knowledge

(1) Ontological Thesis

We acquire beliefs about our own mental states through certain causal processes that are importantly similar to the causal processes involved in our perception of the external world.

“Introspection is a self-scanning process in the brain” (Armstrong (1968), p.314)

(2) Epistemological Thesis

These beliefs are not merely beliefs but knowledge about our own mental states because the causal processes that produce them are reliable, just as the causal processes that produce our perceptual beliefs about the external world are reliable.

(그러므로 자기지식에 대한 내적 감각 이론가들은 인식적 정당화와 관련하여 외재주의적인 입장을 취하고 있음.)

(3) Phenomenological Thesis

While some phenomenal features may accompany when we go through these causal processes, they are incidental and do not play any role in epistemically grounding our beliefs about our own mental states.

(e.g.) my knowledge that I feel pain

대면이론가: 이 지식은 고통감각에 대한 대면을 통해서 획득되고, 이 때 고통감각이라는 현상적 속성은 나의 이러한 지식을 구성하고 있으며 이 지식을 지식이게끔 해주는 정당화 역할을 하고 있다.

· Inner Sense Theorists’ Account of the ‘Privileged Access’

- According to inner sense theorists, we have ‘privileged access’ to our own mental states simply because we are biologically hardwired in such a way that the only mental states that are accessible to us are our own, whereas the ordinary objects of perception in the external world are common things among us.

- But it is a contingent fact that we are asymmetrically hardwired this way. In principle, there is no

bar to knowing others’ mental states through inner sense.

“It was argued elsewhere that it is only an empirical fact that our direct awareness of mental states is confined to our own mind. We could conceive of a power of acquiring non-verbal non-inferential knowledge of current states of the minds of others. This would be a direct awareness, or perception, of the minds of others. Indeed, when people speak of ‘telepathy’ it often seems to be this they have in mind.” (Armstrong (1968), pp.325-326)

· Does Our Knowledge of Our Selves Satisfy Conditions (3)-(6)?

- Regarding (3), ordinary perception involves that the perceiver has a certain relation to the perceived object such that one’s having this relation to that object enters into the causal explanation of one’s knowing facts of a certain kind about it.

- In the case of one’s introspective knowledge of one’s self, however, the relation between the perceiver and the perceived object should be that of identity, and “one obviously cannot come to know facts about a self by getting oneself into the relation of identity with it. The Source of the Idea for Immunity to Error through Misidentification

Wittgenstein, Blue and Brown Books (publised in 1958, which is based on the lecture notes conducted by Wittgenstein from 1933 to 1935.)

“Now the idea that the real I lives in my body is connected with the peculiar grammar of the word ‘I’, and the misunderstandings this grammar is liable to give rise to. There are two different cases in the

use of the word ‘I’ (or 'my' ) which I might call 'the use as object' and 'the use as subject' . Examples of the first kind of use are these: ‘My arm is broken’, s ‘I have grown six inche’, ‘I have a bump on my

forehead’, ‘The wind blows my hair about’.

Examples of the second kind are: ‘/ see so and so’ , ‘/ hear so and so’ , ‘/ try to lift my arm’, ‘/ think it will rain’ , ‘/ have a toothache’.

One can point to the difference between these two categories by saying: The cases of the first category involve the recognition of a particular person, and there is in these cases the possibility of an error, or as I should rather put it: The possibility of an error has been provided for . . . It is possible that, say in an accident, I should feel a pain in my arm, see a broken arm at my side, and think it is mine, when really it is my neighbour's. And I could, looking into a mirror, mistake a bump on his forehead for one on mine.

On the other hand there is no question of recognizing a person when I say I have toothache. To ask ‘are you sure that it's you who have pains?’ would be nonsensical . . .And now this way of stating our idea suggests itself: that it is impossible that in making the statement ‘I have a toothache’ I should have mistaken another person for myself, as it is to moan with pain by mistake, having mistaken someone else for me.

어떤 표현이 대상 지칭 표현이 되기 위해서는, 그 표현이 그 대상을 지칭하지 않을 수도 있어야 한다. 그러나 ‘I’가 IEM 사례들에서 사용된 ‘I’는 그 것이 가리키는 것처럼 보이는 대상을 잘못 가리킬 수 있는 가능성이 없다. 이는 이 때 사용된 ‘I’가 애초에 지칭 표현이 아니었음을 보여주는 것이다.

비록 앤스컴이 이러한 비트겐슈타인의 결론을 받아들이기는 했지만 (Anscombe, “First Person), 이러한 비트겐슈타인의 결론에 대한 Evans의 비판 이후로 오늘날 거의 대부분의 철학자들은 IEM 사례에서 사용된 ‘I’가 지칭 표현이 아니라는 급진적인 결론을 거부하고 있음.

To say, ‘I have pain’ is no more a statement about a particular person than moaning is. ‘But surely the word ‘I’ in the mouth of a man refers to the man who says it; it points to himself . . . ’. But it was quite superfluous to point to himself.” (Blue and Brown Books, pp.66-7)

- Shoemaker against the ‘act-object’ conception of our knowledge of our own sensations

“Sense-datum theory has been out of favor for thirty or forty years, for reasons I cannot undertake to rehearse here. But I think that it is not sufficiently appreciated that the reasons for rejecting the sense-datum theory are at the same time reasosnf or rejecting the act-object conception as applied to pains and after-images. [. . .] Consider the case of double vision. It is natural to say that when my finger is near my nose I see two finger-shaperd and figer-colored images. Suppose we take this literally, and hold that in such cases I am perceiving two mental entities, call them images, having the properties in question. What happens when I move finger away, or focus my eyes, so that, as one might say, the two images coalesed into one? Surely it is intolerable to say that in this case two mental images coalesced into one flesh and blood finger. If we say that when seeing double I saw two finger-shaped mental images, then we had better say that what these coalesced into is one finger-shaped mental image, and that, as the sense-daum theory says, veridically perceiving a physical thing always involves ‘immediately’ perceiving a mental image. And if we find the latter view unacceptable, as most recent philosophers do, then one should resist the temptation, which I think is considerable, to reify double images. And I think we should also resist the temptation, which is almost irresistible, to reify after-images. In other words, we should reject the act-object conception as applied to these.” (pp.262-263).

- According to Shoemaker, we should not be confused between the intentional object of a sensory

state, which is not a real object, and an actually existing object.

- David Armstrong against the act-object conception of after-image

“To have an after-image is to seem to see a physical phenomenon of a certain sort: the after-image itself, I maintain, is a purely intentional object, like the thing believed in the case of a false belief.” (Armstrong (1984), “Consciousness and Causality”, in Armstrong & Malcolm, Consciousness and Causality.)

- In the cited paragraph above, Armstrong defends the ‘inner sense’ model of self-knowledge while

denying that the after-image is an existing object. This shows that Armstrong’s model is not the

object-perception model but the broad perceptual model.

Lecture 2. “The Broad Perceptual Model”

· The Notion of Self-Blindness

- A person is self-blind iff he/she is introspectively blind.

- If a person is a self-blind, he/she will not be able to acquire non-inferential knowledge of his/her own mental states. But he/she can still acquire knowledge of his/her own mental states through inferences based on the observations of his/her behaviors, his/her inner physiology, and the world. Hence a self-blind person knows what it meas that he has those mental states.

“To be self-blind with respect to a certain kind of mental fact or phenomenon, a creature must have the ability to conceive of those facts and phenomena, just as the person who is literally blind will be able to conceive of those states of affairs she is unable to learn about visually” (p.273; italics added)

(e.g.) A self-blind person knows what it means that he feels pain, just as a literally blind person knows what it means that the sky is blue.

슈메이커가 말하는 자기맹인 사람은, 일반적인 맹인의 경우와 정확히 유비가 되고 있음!

· Shoemaker’s Main Argument against the Broad Perceptual Model

(Premise 1) If the broad perceptual model of self-knowledge is correct, then it is (conceptually and metaphysically) possible that a rational creature is self-blind.

질문: 왜 합리적이면서 자기맹인 존재의 가능성까지 함축하는가?

(단순히 자기맹인 존재의 가능성만 함축하는게 아니라)

답변: 일반적인 맹인의 경우와의 유비

맹인이면서 합리적인 사람들이 분명히 가능하다. (가능할 뿐 아니라 실제로 많이 존재!). 그렇다면, 자기맹이면서도 합리적인 개체들도 분명히 가능해야 할 것이다.

(Premise 2) It is not (conceptually or metaphysically) possible that a rational creature is self-blind.

(Conclusion) Therefore, the broad perceptual model of self-knowledge is not correct.

· Shemaker’s Argument for (Premise 1)

- He seems to think that Condition (8), namely the independence condition, implies (Premise 1)

“[F]rom an evolutionary standpoint, widespread self-blindness is not a real possibility. But it will be held that for each kind of mental fact to which we have introspective access, it is at least logically possible that there should be creatures in which such facts obtain, and who have the ability to conceive of them, but who are self-blind with respect to them. This I take to be a consequence of the independence condition that is built into the broad perceptual model of self-knowledge.” (p.273)

· Shomaker’s Argument for (Premise 2): The Case of Pain

- (Step 1) Let us suppose that there is a creature, A, which is rational but which is self-blind with respect to pain.

- (Step 2) If A is rational, A has intellectual and conceptual capacities comparable with ours.

- (Step 3) If A is self-blind with respect to pain, A does not know that A feels pain, although A does feel pain.

- (Step 4) If A has intellectual and conceptual capacities comparable to us, A must be able to exhibit pain behaviors comparable to ours.

- (Step 5) But A’s exhibiting the pain behaviors comparable to ours are (conceptually and

metaphysically) possible only on the condition that A knows that A feels pain, as well as on the condition that A knows various beliefs, desires, and other mental states about the pain he feels, e.g. A’s belief that the pain is unpleasant, A’s desire to get rid of it, A’s wish that it would end.

왜 이러한 지식을 갖고 있어야 하는가?

(한 답변) 합리적인 개체라면, 자신의 행위에 대한 이유를 제시할 수 있어야 한다. 그런데 자신의 행위에 대한 이유를 제시할 수 있기 위해서는, 자신이 어떤 믿음과 욕구를 갖고 있는지를 알고 있어야 한다.

- (Step 6) Since A is self-blind with respect to pain, A cannot have such knowledge.

- (Step 7) Hence A cannot exhibit pain behaviors comparable to ours. (From (Step 5) and (Step 6))

- (Step 8) Therefore, A does have not intellectual and conceptual capacities comparable to us.

(From (Step 4) and (Step 7))

- (Step 9) Therefore, A is not a rational creature (From (Step 2) and (Step 8))  Contradiction!
