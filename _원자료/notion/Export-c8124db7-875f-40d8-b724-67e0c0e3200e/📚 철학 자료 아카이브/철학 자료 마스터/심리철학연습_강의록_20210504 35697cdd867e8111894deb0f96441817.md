· Main Claims in Authority and Estrangement [with a Focus on Beliefs]

- (Claim 1) We are rational agents.

※ This seems to be a transcendental assumption, namely, a condition that must hold given that (i) we have a capacity to “make up” [=form, evaluate, endorse or revise] our own intentional states and that, (ii) if it is to be possible for us to have such capacity, we must be rational agents.

- (Claim 2) The rational agent has a capacity to make up his own intentional states by exercising his rational agency.

※ Exercising one’s rational agency is an activity that is taking place at a personal level, not at a sub-personal level. That is why agency is involved here. In this respect, “making up” one’s own intentional states by exercising one’s rational agency is importantly different from subpersonal control of them.

“[I]f what McGinn means by ‘the rational adjustment of beliefs’ is simply whatever it is that keeps one’s belief-system in good working order, avoiding gross inconsistency, updating and revising beliefs in the light of the constant flow of experience, and so on, then there is no reason this regulation could not take place below the threshold of consciousness, perhaps at a wholly ‘subpersonal’ level. [. . .] Looked at this way, there is no more reason to think that the rational regulation of belief requires awareness of the beliefs in question than there is to think that the regulation of heartbeat, respiration, or metabolism requires conscious monitoring, or even a creature with the capacity for consciousness at all.” (p.110)

“In various cases a person may produce in himself various desires, beliefs, or emotional responses, either by training, mental discipline, drugs, the cooperation of friends, or simply by hurling himself into a situation that will force a certain response from him. But exercising this sort of control over one’s attitudes is not the expression of ‘activity’ relevant to autonomy or rational authority. In such cases of producing a desire in oneself, the resulting attitude is still one I am essentially passive with respect to. It is inflicted on me, even if I am the one inflicting it.” (p.117)

- (Claim 3) The rational agent seeks to have intentional states he ought to have. In the case of beliefs, the beliefs the rational agent ought to have are true beliefs. In the cases of desires and intentions, the desires and intentions the rational agent ought to have are desirable desires and intentions. Therefore, the rational agent seeks to have intentional states that are true and desirable.

- (Claim 4) Since the rational agent seeks to have intentional states that are true and desirable, it is normally the case that he will believe that p if he holds p as true. In other words, it is normally the case that the rational agent will actually believe that p if he is committed to the truth of the belief that p.

- (Claim 5) The rational agent is committed to the truth of a belief that p when there is a reason for believing that p. Therefore, it is normally the case that when the rational agent considers whether p is true and concludes that p is true based on a certain reason, he will be committed to the truth of the belief that p, and accordingly believe that p.

- (Claim 6) For the rational agent, therefore, the theoretical question “Do I believe that p?” is intimately related to the deliberative question “Shall I believe that p?”.

- (Claim 7) Therefore, it is normally the case that the beliefs possessed by the rational agent obey the following Transparency Condition: For any belief p possessed by the rational agent, he can know that he believes that p only by considering facts about p and concluding that p is true based on a certain reason. This knowledge is immediate and authoritative because (i) The rational agent believes that he believes that p without any evidence about his psychological states or behaviors, and (ii) the rational agent’s belief that he believes that p is guaranteed to be true because it is normally the case that the rational agent will believe that p when he concludes that p by considering facts about p.

※ As the following quotes illustrate, in Authority and Estrangement Moran formulates the Transparency Condition and the accompanying notion of avowal not in terms of one’s knowledge of one’s belief that p but in terms of one’s report about one’s belief that p in answering the question of whether one believes that p. · Question about (Claim 7)

- It seems that (Claim 1)~(Claim 6) are all plausible and insightful.

- For our purpose, however, the most important claim is (Claim 7), for it is this claim that explains how we as rational agents can have self-knowledge, i.e. knowledge of our own mental states and why this knowledge has the peculiar feature of immediacy and authority.

- Unfortunately, Moran’s argument for (Claim 7) in Authority and Estrangement is obscure and confusing.

- In particular, it is far from clear whether Moran in Authority and Estrangement is effectively addressing the so-called “Puzzle of Transparency”, namely the puzzle of how one could know what one’s belief is like, which is a psychological fact about a certain person, by considering non-psychological facts in the world that apparently have nothing to do with the person in question.

(e.g.) How could I know that I believe that it is raining only by considering the facts about what the weather is like, given that these facts apparently have nothing to do what my psychological states are like?

· A Possible Reconstruction of Moran’s Argument for (Claim 7)

- The rational agent knows <I believe that p> only if

(i) he believes <I believe that p>

(ii) his belief <I believe that p> is true

(iii) his belief <I believe that p> is warranted.

only by considering facts about p and not by considering anything about himself

- Therefore, we have to show that (i) the rational agent believes <I believe that p>, (ii) his belief <I believe that p> is true, and (iii) his belief <I believe that p> is warranted when he is considering the question whether he believes that p only by considering facts about p.

· Argument for (i)

- Given that the rational agent considers facts about p and reaches a conclusion that p based on a certain reason, he will normally form a belief <p>.

- But would it be the case that the rational agent will also form a belief <I believe that p> when he considers facts about p and reaches a conclusion that p?

- Moran’s answer is that the rational agent will indeed form such a belief, as long as he is considering the question whether he believes that p. And he seems to justify this answer by appealing to the (brute?) fact that this is indeed what we as rational agents are doing when we are being asked “Do you believe that p?”.

- That is to say, (i) we as rational agents are considering only facts about p when we consider whether we believe that p, and (ii) we normally come to believe that we believe that p as a result of this consideration.

※ According to Shoemaker’s Constitutivism, the functionalist architecture of the beliefs of the rational creature is such that, whenever he believes that p, he will also believe <I believe that p>. So this is one way of explaining why we as rational agents believe <I believe that p> when we are engaged in considerations about whether p is true and reach a conclusion that p. But Moran denies that the rational agent believes <I believe that p> whenever he believes that p, making it clear that he is only making a weaker claim that the rational agent will believe <I believe that p> only when he is considering the question of whether he believes that p.

· Argument for (ii)

- Given that the cognitive subject here is the rational agent, normally he will indeed form a belief that p when he considers facts about p and reaches a conclusion that p.

- Therefore, his belief <I believe that p> will be normally true.

· Argument for (iii)

- This is the most problematic part.

- A relatively easy way to defend (iii) is to take Reliabilism about warrant, according to which a belief is warranted if and only if it is produced by a reliable process, i.e. a process that tends to produce true beliefs.

- Since the Argument for (ii) shows implies that the rational agent’s self-attributed belief <I believe that p> is normally true and not just something that happens to be true, it seems that it is produced by a reliable process, which would then show that the rational agent’s belief <I believe that p> is indeed warranted.

· Moran’s Comparison of Knowledge of One’s Intentional States and Knowledge of One’s Action

- Two different ways of knowing one’s own intentional states

(1) Purely theoretical knowledge that one believes that p

One gathers evidence about oneself, based on which one knows that one believes that p.

(e.g.) Younghee finds that she no longer enjoys talking with her brother, that she feels angry with him and cannot sleep well whenever she is thinking of him. Based on these psychological and behavioral facts about herself, together with a number of counseling she had with a psychotherapist, she knows that she believes that her brother has betrayed her, although she cannot accept the fact that he has done such a terrible thing to her.

Younghee: “I believe that my brother has betrayed me. But he has not betrayed me!”

(2) Deliberative knowledge that one believes that p ( immediate, authoritative)

One is considering whether one should believe that p (=whether p is true) and arrives at the belief that p. Then one knows immediately and authoritatively that one believes that p in so far as one is considering the question of whether one believes that p.

(e.g.) Younghee considers whether her brother has betrayed me. Based on the facts that he inappropriately used the money in her company for personal use and that he has been involved in an embezzlement scheme, she concludes that her brother has indeed betrayed me. Then she knows immediately authoritatively that she believes that her brother has indeed betrayed me in so far as she is considering the question of whether she believes that p.

Younghee: “I believe that my brother has betrayed me. He inappropriately used the money in my company for his personal use, and he has been involved in an embezzlement!”

- Two different ways of knowing one’s own actions

(1) Purely theoretical knowledge that one will do

One gathers evidence, based on which one knows that one will do .

(e.g.) Younghee knows that she will come to her office tomorrow, based on the evidence that, in her planner, it is written that a meeting with a student is schedule tomorrow.

Chulsoo: “Will you come to office tomorrow?”

Younghee: “Let me see. . . Yes, I will come to office tomorrow. I have a meeting appointment with a student.”

(2) Practical knowledge that one will do  ( immediate, authoritative)

One deliberates about what one should do (=what is desirable thing to do), makes up one’s mind, decides to do , and thus intends to do . Then one knows immediately and authoritatively that one will do  in so far as one is considering the question of whether one will do .

(e.g.) Younghee deliberates about what she should do tomorrow and decides to clean her office.

Then she knows immediately and authoritatively that she will come to her office tomorrow

in so far as she is considering the question of whether she will do so.

Chulsoo: “Will you come to office tomorrow?”

Younghee: “Yes, I will come to office tomorrow. My office is too dirty and I need to clean it.”

Shoemaker (2003), “Moran on Self-Knowledge”

· Three Questions Raised by Shoemaker

- (Question 1) Belief without Commitment to Truth?

“Moran assumes that, in abnormal cases, a rational agent can only have a theoretical stance toward his belief that p without taking a deliberative stance. That is, Moran assumes that, in abnormal cases, a rational agent can only self-attribute the belief that p without being committed to the truth of p. But Moran himself says that ‘commitment is internal to the very concept of belief’ (p.105). . .[So] it seems impossible that one could believe that P without endorsing and being commtted to the proposition that P.” (Shoemaker 2003, p.393).

- (Question 2) Does Self-Knowledge Need Deliberation or the Exercise of One’s Rational Agency?

“[I]t is plain that deliberation is an exercise of agency. But as Moran acknowledges, most cases in which one is aware of one’s beliefs are not cases in which there is deliberation. In the vast majority of cases they are not. [. . .] I know that I am wearing pants, so I believe that I am wearing pants. I know and believe that I believe that I am wearing pants. This is as substantial a piece of self-knowledge as there can be, and a theory of self-knowledge should account for it. But it is hard to think of circumstances, other than those of a dream, in which it could be a question for me whether I believe this. I would also have a hard time saying what reasons I have for believing it. And I cannot think of any good sense in which it is ‘up to me’ whether I believe it.” (Shoemaker 2003, pp.395-396).

자신이 이미 믿고 있는 비교적 분명한 믿음들의 경우, 이 믿음들을 내가 갖고 있다는 것을 이 믿음들의 내용이 참인지 여부에 대해 숙고함으로써 알게 되는 것 같지는 않다.

- (Question 3) How Can We Acquire Self-Knowledge from Deliberation? (= Question about the Puzzle of Transparency)

“Now when one deliberates in response to the question whether one believes that P, and does this in the way Moran describes, focusing one’s attention to facts about the world that might be reasons for believing that P, and this deliberation reaches an affirmative conclusion, the immediate upshot of the deliberation will be (1) an affirmation, or reaffirmation, that P. But this affirmation will be accompanied with (2) an affirmation that one believes that P, and affirmation that constitutes immediate and authoritative knowledge. This is what an account of self-knowledge of belief should explain, and I don’t see how Moran’s account is supposed to explain it. His account in terms of epistemic agency may explain how we get to the affirmation or reaffirmation of the content proposition, but it seems to me not to explain how we get to the affirmation of the self-attribution.” (Shoemaker 2003, p.397)

“If reflecting on the reasons is just reflecting on facts (or presumed facts) about the world that are (or would be) evidence that P, this can certainly contribute to his arriving at an affirmation that P, but it has yet to be explained how can it lead to his arriving at an affirmation of ‘I believe that P’. (Shoemaker 2003, p.398)

· Shoemaker’s Constitutivist Answer to (Question 3)

“On my view, assenting to P entails assenting to the proposition that one assents to P, and assenting to the proposition that one assents to P entails assenting to P. If this is true, there is a question as to why it is true. [. . .] I think the answer to this lies in a constitutive relation between belief and rationality. Roughly, being a believer requires a certain degree of rationality, and that degree of rationality requires access to one’s own cognitive attitudes. (Shoemaker 2003, p.398)

Moran (2003), “Responses to O’Brien and Shoemaker”

· Answer to (Question 1): Distinction between Implicit and Explicit Commitment to Truth

- “If I do in fact have some belief, then I am to that extent committed to the truth of the proposition in question. But this tie between belief and commitment still seems to allow for the possibility that I could be committed to the truth of some belief that is the subject of such shame and anxiety for me that I deny it in public.” (Moran 2003, p.407)

“To say that I cannot avow the belief is to say that when the question of its truth is explicitly raised for me, I hold back, I resist saying to myself or to others, ‘Yes, he utterly betrayed me’. I cannot face this thought. When I am not reflecting on it, there is ample reason to think that I nonetheless cling to this very belief, perhaps tacitly shieling it from criticism, ignoring or explaining away counter-evidence.” (Moran 2003, p.407)

· Answer to (Question 2): Distinction between Weak Form of Agency and Strong Form of Agency

- “[T]he sense of ‘agency’ I have in mind with respect to beliefs and other intentional states is specifically the agency that is engaged by evidence, reasons, and justification.” (Moran 2003, p.403)

- “With respect to my beliefs and other attitudes there is some form of agency reflected in the verbs of weighing evidence, drawing conclusions, responding to criticisms, and a host of other cognitive activities that are part of what constitutes being sensitive to reasons.” (Moran 2003, p.403)

어떤 심리 상태가 이유에 민감하다는 특성만을 갖고 있으면, 해당 심리 상태는 모란이 그것에 대한 자기지식을 설명하고자 하는 지향적 상태에 속한다.

우리의 많은 감정들과 느낌들은 이러한 이유 민감성을 갖고 있으므로, 모란이 말하는 지향적 상태들은 소위 순수하게 “인지적”인 상태들만으로 포함하고 있는 것은 아니다.

- “In speaking of agency here at all, the contrast that is important to me is with other states of oneself, states for which there is simply no question of reasons, sensitivity to reasons, or the possibility of changing one’s mind.” (Moran 2003, p.403)

- “Without some assumption of agency, there would be no point to the interpersonal demand for justification or correction, for there would be nothing the person could be expected to do in response to this demand.” (Moran 2003, pp.403-404).

· Answer to (Question 3): A (Transcendental) Assumption about Transparency?

- “What right (=what kind of epistemic entitlement?) have I to think that my reflection on the reasons in favor of P (which is one subject-matter) has anything to do with the question of what my actual belief about P is (which is quite a different subject matter)? Without a reply to this challenge, I don’t have any right (=I am not entitled?) to answer the question that asks what my belief is by reflection on the reasons in favor of an answer concerning the state of the weather.

And then my thought at this point is: I would have a right (=I would be entitled?) to assume that my reflection on the reasons in favor of rain provided an answer to the question of what m(y belief about the rain is, if I could assume that what my belief here is was something determined by the conclusion of my reflection on those reasons. An assumption of this sort would provide the right sort of link between the two questions. · Shoemaker’s Puzzlement about Moran’s Answer to (Question 3)

“Moran says at one point in ‘Responses’ that he would have the right to take his world-directed reflection in favor of rain as providing an answer to the question of what his belief about rain is ‘if I could assume that what my belief here is was something determined by the conclusion of my reflection on these reasons.’ I take it he does not mean that the assumption functions as a suppressed premise that somehow contributes to generating the self-ascription, and in so doing contributes to justifying it. This leaves me somewhat in the dark about what justificatory role this assumption is supposed to play. Must I actually make this assumption, actually believe in its content, in order to be justified in saying that I believe so and so? Or is it sufficient that the assumption be true?” (Shoemaker 2003, p.401)

· Tentative Answers

- An answer to (1):

Perhaps what Moran means by ‘assumption’ here is purely dispositional knowledge, an ability or know-how, that cannot play the role of a premise in an inference but that is nevertheless exhibited in the process of the rational agent’s believing <I believe that p> when he is considering whether he believes that p and comes to believe <I believe that p> if he concludes that p based on his consideration of whether there are reasons in favor of p.

- An answer to (2):

Since the ‘assumption’ is (purely dispositional) knowledge and not merely (purely dispositional) belief, it entitles the rational agent to believe <I believe that p> when he is considering whether he believes that p and comes to believe <I believe that p> if he concludes that p based on his consideration of whether there are reasons in favor of p.
