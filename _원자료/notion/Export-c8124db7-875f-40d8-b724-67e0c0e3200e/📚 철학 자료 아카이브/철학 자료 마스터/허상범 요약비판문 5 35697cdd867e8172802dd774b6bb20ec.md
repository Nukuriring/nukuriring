Critical Note on Shoemaker (1994)

2019-28574 허상범

Shoemaker identifies two conditions that he sees are essential to what he calls the “broad perceptual model” and proceeds to argue that these are not met for introspective self-knowledge. These two claims are as follows:

Causal Condition: Our beliefs about mental states are causally produced by states, via a reliable belief-producing mechanism, which qualifies them as knowledge of those states.

Independence Condition: The existence of these states is independent of their being known as in (a) and also independent of such mechanisms as described in (a) existing.

In this critical note, I give a brief exposition on Shoemaker’s argument against the broad perceptual model and then cast doubt on the success of his argument that the independence condition cannot be met in the case of introspective self-knowledge about sensations.

Shoemaker’s general strategy is to undermine the independence condition and by doing so undermine the causal condition as well. The compelling intuition seems to be this: if mental states and their being known to their subject cannot plausibly thought to be independent of each other then it seems that they cannot be contingently connected (i.e. it seems that they are conceptually connected). If this is the case, then the causal condition fails because there would be no conceptual work left to do for reliable belief-producing mechanisms in acquiring introspective self-knowledge; causation would seem the wrong kind of connection between states and knowledge. Thus, he objects to the independence condition by arguing that it is impossible to be incapable of introspective self-knowledge about sensations and intentional mental states.

More specifically, Shoemaker argues that the independence condition fails for both introspective self-knowledge about sensations and introspective self-knowledge about intentional mental states because of the impossibility of ‘self-blindness’. Here,

a subject of mental states is self-blind if

it is impossible for that subject to know of a mental fact or phenomenon through introspection while being

(i) able to conceive of those facts and phenomena and

(ii) able to learn about those facts and phenomena through other means than introspective access.

In the case of intentional mental states, Shoemaker argues roughly as follows. A normally rational being (i.e. one that meets conditions (i) and (ii)) is just the kind of being who is self-aware, i.e. those whose beliefs and desires rationalize their behavior. Because first order mental states such as beliefs and desires just do give rise to second order beliefs about such first order mental states for such beings, it cannot be that normally rational beings can be self-blind.

In the case of sensations, Shoemaker argues that it is impossible to be in a state such as pain without being capable of being introspectively aware of it. The reason for this, Shoemaker says, is that sensations such as pain give rise to other sorts of mental states that one must be aware of to engage in behaviors that are indicative of that pain and from which the subject would learn of that one is in pain.

The thought is that since the subject is not introspectively aware of pain, she must learn through inference from behavior. If the subject is self-blind, however, then she must be self-blind to these other mental states which allow one to engage in these behaviors, namely, the reasons that make it intelligible for the subject to engage in such behavior.

As an example, take a commonplace response to being in pain: taking some aspirin. This behavior seems to require awareness of disliking the pain and wishing it to end, which serve as reasons that rationalize the taking of aspirin. But without an awareness of pain, Shoemaker says, awareness of these reasons is unintelligible. Thus, Shoemaker concludes, it is impossible to be self-blind about sensations such as pain.

==================

This last argument, I think, is circular. The central assumption that Shoemaker seems to be making is that the intelligibility of pain-related behavior depends on introspective access to the reasons that rationalize it. However, if it is true that a self-blind subject is able to conceive of facts about pain and pain itself (as per stipulation (i)) as well as learn about them through other means, (as per stipulation (ii)) then a defender of self-blindness can insist that because of this fact, the self-blind subject can make the reasons behind pain-related behavior intelligible, in whatever alternative means that they used to learn about that sensation.

An analogy to the intelligibility of color to those with perceptual blindsight

(맹시: 시각적 경험은 하지 못하지만, 시각적 경험을 하고 있는 사람이 하는 행동들과 동일한 행동을 하는 환자:

(예) 자신의 앞에 있는 장애물들이 보이지 않는다고 호소함. 그러나 이 장애물들을 능숙하게 잘 피해갈 수 있음)

may help us imagine how this might be possible. Suppose that a subject that exhibits blindsight is initially unaware of the fact that she has blindsight. But, through successive experiments with neuroscientists, she discovers that she can ‘blind-see,’ as it were; she now knows that she has blindsight. Suppose she correctly guesses the color of a green plant in front of her in an experiment subsequent to her discovery that she has blindsight. When she guesses, in this experiment, that the object in front of her is green, she has engaged in verbal behavior that is rationalized by the belief that there is a green object in front of her.  Her behavior is rationalized by something other than an effect of visual perception (i.e. the visual perception of the color green), namely blindsight, but it is intelligible nonetheless.

논평: 맹시 환자의 사례는 매우 흥미로운 사례이긴 하지만, 자기맹 논증과 관련해서 적절한 사례인지는 의문이 들기는 함.

맹시 환자의 경우는 대상에 대한 지각적 지식을 갖고 있다는 보는 것이 더 적절함. (어떠한 현상적 특성도 지각하지 못한다는 점에서 우리의 지각적 지식과는 다르지만, 맹시 환자가 장애물을 피할 때 그 환자는 자신의 앞에 이러 저러한 장애물이 이러저러하게 있다는 것을 추론에 의해 알지 않음. 그래서 맹시 환자의 경우 그가 장애물에 대해 얻는 지식은, 비지각적 지식이라기보다는 현상적 지식을 동반하지 않은 지각적 지식으로 보는 것이 더 그럴듯함.)

Something similar, I think, may hold for self-blindness. Without appropriate descriptions of the available alternatives for introspection, awareness of reasons that rationalize pain-related behavior may seem unintelligible. But suppose the subject has the introspective analogue of blindsight. Then, this subject may be able to use this introspective blindsight to make pain-related behavior intelligible.

This line of thought, of course, requires further argument – for instance, with respect to the possibility of something like introspective blindsight. But Shoemaker is too quick to dismiss the independence condition as well. He needs an independent argument for the claim that reasons for sensation-related behaviors are unintelligible in the absence of introspective awareness.
