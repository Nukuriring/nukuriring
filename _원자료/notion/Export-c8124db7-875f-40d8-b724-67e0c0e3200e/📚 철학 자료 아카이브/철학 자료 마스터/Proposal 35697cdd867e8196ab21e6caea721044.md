Proposal Title: Maintaining the Self: Rethinking Social Identity as a Fluid Object

Abstract

This paper proposes a new ontological model of social identity as a “fluid object,” expanding on and diverging from Youjin Kong’s influential account of identity as a process of navigating power. While Kong emphasizes the lived and strategic aspects of marginalized identity formation, I argue that identity must also be understood as a materially and affectively sustained object — one subject to breakdown, repair, and infrastructural neglect. Drawing from Science and Technology Studies, particularly the work of Mol and de Laet, I conceptualize social identity as a fragile assemblage held together through distributed labor and maintenance. Through a reinterpretation of Kong’s own case studies — particularly the “Asian philosopher” — I show how identity becomes burdensome when the cost of its upkeep is individualized. I further argue that the breakdown of identity objects can give rise to publics that demand their collective repair, reframing identity politics as a form of infrastructural care. Finally, I address concerns about fragmentation by demonstrating how structurally analogous maintenance burdens allow for solidarity across divergent identity groups. This model provides a new framework for understanding intersectional justice not through metaphysical unity, but through networked fragility and shared attachment.

1. Introduction: Identity, Intersectionality, and the Problem of Fixity

This paper begins from a shared motivation articulated in Youjin Kong’s recent account of social identity: the need to move beyond static, metaphysical understandings of identity that generate persistent philosophical anxieties within intersectionality discourse. These anxieties center on the worry that if identities are treated as fixed categories, intersectionality will result in ontological fragmentation and political incoherence. Kong’s model responds to this concern by theorizing marginalized social identity as a power-navigating process — a dynamic negotiation between subjects and oppressive structures, rooted in the lived experiences of multiply marginalized individuals.

While I affirm Kong’s anti-essentialist motivation and her non-ideal, power-sensitive methodology, I propose a distinct ontological model of identity. Specifically, I argue that social identity is best conceptualized not as a process, but as a fluid object in the sense articulated by Annemarie Mol and Marc de Laet. Whereas Kong frames identity as something one "does" or "negotiates," I suggest that identity is better understood as something one maintains — an assemblage of shifting, fragile components that must be materially and affectively stabilized in order to persist across contexts.

This shift requires a new functional understanding of what social identity does. In this model, social identity is not merely a descriptive category or a symbolic expression, but a mediating device between the self and social worlds. It enables practical coordination (e.g., access to institutions, legibility within norms), affective orientation (e.g., belonging, pride, shame), and existential coherence (e.g., narrative integration of one’s life). As a fluid object, identity’s coherence across these domains is never guaranteed: it must be maintained through personal, interpersonal, and infrastructural work. The concept of identity as a fluid object thus foregrounds its instrumentality without instrumentalism — identity functions as an operational interface between person and world, but it also exerts a grip on the subject through attachment, obligation, and affective investment.

2. From Power-Navigating Process to Maintainable Object: Shifting the Frame

Kong’s conception of identity as a power-navigation process offers key insights: it captures identity’s contingency, multiplicity, and embeddedness in structural oppression. However, it also tends to prioritize agency and experience while downplaying the material and infrastructural conditions under which identities are maintained, fail, or mutate.

By contrast, the fluid object model, drawn from Science and Technology Studies (STS), emphasizes not how identities are performed or negotiated, but how they are kept going — how they break down, get repaired, and persist through maintenance labor. The term "fluid object," developed by Mol and de Laet in their analysis of the Zimbabwe Bush Pump, refers to an entity whose identity persists despite undergoing continuous reconfiguration across sites and users. Social identities, I argue, exhibit the same ontological structure: they are unstable but durable, multiply enacted but loosely held together, and dependent on care, repair, and infrastructure.

This reframing offers three primary theoretical shifts. First, it reconceptualizes breakdown as an ordinary, ongoing condition of social identity rather than an exceptional failure. Second, it foregrounds the labor—affective, epistemic, and material—through which identity is stabilized. Third, it highlights the unequal distribution of maintenance burdens, particularly how marginalized subjects disproportionately bear the responsibility of keeping their identities livable and legible within hostile or indifferent systems. This makes visible a crucial dimension of structural injustice: the invisibilized labor of identity maintenance. In short, while Kong captures the strategic and navigational aspects of identity under oppression, the fluid object model illuminates its infrastructural fragility and affective cost.

3. Core Features of the Fluid Object Model

The model of social identity as a fluid object is grounded in the following interrelated features:

Ontological Fluidity: A fluid object does not have a fixed internal essence but maintains coherence through variable configurations across time and context. A social identity is fluid not because it lacks structure, but because it retains viability through its adaptability. Its multiplicity is not a flaw but a functional characteristic.

Maintenance: The viability of an identity depends on continual upkeep — affective (emotional investment, belonging), epistemic (narrative coherence, intelligibility), and material (documentation, recognition systems, institutional acknowledgment). Identity is something one must work to keep functioning.

Attachment: Individuals do not merely hold identities as external classifications; they are attached to them affectively and existentially. This attachment generates a normative obligation to maintain the identity even when it fails to confer dignity or recognition.

Fluid(Ambiguous) Breakdown: Identity does not always collapse in dramatic or totalizing ways. More often, it falters through low-grade failures: misrecognition, institutional neglect, stigmatization. These ambiguous breakdowns do not terminate identity but degrade its usability and livability.

Socio-Technical Embeddedness: Social identities are not simply self-ascriptions or intersubjective meanings. They are enacted and stabilized within broader socio-technical systems — language, law, algorithms, paperwork, disciplinary norms. Identity is infrastructural.

These features recast identity as a dynamic and fragile assemblage that persists not through essential features but through ongoing relations of enactment, repair, and care. The social ontology that emerges is not one of categorical coherence but of conditional durability — an identity that “works” so long as enough pieces hold together and enough labor is invested in sustaining them.

This structure becomes especially clear when considered alongside STS cases such as the Zimbabwe Bush Pump or, in digital technoculture, the MMORPG server. Like the Bush Pump, a social identity persists despite internal variation or external stress, so long as it is rendered usable through contextual adaptation. Like a multiplayer game server, it remains “on” only through sustained infrastructural care, vulnerable to silent decay when neglected. These analogues show that identity-as-fluid-object is not a metaphorical flourish but an ontological claim about how coherence is achieved through ongoing, partial maintenance — not essence, but endurance.

We can formalize this ontological claim as follows:

A fluid object persists through reconfiguration, not stability of parts.

Social identities require such reconfiguration to remain intelligible and livable.

Their usability is ensured by ongoing labor of attachment, stabilization, and contextual adaptation.

∴ Therefore, social identities are best understood as fluid objects: multiply enacted, maintained, and functionally sustained assemblages.

The case of Mabinogi provides a particularly rich illustration of how “fluid breakdowns” become socially and politically salient. Here, breakdowns are not binary states of function or failure, but ambiguous degradations that shift the burden of functionality onto users. Game mechanics that once embodied design flexibility (e.g., gold pouching systems, skill training thresholds) gradually became dysfunctional, not because the system stopped operating, but because players had to engage in increasingly elaborate workarounds. This ongoing self-repair by users — an improvisational labor born from attachment — kept the game playable, but deferred structural maintenance.

Yet over time, the cumulative affective burden of these micro-breakdowns transformed irritation into interpretive rupture. As updates and patches privileged monetized features (e.g., “kits” and “reforging” mechanics) while neglecting user-reported flaws, players began to read their own maintenance labor as evidence of structural abandonment. Maintenance was no longer a gesture of love but a symptom of exploitation. The game still functioned — but the relationship between players and developers broke down.

We can formalize the theoretical claim as follows:

Fluid breakdowns do not disrupt functionality categorically but transfer maintenance labor onto users.

When this labor accumulates without acknowledgment or reciprocal repair, it becomes affectively and politically charged.

Users begin to interpret neglected breakdowns as symptoms of structural disrespect.

This interpretive shift transforms infrastructural irritation into a breakdown of social relation. ∴ Therefore, fluid breakdowns can lead to the emergence of publics demanding collective repair — not just of systems, but of relational dignity.

This reframes identity politics not as a struggle for authenticity or recognition alone, but as a demand for infrastructural respect. It is not only “who I am” that is at stake, but whether my enacted identity is being sustained or exploited. The model of identity-as-fluid-object allows us to see that political rupture often emerges not from dramatic crises, but from the slow erosion of trust in the infrastructures that make relational life possible.

4. Reinterpreting Kong’s Cases through the Fluid Breakdown Lens

Rather than dispersing analysis across all of Kong’s examples, we here focus on the figure of the “Asian North American philosopher” — a case that makes visible the precise dynamic of fluid breakdown.

At first glance, the philosopher’s identity appears functional. She teaches, publishes, mentors, and participates in her scholarly community. But this surface stability conceals a deeper functional load: her identity-object does not merely reflect who she is, but actively sustains a world in which she is legible, credible, and safe. In this sense, her identity maintains a livable epistemic world — one where she can both do philosophy and be recognized as doing so.

This world is not merely symbolic. It includes patterns of recognition, everyday routines of academic life, mentorship relationships, and a broader sense of purpose. The identity’s function, then, is not only expressive but infrastructural: it organizes a form of life that is socially legible, affectively coherent, and existentially sustainable.

This function requires constant calibration: appearing rigorous but not angry, critical but not parochial, diverse but not divisive. Her presence mediates between conflicting expectations — institutional norms, student hopes, racialized scrutiny — to keep that world coherent enough to inhabit. This is the identity’s primary function: to hold together the fragile relational infrastructure that makes her philosophical life possible.

But this function is not self-sustaining. Like the Bush Pump, the identity continues to operate only through continuous, distributed repair — except here, the distribution collapses. No policy, mentor, or norm absorbs the wear. The philosopher alone must manage the leaks: being tokenized in committees, dismissed as non-philosophical, asked to mentor excessively, or to represent “perspectives.” These are not symbolic tensions — they are localized breakdowns in identity’s infrastructural functionality.

Early on, she may interpret this labor as care — for students, for the field, for herself. But over time, the imbalance accumulates: maintenance begins to feel less like agency and more like unpaid debt. The object continues to “work,” but she begins to question whether she should be the only one keeping it intact.

This is the moment where attachment begins to curdle. The philosopher’s continued labor is not simply professional commitment; it is driven by a deep attachment to the epistemic world her identity sustains. This world is not merely strategic but affectively saturated — a place where her intellectual and existential coherence reside. She does not merely maintain the object — she cannot yet walk away from it.

Yet as no reciprocal maintenance appears — no institutional support, no structural relief — her attachment begins to shift. What was once interpreted as fidelity now appears as neglect. What was once love now feels like abandonment. Her maintenance labor, once an act of contribution, begins to register as coerced compensation for others’ indifference. The identity-object still “works,” but only because she works harder.

Here, identity breakdown is not about dysfunction, but about refusal to continue compensating for dysfunction without recognition or redress. The philosopher stops patching the cracks and starts pointing to them. She stops performing functionality and starts organizing for repair. Her relation to identity transforms — not because she changes, but because her labor is reinterpreted.

Attachment as Precondition of Maintenance, and the Politics of Refusal

The philosopher’s labor is not freely disposable — it is bound to a lifeworld that her identity makes possible. She remains because that world is livable, and detaching would entail existential loss. As with Mabinogi players who stay not due to hedonic preference but because the game constitutes their emotional and historical reality, her continued effort reflects an entangled dependence.

This is what renders the fluid breakdown ambiguous. It does not announce itself as failure. Instead, it accumulates where the identity still “works,” but only because she works harder. She absorbs the instability — not because she is naive, but because she is attached.

But as labor accumulates without reciprocation — no mentoring for her, no institutional buffering, no uptake of the world she helps maintain — her interpretive frame shifts. Love becomes abandonment; contribution becomes coerced caretaking. The identity she upheld becomes the site of unreciprocated demand.

This is not collapse but refusal. She ceases maintenance not because the identity fails, but because she no longer consents to maintain it alone. Her reinterpretation is political cognition: she identifies the asymmetry, names it as injustice, and calls for collective repair.

This directly parallels the Mabinogi case. Players did not revolt when the game crashed, but when they saw that their labor masked systemic disregard. What sustained them — attachment — also fueled protest. In both cases, livable worlds are not abandoned easily. They must be betrayed first.

Thus, fluid breakdown is not dysfunction but disproportion. When maintenance becomes solitary and invisible, the livable world reveals its political architecture. And from that recognition, publics emerge — not to destroy the object, but to make its care collective.

5. From Breakdown to Public Maintenance: Collective Identity as Political Demand

A final step in this model is to examine how the breakdown of a shared social identity can exceed the individual and generate a collective response. If identity is a fluid object — maintained through distributed care, and vulnerable to structural degradation — then its failure can become the condition for public mobilization.

Consider how a racialized professional identity like "Asian philosopher" might, when continually strained by underrepresentation, stereotyping, and misrecognition, come to be experienced not only as a personal burden but as a systemic defect. When the labor of maintaining intelligibility, recognition, and coherence is experienced as unfairly individualized — and when institutions fail to distribute that labor — subjects may come to perceive not just their own suffering, but the object’s failure to work collectively.

In such moments, what begins as a dispersed sense of breakdown may coalesce into a demand for maintenance. This demand is political. It seeks not only the recognition of the identity but the restructuring of its material supports — changes in representation, pedagogy, mentorship, and norms of disciplinary legitimacy. The emergence of a public around a broken identity-object parallels what Latour and Marres call issue-publics: collectives that form not around shared beliefs, but around shared concerns over fragile or failing infrastructures.

This process links identity to both power-over and power-to. The failure of a marginalized identity often stems from power-over: the unacknowledged dominance of institutional norms, epistemic exclusions, and social silencing. But the collective response — the demand for maintenance — is an assertion of power-to: the capacity to act together to sustain and reconstruct the identity-object. It is the shift from individualized survival to collective preservation, from endurance to transformation.

Understood this way, identity politics is not only about recognition or self-expression; it is a politics of repair and shared upkeep. It reframes identity as an object whose livability requires not only freedom from domination, but also the active construction and renewal of enabling conditions. A broken identity-object, when held together by a public, becomes the very site of democratic action.

6. Coalition Without Homogeneity: Responding to Fragmentation Objections

A common critique of intersectional identity-based politics is that it encourages fragmentation rather than solidarity: if every identity group becomes its own site of maintenance and contestation, then what prevents collective action across those groups? Doesn’t the fluid object model, by focusing on the specificity and partiality of identity breakdowns, reinforce siloed publics rather than unified coalitions?

This objection misunderstands the ontology of fluid objects and the politics of attachment. If identities are not metaphysical essences but fragile assemblages held together through shared labor and care, then coalition is not achieved through sameness, but through shared experience of maintenance. Groups with distinct identities — e.g., queer disabled people, Black feminists, undocumented workers — may encounter different breakdowns, but they all face the structural concentration of maintenance labor on the marginalized subject.

In this light, coalition emerges not despite difference but because of structurally analogous relations to breakdown and repair. Maintaining identity under systemic pressure becomes a common ground for solidarity, not a source of isolation. Furthermore, fluid objects often overlap, intersect, and co-condition one another — a breakdown in one identity assemblage can ripple across others. Thus, publics organized around one failing identity-object can become aware of and entangled with others, forming chains of concern and shared action.

Importantly, this model explains how identity-based publics can maintain incompatible demands, distinct interests, and even conflicting attachments, while still recognizing the political urgency of mutual reinforcement. The model does not assume a convergence of values or priorities; rather, it identifies solidarity in the recognition that all identity objects require upkeep, and that these labors are structurally neglected and unequally distributed. Just as technical infrastructures do not need to be uniform to require shared maintenance, identity infrastructures can sustain difference while inviting collaboration.

In STS terms, this is not a politics of abstract unity, but of networked fragility. Coalition is a form of infrastructural alliance: not the fusion of identities into a meta-identity, but the linking of maintenance struggles through mutual recognition of precarity. Such coalitions are dynamic, responsive, and materially grounded — and they point toward a pluralistic, non-hierarchical model of solidarity that meets the challenge of intersectional justice without ontological totalization.

7. Anticipating Objections: Future Directions for Clarification

In what follows, I sketch three types of objections that might be raised against the fluid object model of identity. These are not fully resolved here, but I indicate pathways for response:

(1) Ontological Incoherence Objection: One might argue that the concept of a "fluid object" is too unstable to underwrite meaningful identity claims. If identity is constantly shifting, how can it serve as a basis for recognition, political demand, or moral accountability?

Response Direction: The strength of the fluid object model lies precisely in its account of stability-in-fragility. It does not deny coherence; it relocates coherence to the site of maintenance. Identity persists not because it is essence-like, but because it is sustained. This model helps explain how identities function even when fractured — through affective attachment and infrastructural support.

(2) Individualization of Burden Objection: Critics might claim that this model risks placing too much focus on the individual’s labor of maintenance, reinforcing neoliberal norms of self-care and responsibilization.

Response Direction: The model’s point is not to normalize individualized labor but to diagnose its injustice. The aim is to expose how the invisibilized burden of identity upkeep is a form of structural inequality — one that must be collectivized and politically addressed, not valorized.

(3) Anti-Intersectional Critique: Some may worry that focusing on discrete identity-objects will prevent intersectional analysis. If each identity is treated as an object with its own maintenance logic, are we not recreating silos?

Response Direction: Fluid objects are not bounded silos — they are overlapping infrastructures. Intersectionality is precisely the site where multiple breakdowns co-occur and compound. The fluid object model can clarify how certain subjects experience the convergence of maintenance burdens, thereby enriching intersectional analysis with a material ontology of structural stress.

These directions suggest that the model’s greatest strength — its commitment to complexity and lived precariousness — is also the source of its political potential.

8. Conclusion: Toward a Politics of Shared Maintenance

This paper has proposed an alternative model of social identity grounded in the ontology of fluid objects. Against static and essentialist accounts, and in partial departure from Kong’s navigational process model, I have argued that social identities are best understood as fragile, materially enacted assemblages that require ongoing maintenance. This model preserves the insights of decolonial and feminist accounts of identity while adding a theoretical vocabulary for breakdown, repair, infrastructural distribution, and collective obligation.

By emphasizing maintenance rather than coherence, and attachment rather than essence, we gain a more realistic and politically generative framework for theorizing how identities survive under conditions of marginalization. The labor of keeping an identity livable — and the injustice of bearing that labor alone — becomes central to our critique of power. Moreover, the very sites of breakdown can become grounds for the emergence of publics, coalitions, and solidarity movements committed to shared repair.

In this view, intersectional justice is not a matter of fitting diverse experiences into a stable ontological schema. It is about recognizing and addressing the differentiated burdens of identity maintenance, the politics of their distribution, and the potential for cross-identity alliance rooted in mutual precarity and co-sustained care. What we need is not purity, but durability — not unity, but shared upkeep. Identity, as a fluid object, demands not only recognition but repair.
