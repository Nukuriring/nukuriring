The Impossibility of Self-Blindness and Its Implication

The second lecture of Sydney Shoemaker’s 1994 lecture series entitled “Self-Knowledge and ‘Inner Sense’” is chiefly a criticism of the “inner sense” view on self-knowledge based on what he calls the “broad perceptual model”—the view according to which our introspective awareness of our own mental states are caused by those mental states via a certain causal mechanism, and relatedly, those mental states could exist even if we lacked such a causal mechanism and thereby were unable to be introspectively aware of them. Shoemaker’s criticism consists logically of two parts. The first part is where he argues that what he calls “self-blindness” is impossible. The second part is where he argues that the inner sense view implies that self-blindness is possible. In this paper I will summarize the criticism by explaining the notion of self-blindness and then describing each of the two parts. I will then make some critical comments.

What is “self-blindness”? For a creature to be self-blind to a certain kind of mental states is for it to be unable to be introspectively aware of those mental states while at the same time having the normal rational or conceptual capacity so that it may appreciate and learn about those mental states. Roughly saying, a self-blind creature is one who has both the rational capacity and the introspective incapacity at once.

The first part is where Shoemaker argues that self-blindness is impossible, which means that the rational capacity and the introspective incapacity are incompatible, or that the rational capacity implies the introspective capacity. In arguing for this, he attempts to convince us that, with respect to each of the various kinds of mental states—pain, perceptual experiences, intentions, and beliefs and desires—it is inconceivable that there be a creature who has the rational capacity but at the same time is introspectively ignorant of mental states of the kind in question.

Regarding pain, he points out that, much of the behaviors that a rational creature must exhibit insofar as it is truly in pain—behaviors the lack of which would indicate that the subject lacks either pain or the rational capacity, such as taking some measures to end it—presupposes that the subject is introspectively aware of the pain in question.

Regarding perceptual experiences, he suggests that, the sensitivity which certain beliefs of a rational creature are to display with respect to perceptual experiences—the sensitivity such that when it does not hold between one’s certain beliefs and one’s perceptual environment one cannot even be said to have the relevant perceptual experiences—presupposes that the subject is introspectively aware of her perceptual experiences.

Regarding intentions, subtleties aside, he argues that, whenever we try to imagine a creature who claims that it is introspectively ignorant of what it intends, we are inclined to regard the subject of that claim and the subject of the intention as distinct subjects from each other—which means self-blindness to intentions is inconceivable.

Regarding beliefs and desires, what Shoemaker puts forward are two arguments that are independent of each other. He argues, on one hand, that a rational creature with some beliefs and desires will, thanks to its recognition of the cooperative relationship between rational creatures, necessarily be disposed to manifest those beliefs and desires to others. And this manifestation will easily include, he claims, utterances of words that self-ascribe the introspective awareness of those beliefs and desires, which means that the creature is not self-blind to those states. He also argues, on the other hand, that any rational creature must be capable of evaluating and revising its beliefs and desires, and this capacity presupposes that the creature is introspectively aware of those beliefs and desires. Therefore, Shoemaker concludes, self-blindness is impossible; any creature with the rational capacity cannot but have the introspective capacity as well.

The second part, where he argues that the inner sense view implies that self-blindness is possible, is presented only in quite a sketchy fashion. His argument in this part is, or can be reconstructed as, the following: According to the inner sense view, the relation between mental states and the introspective access to them is a causal one. But any two existences causally related to each other are distinct from, and thus cannot be necessarily connected with, each other. It is possible, therefore, for any creature who has some mental states to at the same time lack the introspective access to them. This seems to mean that self-blindness is possible. Let me now make some brief comments. I doubt, in regard to the first part, whether the rational capacity—that is, what Shoemaker calls “normal intelligence, rationality, and conceptual capacity” (for example, on page 284)—is really rich enough for it to necessitate all what he takes it to necessitate: “pain-behaviors” (p. 274), the sensitivity of certain beliefs to perceptual experiences, the disposition to manifest one’s beliefs and desires to others, and the capacity of evaluating and revising one’s beliefs and desires, and so on.

I also doubt, again in regard to the first part, whether a disposition to exhibit what can be seen as evidence of a certain capacity (the disposition to manifest one’s own beliefs and desires, in this lecture) can be easily identified with the very capacity itself (the introspective awareness of one’s own beliefs and desires, in this lecture). I will not, however, go deep into these complex and controversial issues here.

What I find particularly unconvincing is the second part, where Shoemaker takes the possibility of self-blindness to be a consequence of the inner sense view (stated explicitly on page 273).

Let us be more exact and careful: The possibility the view implies is not the possibility of a rational creature devoid of the introspective capacity (a self-blind creature), but just the possibility of a creature devoid of the introspective capacity (whether it is even rational, and thus self-blind, or not); the view never is committed to whether or not a creature devoid of the introspective capacity could still be rational.

In this respect, proponents of this view would have no difficulty in recognizing the impossibility of a self-blind creature, namely the impossibility of a rational creature devoid of the introspective capacity. For their part, they could embrace the impossibility of self-blindness by saying something like the following: We admit that it is possible that a creature lacks the very causal mechanism, and thereby lacks the introspective capacity. This much is surely what is implied by our inner sense view. Apart from this, however, any creature that lacks the introspective capacity will thereby necessarily lacks the rational capacity as well, for, as Shoemaker rightly observes, self-blindness is impossible. It may be that the causal mechanism that makes the introspective access available is at the same time what endows us with the rational capacity.
