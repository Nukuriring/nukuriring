(1) If we understand the notion of infrence narrowly, so that we think of a transition from premises to a conclusion in accordance with a certain rule as an inference only if those premises provide a ground or evidence for the conclusion, the proponents of the so-called ‘inferential’ approach will deny that they are committed to an inferential view. (2) If we understand the notion of inference broadly, so that inference is “a non-accidental transition between belief contents, where the reasonableness of the transition is open to assessment” (Boyle 2011, p.227), then the proponents of the inferential approach can accept that he takes an inferential view. How to Formulate the Closure Principle for Knowledge

· The Standard Formulation before Williamson (2000) and Hawthorne (2004)

(Closure) Necessarily, if S knows that p and S knows that p entails q, then S knows that q.

· Problem for the Standard Formulation

“If at t, I know that p and know that p entails q, I may still have to do something – namely perform a deductive inference – in order to come to know that q. Until I perform that inference, I do not know that q. At any rate, that seems to be the natural view of the matter. Williamson’s statement of the closure idea is thus preferable: ‘knowing p1, . . . , pn, competently deducing q, and thereby coming to believe q is in general a way of coming to know q.’ (Williamson 2000: 117).” (Hawthorn (2004), p.32).

(e.g.) Smith knows that he has 10 fingers. Smith knows that if his number of fingers is 10, then his number of fingers is not prime. But Smith does not actually deduce and form the belief that his number of fingers is not prime. Hence Smith does not know that his number of fingers is not prime.

- The Multi-Premise Closure

(MPC) Necessarily, if S knows p1, . . . , pn, competently deduces q, and thereby comes to believe q, while retaining knowlege of p1, . . . , pn throughout, then S knows q.

- The Single-Premise Closure

(SPC) Necessarily, if S knows p, competently deduces q, and thereby comes to believe q, while retaining knowledge of p throughout, then S knows q.

Boyle (2019), “Transparency and Reflection”

· Boyle’s Main Thesis

- Main Thesis

We can solve the “puzzle” or problem of transparency by adopting (i) the Sartrean distinction between ‘positional’ and ‘non-positional’ consciousness, as well as (ii) his idea that all our, ‘positional’ consciousness of the world essentially involves ‘non-positional’ consciousness of our own consciousness.

“Sartre claimed, in effect, that our capacity to know our own minds is linked to our capacity to know the world because our awareness of the world constitutively invovles a kind of implicit self-awareness, and that we draw on this awareness when we reflect.” (p.1014; emphasis added)

- From tacit knowledge to ‘non-positional’ awareness

In this paper, Boyle gives up his previous proposal in Boyle (2011) that one who believes that p already has tacit knowledge that one believes that p, and instead proposes that one who believes that p already has ‘non-positional’ awareness of one’s belief that p.

- Boyle develops his Sartrean account of self-knowledge while criticizing three prominent approaches to the problem of transparency, namely Moran’s, Byrne’s and Peacocke’s, as well as criticizing his early version of the ‘reflectivist’ approach based on the notion of tacit knowledge.

· Transparency (‘투명성’, ‘투과성’?) as a Solution and as a Problem

- The phenomenon of transparency

“I seem to be able to know various facts about my own mind simply by considering questions about the non-mental world. To obtain this knowledge, I look, as it were, not inward but outward.” (p.1013)

- Transparency as a solution to the problem of self-knowledge

“Evans regarded his observations [about tranparency] as demystifying our capactify for certain kinds of privileged self-knowledge. It can seem mysterious how we are able to say what we believe without observing ourselves, even though we must observe another person to determine what she believes; but, Evans suggests, this should not seem strange once we recognize that a person can answer the question whether she believes p by ‘putting into operation whatever procedure [she has] for answering the question whether p’. For whether where p is a proposition about the non-mental world, it is no surprise that a subject can answer the question whether p without needing evidence about her own psychology.” (p.1013; emphasis added)

- Transparency as a problem

“Many subsequent writers, however, have thought of Evans’s observations, not primarily as providing the solution to a puzzle [of self-knowledge], but as presenting a puzzle in their own right. [=the puzzle of transprency]. . . .[W]hat justifies me in answering such questions about my own psychology by looking to seemingly independent facts about the world?” (p.1013-4)

- Byrne’s main idea

We can acquire transparent self-knowledge by making an inference from world to mind. This inference is neutral (=its premise does not presuppose self-knowledge), reliable, and safe, and accordingly is knowledge-conducive.

· Comments on Peacocke’s Account

- Peacocke’s main idea

A subject’s basis for self-ascribing a belief that p is not sheer awareness that p but awareness of her judgment that p.

(Step 1) Considers whether p or not.

(Step 2) judges that p.

(Step 3) is conscious of her judging that p.

(Step 4) believes that one believes that p

Accroding to Peacoke a subject’ belief that she believes that p amounts to knowledge because awareness of her juding that p is a normally reliable indicator that one believes that p.

- Problems

(Problem 1) Consciously judging that p and knowing one’s belief that p as two separate awarenesses?

“For my part, I am skeptical of the intelligibility of this notion of conscious judgment. . . Judging

that p requires not merely inwardly affirming that p (whatever that might mean), but affirming

p in the conviction that p is true. And it is hard to see how this can mean anything less than:

it requires inwardly expressing one’s belief that p. But then it is hard to see how my

consciousness of juding that p can provide me with an independently-available ground for

believing that I believe that p.” (pp.1025-26).

(Problem 2) Does this account provide non-spectatorial knowledge of one’s own beliefs?

“If I assert ‘I believe that p’ on this sort of basis, it is surely a speculation about myself, not a conscious expression of conviction that p. In locating my ground for self-ascribing a belief in a fallible indicator of belief, Peacocke’s account drives a wedge between my consciously taking p to be true (which I express in judging that p) and my knowledge that I believe p (which I infer from, but am not entitled to identify with, my conscious judgment that p). What I can acquire by this method, if anything, is a bit of information about myself, not a consciously held stance on the question whether p.” (p.1026; emphases added)

· Summary of Boyle’s Comments on Moran, Byrne, and Peacocke

(1) Comments on Moran

- Moran is wrong to think that self-knowledge is essentially related to our capacity for deliberation.

- But Moran’s account of self-knowledge contains an important sight that genuinely transparent

self-knowledge is non-spectatorial and non-alientated in the sense that “it is not merely

arrived at by considering whether p; it remains a mode of knowing in which I (self-consciously)

look outward.” (p.1026)

“This, I believe, is the deeper sense in which our knowledge of our own minds can be transparent: not merely that it can be based on a consideration of the world, but that it can consist simply in a self-consciousness stance toward the world, not an independent knowledge of one’s holding such a stance.” (p.1026; emphases added)

(2) Comment on Byrne

- Byrne is wrong to think a subject’s basis for transparent self-knowledge is a sheer proposition about the world. Such a basis could at best provide the subject with a spectatorial, alienated knowledge of her own mind.

- But Byrne is right in his insistence that transparent selt-knowledge must look outward, not inward.

(3) Comment on Peacocke

- Peacocke is wrong to think that a subject’s basis for transparent self-knowledge is a conscious event that serves as an indicator of her own belief. Such a basis could at best provide the subject with a spectatorial, alienated knowledge of her own mind.

- But Peacocke is right that the basis for transparent self-knowledge must not be a sheer awareness of the world.

· Interim Conclusion, and a Question

- Interim conclusion

If genuinely transparent self-knowledge is to be possible, there must a category of awareness that is “outward”, namely awareness of the world, but not merely an awareness of the world. It must be a sort of self-awareness in the sense that it is involved in the subject’s ways of representing the world .

· Boyle’s Earlier Proposal and Its Problems

- Earlier Proposal:

“[T]he existence of the relevant mental states (perception, belief, intention, etc.) itself involves the subject’s ‘taci knowledge’ of their existence, so that all the subject needs to do to achieve explicit knowledge of these states is to reflect on what she already knows.” (p.1027)

- Problems

(Problem 1) ‘Tacit’ knowledge?

First horn of the dilemma

“[O]n the one hand, if a subject already knows (e.g.) that she believes that p, mustn’t this involve her representing herself as believing that p? But then in what sense can this knowledge be ‘tacit’, other than the inconsequential sense that she has not yet verbalized it?” (p.1027)

Second horn of the dilemma

“[O]n the other hand, if the subject’s knowledge that she believes p is truly tacit, in what sense can she be said already [to] know this at all? States of knowledge are standardly thought to be individuated by their propositional objects. If a subject does not yet know ‘explicitly’ that she believes that p, what can this mean but that she does not yet possess this particular piece of knowledge?” (p.1027)

※ 이 경우 지식의 내용은 <나는 p를 믿고 있다>라는 명제적 내용이므로, 이른바 know-how 개념이나 practical knowledge 개념에 호소해서 여기서 문제가 되는 ‘tacit knowledge’ 개념을 해명하기는 어려워보인다.

(Problem 2) Must we possess the concept of belief in order to have beliefs?

“Knowledge is commonly assumed to be subject to the following Concept Possession Requirement:

(CPR) A subject can know that p only if she possesses the concepts necessary for understanding the proposition that p.

If the tacit knowledge invoked by reflectivism were subject to (CPR), then the reflectivist would be

committed to holding that a subject can have beliefs only if she possesses the concept of belief. But

this is an implausibly strong intellectual requirement on belief.” (p.1028)

- Boyle’s New Proposal

“I. . .held our capacity for explicit, reflective knowledge of our own mental states to be grounded in another, more basic mode of awareness. I called this more basic awareness ‘tacit knowledge’, but I have come to think it better to follow Sartre in calling it ‘non-positional consciousness’, reserving the term ‘knowledge’ for an awareness that posits its object. . . Characterizing the relevant awareness as ‘non-positional consciousness’ does not by itself provide the needed clarification, but it at least marks the spot where clarification is needed.” (p.1030)

· Satre on the Nature of Consciousness: Four Theses

- (S1) “All consciousness [conscience] . . . is consciousness of something. This means that there is no consciousness that is not a positing [position] of a transcendental object.”

- (S2) “[A]ll knowing consciousness [conscience connaissante] can be knowledge [connaissance] of nothing but its object.”

- (S3) “[A]ll positional consciousness of an object is at the same time non-positional consciousness of itself.”

- (S4) “[I]t is the non-reflective consciousness pconscience non-réflexive] that makes reflection possible.”

· Sartre’s Argument for (S3) and Its Problem

- Sartre’s argument

“If my consciousness were not consciousness of being conscious of the table, then it would be

consciouisness of the table without consciousness of being such, i.e. a consciousness ignorant

of itself, an unconscious consciousness – which is absurd.” (p.1029)

- Problem for Sartre’s argument

What Sartre calls an “unconscious consciousness”, which does sound absurd, is in fact a consciouness of an object without consciousness of that very state of consciousness. This seems to be a perfectly coherent notion.

· 투명성 현상, 즉 내가 세계의 사태들만을 고려(의식)함으로써 내 심리상태에 대한 지식을 획득할 수 있는 이유는,

(i) 내가 세계의 사태들을 의식할 때 나는 그 사태들을 반드시 어떤 특정한 방식(manner)으로 의식해야 하고,

(ii) 나는 내가 세계의 사태들을 의식하는 방식을 (비정립적으로) 의식하고 있어야 하며

※ ’정립적’ 의식 = 세계의 사태들에 대한 의식 = 내용(content)이 있는 의식

‘비정립적’ 의식 = 세계의 사태들을 의식하는 방식에 대한 의식 = 내용이 없는 의식

(iii) 내가 세계의 사태들을 의식하는 각 방식들이 그것에 대응되는 심리상태들을 전제하고 있기 때문이다.

· (ii)에 대한 해명: 내가 세계의 사태들을 의식하는 방식에 대한 나의 깨달음(의식)이 비정립적인 의식인 이유

내가 세계의 사태들을 의식할 때, 나의 의식 내용에 내가 그 사태들을 의식하는 방식이

포함되어 있지는 않다.

(예) 내가 <한국의 수도는 서울이다>라는 세계의 사태를 의식할 때, 나의 의식의 내용인 <한국의 수도는 서울이다>에 내가 이 사태을 의식하는 방식(=상상이나 가정, 의혹이 아니라 믿고 있는 방식으로 이 사태를 의식함)이 포함되어 있지는 않다. 그러므로 내가 이 사태를 의식하는 방식에 대한 의식은 내 의식의 내용에서 어떤 대상으로 정립되어 있지 않으며, 그러한 의미에서 내가 이 사태를 의식하는 방식에 대한 의식은 정립적 의식이 아니라 비정립적 의식이다.

내가 <철학적 문제들은 해결책을 찾을 수 있다>라는 세계의 사태에 대해 의혹을 가질 때, 나의 의식의 내용인 <철학적 문제들은 해결책을 찾을 수 있다>에 내가 이 사태를 의식하는 방식 (= 믿음이나 상상, 가정이 아니라 의혹한다는 방식으로 이 사태를 의식함)은 포함되어 있지 않다.  그러므로 내가 이 사태를 의식하는 방식에 대한 의식은 내 의식의 내용에서 어떤 대상으로 정립되어 있지 않으며, 그러한 의미에서 내가 이 사태를 의식하는 방식에 대한 의식은 정립적 의식이 아니라 비정립적 의식이다.

내가 <나는 시험에 떨어질 것이다>라는 세계의 (미래) 사태를 의도할 때, 나의 의식의 내용인 <나는 시험에 떨어질 것이다>에 내가 이 사태를 의식하는 방식 (=믿음, 상상, 가정, 지각이 아니라 의도한다는 방식으로 이 사태를 의식함) 은 포함되어 있지 않다. 그러므로 내가 이 사태를 의식하는 방식에 대한 의식은 내 의식의 내용에서 어떤 대상으로 정립되어 있지 않으며, 그러한 의미에서 내가 이 사태를 의식하는 방식에 대한 의식은 정립적 의식이 아니라 비정립적 의식이다.

· Reconstruction of Boyle’s Argument for (S3)

- Our psychological states including intention, perception, and belief are such that they are not

merely possessed by us but express our manners of representing the world.

- Therefore, when I am aware of the world in such and such manners, these awarenesses implicitly presuppose those psychological states. We might say the relevant psychological states are expressed “non-positionally” in those awarenesses of the world.

· Example 1: Intention

- Suppose I think about the world, in this case about my future action, and think that I will .

“I do not explicitly ascribe an intention to myself; rather, I think that I will  in a manner that implicitly presupposes such an intention. The fact that I now intend to do this is, we might say, expressed ‘non-positionally’ in [“I will ”]: It is not made explicit in my thought, but it is also not something to which I am oblivious. My awareness of it will come out in the specific kinds of grounds I consider for propositions like [“I will ”], and the specific kinds of consequences I draw from them. My grounds will speak primarily to the desirability of -ing, rather than to the evidential question whether it will be the case that I . And I will draw consequences, not about what I am likely to do, but about what else I must do in order to  and how my -ing should affect my other plans. I will, in short, treat such propositions in ways which indicate that I understand them to express decisions rather than mere predictions. But this understanding will be expressed, not in my explicitly thinking I intend to , but in my distinctive way of thinking of my future -ing.” (p.1031; emphases added)

· Example 2: Perception

- Suppose I think about the world by perceiving that this cat is purring.

“[C]onsider the way the cat is represented in [“This cat is purring”]: It is presented as available for demonstrative reference, expressible using a ‘this’: Now, an object is available to a given subject for this sort of demonstrative reference only in specific kinds of circumstances. Although a cat may be purring, I cannot successfully think of it as this cat when it is miles away, or hidden behind a screen, or known to me only by hearsay. Philosophers commonly call such a ‘this’ a ‘perceptual demonstrative’ precisely because it expresses a mode of demonstration that is available just when the relevant object is perceived. [. . .] Nevertheless, a subject who thinks [“This cat is purring”] on the basis of perceptual consciousness does not think that she perceives the relevant cat: the only object she thinks about is the cat. [. . .] [But] her manner of thinking about this cat presupposes that she perceives it. We might therefore say that her perceptual relation to the cat is expressed ‘non-positionally’ in her thought: it is not posited, but it is a presupposition of the soundness of what is posited.” (p.1033)

“So again, we have a mode of consciousness of the world that is possible only in virtue of non-positional consciousness of one’s own consciousness. And if the subject goes on to think the reflective thought [“I perceive a purring cat”], she will be making explicit a psychological state whose presence was already presupposed in her world-directed representation of the cat.” (p.1033; emphases added).

· Example 3: Belief

- Suppose I wonder whether there will be a third world war and concludes: “There will be a third

world war”.

“What I conclude here is a proposition about the non-mental world, but my manner of representing this proposition differs from the way I would represent it if I were merely supposing [“There will be a third world war”] for the sake of argument, imagining a possible world in which it held true, etc. Moreover, subjects who can deliberate competently about factual questions must have an implicit awareness of their manner of representing such propositions. For such subjects must be able to distinguish between propositions represented in the assertoric mode of belief and those represented in other modes. [. . .] We might therefore say that in concluding that there will be a third world war, she expresses a non-positional consciousness of her own belief: an awareness that figures, not as the object of her thought, but as the necessary background of her thinking of the question of whether there will be a thirld world war as settled. If the deliberating subject then goes on to think the reflective thought [“I believe that there will be a third world war”], she will be making her attitude on the question explicit, but this awareness was already implicit in her world-directed representation of the likelihood of a third world war.” (pp.1034~35).

· Reconstruction of the Central Ideas in Boyle’s New Proposal

(1) When a subject is aware of the world in her intention, perception, belief, etc., she must be aware of the world in specific manners, for those mental states are not merely items possessed by the subject but the states from whose perspective the subject approaches the world.

(2) Moreover, the subject must be implicitly aware of the manners in which she is aware of the world, for otherwise she cannot be aware of the world in proper ways.

(3) Since each manner in which the subject is aware of the world corresponds to a certain mental state she is in, and since she is implicitly aware of the manner, she can make it explicit by self-ascribing the mental state that corresponds to the manner in which she is aware of the world.

and “active” kinds of self-knowledge.

- Our knowledge of our own perceptual experience belongs to a “passive” kind of self-knowledge,

for we cannot modify or revise our perceptual experience through deliberation.

· Byrne’s Initial Replies to Boyle’s Critique that an Inference with the Doxastic Schema is “Mad”

- Two possible ways of understanding Boyle’s critique

(First Unerstanding)

Knowledge must be derived from adequate evidence. But ‘p’ is not adequate evidence for ‘I believe p’. Therefore, an inference in accordance with the doxastic schema cannot yield knowledge.

(Second Understanding)

Knowledge cannot be derived from inadequate evidence. But ‘p’ is inadequate evidence for ‘I believe p. Therefore, an inference in accordance with the doxastic schema cannot yield knowledge.

- Byrne’s Replies

(1) Reply to the (First Understanding)

“Far from being a problem, this seems to be exactly what we want. The view that self-knowledge is (often) not based on evidence is frequently taken as a starting point for any theory, from Armstrong to Moran.” (p.123)

(2) Reply to the (Second Understanding)

One can indeed acquire knowledge based on inadequate evidence as long as the process that produced one’s belief is reliable and the belief is safe. An inference in accordance with the epistemic rule bel is indeed reliable, and the belief produced by bel is indeed safe.

· Byrne’s Replies to Boyle’s Critiqe Based on the First-Person Perspective

- Byrne’s reconstruction of Boye’s critique based on the first-person perspective

“Specifically, when I ask myself what grounds I have for drawing the conclusion that I believe that p, a little reflection appears to tell me that my sole premise was that p, which is manifestly not a reason, or item of evidence, that supports my conclusion.” (p.123)

- Byrne’s reply: Unlike knowledge of our own mental states, knowledge of inferences followed by

us need not be evident.

“There is little evidence that knowlede of one’s inference is especially easy to come by, and

mistakes are to be expected. This is all to the good, for supposing that one’s inferences are evident, the transparency account, if true, must also be evident. Since the account is not evident, if one’s inferences are evident then the account is false.” (p.114)

- Possible response on behalf of Boyle:

“Granted that first-person reflection is not going to exhibit the instability, what about that combined with a dose of theory as, say, presented in this chapter? Suppose my first-person and theoretical reflections combied ‘tell me that my sole premise was that p’: wouldn’t that lead to a rational demolition of my self-knowledge along the same lines?” (p.124; italics added)

- Byrne’s reply

“No. As argued earlier, this is a good inferenc in the sense of being knowledge-conducive [=reliable and safe]. Boyle has not directly attacked these earlier arguments. Applying this to the present case, if I am rational I will conclude that although my second-order belief is unsupported, that is no reason for ditching it. There is consequently no instability” (p.124; some italics added)

Some Concluding Remarks

· The Problem of Self-Knowledge in the 21st Century

- From Descartes to logical positivism, it has been generally assumed that knowledge of our own mental states is the least problematic of all categories of knowledge, and that a central task of epistemology has been to justify our knowledge of the external world and knowledge of other minds based on knowledge of our own mental states.

- In today’s epistemology, however, it is no longer supposed that knowledge of the external world and knowledge of other minds are particularly problematic. Skepticism about these kinds of knowledge is no longer a major concern for epistemologists.

- On the other hand, it has become difficult to explain the supposedly special nature of self-knowledge for various reasons, including

(1) the rise of physicalism in metaphysics

(2) the influence of the ‘naturalized epistemology’ movement

(3) the wide acceptance of externalism about mental content in philosophy of mind

* If externalism about mental content is right, then it seems that knowing about what we think or believe requires knowing about the relevant facts in the environment.

* But the latter kind of knowledge requires empirical observation of the external world. Hence we cannot have knowledge about what we think or believe merely by introspection. But it has been traditionally understood that self-knowledge is the kind of knowledge we acquire by introspection alone. Therefore, there is no such thing as self-knowledge as it has been traditionally understood.

(4) results from psychological research that seem to cast doubt on the privileged epistemic status of self-knowledge

· Final Comments on Transparency Theories of Self-Knolwedge

- Transparency theories of self-knowledge, whether they are in the form of inferentialist approach

or in the form of ‘rational agency’ or ‘reflectivist’ approach, attempt to explain the apparently

distinctive features of our self-knowledge, namely its epistemic authority and the special method

of acquiring it, while supposing that self-knowledge is intimately related to our knowledge of the

external world.

- In particular, transparency theories of self-knowledge do not require any special epistemic faculty with which we acquire self-knowledge.

- If transparency theories of self-knowledge are on the right track, the content of our self-knowledge, for example my knowledge that I believe that water quenches thirst, will be inherited from the content of the relevant mental state, namely my belief that water quenches thirst.

- If so, it seems that transparency theories of self-knowledge can give a nice explanation of how we can have ‘privileged access’ to our own mental states even if their contents are “external”.
