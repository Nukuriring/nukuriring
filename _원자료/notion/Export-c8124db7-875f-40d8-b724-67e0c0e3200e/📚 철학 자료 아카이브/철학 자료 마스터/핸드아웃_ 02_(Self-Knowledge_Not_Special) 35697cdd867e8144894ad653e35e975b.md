Epistemic Safety and Sensitivity

Two modal conditions for knowledge, safety and sensitivity, are widely discussed in contemporary epistemology. Some philosophers claim that S’s true belief is knowledge only if it is safe (Sosa, Pritchard, Williamson), while others claim that S’s true belief is knowledge only if it is sensitive (Nozick).

Epistemic Safety 운에 의해서 참인 믿음을 얻은 것이 아니다, 근거 개념을 양상 개념인 안전성으로

- S’s true belief that p is safe [=not with luck]

iff S’s belief that p could not have been easily false.

쉽게 거짓이 될 수 없다 S가 p를 믿고 있고 다른 것이 비슷한 가능세계에서 p가 거짓이면 안됨

iff If S were to believe that p, p would not be false.

iff In almost all nearby possible worlds w in which S believes p, p is true.

(e.g.) Suppose Chulsoo believes, without any evidence, on 2021.2.12 that it will rain tomorrow. Suppose further that it does rain on 2021.2.13, and hence that Chulsoo’s belief happens to be true. But Chulsoo’s belief is not safe because it could have been easily false.

Epistemic Sensitivity

- S’s true belief that p is sensitive

iff If p were false, S would not believe that p.

iff In almost all nearby possible worlds w in which p is false, S does not believe that p

(e.g.) Suppose Chulsoo believes that he is not a brain in a vat. Chulsoo’s belief is true (of course!). But Chulsoo’s belief is not sensitive because, even If Chulsoo had been indeed a brain in a vat, he would have still believed that he is not a brain in a vat. According to Nozick, this is why we cannot refute skepticism, for our belief that the skeptical scenario does not hold (e.g. “We are not brains in a vat!”) fails to meet the sensitivity condition and hence does not count as knowledge.

- Note that the sensitivity condition for knowledge implies that the following principle of

knowledge closure does not hold:

(KC) If S knows p and knows that p entails q, then S knows q.

(KC) implies, for example, that If Chulsoo knows that he has two hands he must also know that he is not a brain in a vat, for Chulsoo knows that <Chulsoo has two hands> entails that <Chulsoo is not a brain in a vat>. If we accept the sensitivity condition for knowledge, however, Chulsoo’s belief that he has two hands is knowledge while his belief that he is not a brain in a vat is not. For theformer belief is sensitive but the latter is not.

A Single Special Method for Self-Knowledge?

Regarding our dispositional mental states, most philosophers accept that we cannot introspect them. 직접적으로 알 수 없다는 것은 아님 다만 그 방법이 내성이 아닌 것일 뿐

From this, some philosophers conclude that we do not possess any special method by which we

know our own dispositional mental states.

중요한 결론일 수 있음. 왜냐하면 (우리의 생각보다) 많은 심리 상태들이 성향적 심리상태이기 때문

But some philosophers insist that, although it is not introspection, there is some special method by which we know (some of) our own dispositional mental states better than we know the external world or others’ dispositional mental states.

Regarding our occurrent mental states, many philosophers accept that we can introspect them (whether it be ‘acquaintance’ or some sort of ‘inner sense’) and that we know them better than events in the external world or other people’s occurrent mental states.

So one theoretical option is to accept the following: (1) we have privileged access to both our own dispositional and occurrent mental states, but (2) the special method by which we know our dispositional mental states is different from the method by which we know our occurrent mental states. That is to say, there is no single special method by which we acquire self-knowledge.

Another option is: (1) we have privileged access to only our occurrent mental states, so that only self-knowledge of this type is special, and (2) this special method is introspection.

Still another option is: (1) We have privileged access to both our own dispositional and occurrent mental states, (2) the method by which we know both types of mental states is the same, but (3) this method is not introspection. (e.g. Unified Transparency Theory (Alex Byrne))

The Cartesian Theory of the Twofold Privileged Access to One’s Own Mental States

The Cartesian ontological dualism, according to which the objective physical realm and the subjective mental realm are fundamentally different from each other, is naturally accompanied by the epistemological dualism, according to which knowledge of the physical realm and knowledge of the subjective mental realm (‘self-knowledge’) are two fundamentally different kinds of knowledge.

According to the Cartesian theory of self-knowledge, we have a twofold privileged access to our own mental states, namely constant awareness of them (‘consciousness’) and a species of non- sensuous perception of at least some of them (‘introspection’)

The supposedly “self-intimating” character of mental states

“It is part of the definition of their being mental that their occurrence entails that they are self-intimating. If I think, hope, remember, will, regret, hear a noise, or feel a pain, I must, ipso facto, know that I do so. Even if I dream that I see a dragon, I must be apprised of my dragon-seeing, though, it is often conceded, I may not know that I am dreaming.” (p.140; italics added).

Ryle against the Special Status of Self-Knowledge

Ryle’s ‘Regress’ Objection (pp.144-145)

(Step 1) According to the Cartesian theory of self-knowledge, every mental state must be an object of consciousness.

(Step 2) But consciousness of one’s mental state is itself a mental state. Also, the former and the latter are different mental states given that their objects are different.

(Step 3) Since one is supposedly conscious of every mental state, one must be conscious of one’s consciousness of one’s mental state, too.

(Step 4) By the same reasoning, one must be again conscious of one’s consciousness of one’s consciousness of one’s mental state. . . Hence it follows that there must be “an infinite number of onion-skins of consciousness embedding any mental state or process whatsoever.”(p.144). But this is impossible.

(Step 5) If we reject this conclusion, then we must allow that there are mental states of which we cannot be conscious. But then the Cartesian theory of self-knowledge is wrong, for “’consciousness’ could no longer be retained as part of the definition of ‘mental’.” (p.145).

(Step 6) Therefore, the Cartesian theory of self-knowledge faces a dilemma.

The Cartesian Theory of Introspection

Difference between introspection and perception:

According to the Cartesian theory, introspection is a species of perception. But it is different from perception in the following three respects.

The objects of introspection are private, not public.

There is no bodily organ dedicated to introspection.

Introspection is infallible.

Difference between introspection and consciousness

According to the Cartesian theory, “introspection is an attentive operation and one which is only occasionally performed, whereas consciousness is supposed to be a constant element of all mental processes and one of which the revelations do not require to be receipted in special acts of attention.” (p.146; italics).

Also, “we introspect with the intention of finding the answer to particular problems, whereas we are conscious, whether we wish it or not.” (p.146).

Again, Ryle thinks that we have strong grounds for thinking that the Cartesian theory of introspection is wrong even if there “did exist events of the postulated ghostly status.” (p.146)

Ryle’s Second Objection: ‘Regress’ Objection (pp.146-147)

(Step 1) Introspecting one’s mental state is itself a mental state, and hence must be a potential object of introspection.

(Step 2) This higher-order introspection must be again a potential object introspection . . . and so ad infinitum.

(Step 3) But it should be impossible to perform infinite number of introspections at the same time.

(Step 4) Therefore, “there must be some mental processes which are unintrospectible, namely those introspections which incorporate the maximum possible number of synchronous acts of attention.”(pp.146-147)

(Step 5) If so, our knowledge that these mental processes have occurred should not rest on introspection.

(Step 6) But “if this knowledge does not always rest on introspection, it is open to question whether it ever does.” (p.147)

Consciousness to the Rescue?

- Commenting on the above objection, Ryle writes, “This objection might be countered by appeal to the other form of Privileged Acccess; we know that we introspect not by introspecting on our introspection, but from the direct deliverance of consciousness. To the guests of Charybdis, Scylla appears the more hospitable resort.” (p.147)

- Although Ryle does not make it explicit, he seems to think here that an appeal to consciousness will not help because, as we have see, consciousness has its own problem of regress.

Ryle’s Third Objection: Wide Disagreements about the Results of Introspection

If introspections are such a reliable source of information about how our minds are working, then why are there disputes and debates about the findings of introspection?

(e.g.) debates about whether there are activities of conscience, distinct from activities of intellect and from those of habitual deferences to taboos.

Ryle’s Fourth Objection (Hume): Some Emotional States Cannot Be Introspected.

Introspection is something dispassionate, whereas such emotional states as panic and fury are not dispassionate by its nature. Hence one cannot truly introspect these states, for one’s performing the very act of introspecting them already implies that one is no longer in those states.

When we talk about introspecting such emotional states, we are in fact performing retrospection, not introspection.

And “there is nothing intrinsically ghostly about the objects of retrospection.” (p.148). “the fact that retrospection is autobiographical does not imply that it gives us a Privileged Access to facts of a special status.” (p.149)

Ryle’s Positive Theory of Self-Knowledge

- A single methodology for self-knowledge and knowledge of other minds:

According to Ryle, our method of acquiring two kinds of knowledge is essentially the same, namely by observing behaviors (whether they are one’s own or other people’s) and making inferences about people’s mental states based on these behaviors together with inductive laws we have established about how people behave.

According to Ryle, the problem of how one acquires knowledge about one’s own mental states is “simply the methodological question, how we establish, and how we apply, certain sorts of law-like propositions about the overt and the silent behaviour of persons.” (p.150; italics added)

※ What Ryle means by ‘behaviors’ here is broader than is commonly assumed, for it includes

“unspoken soliloquies” (p.151)

Question: Is the above methodology sufficient for acquiring knowledge of our occurrent mental states, although it may be sufficient for acquiring knowledge of our dispositional mental states?

Ryle on the distinction between two categories of mental states

“So far we have been considering chiefly those brands of self-knowledge and the knowledge of others which consist in the more or less judicial assessment of long-term propensities and capacities, together with the application of those assessments in explanations of particular episodes. We have been considering how we interpret or understand courses of conduct. But there remains another sense of ‘know’ in which a

person is commonly said to know what he is at this moment doing, thinking, feeling, etc., a sense which is nearer to what the phosphorescenece-theory of consciousness tried, but failed, to describe.” (p.155; italics added)

Ryle’s answer: According to Ryle, a person knows what he is doing, thinking, feeling, etc. better

than what other people are doing, thinking, feeling, etc. just because he is “alive to” (p.159) to what he is doing, thinking, feeling, etc. better than he is alive to what others are doing, thinking, feeling, etc.

It is not entirely clear what Ryle means by one’s being “alive to” what one is doing, thinking, feeling, etc., but he seems to have in mind something like being ready to make appropriate responses to.

Ryle admits that one can also be “alive to what someone else is doing” (p.160). But his point seems to be that, in general, an agent is ready to make appropriate responses to his/her own doing, thinking, feeling, etc. much better than others’ doing, thinking, feeling, etc. because these are episodes that occur to himself/herself.

Ryle on Observation of One’s Own Inner Speeches as a Source for Self-Knowledge

Ryle seems to think that one’s inner speeches or “silent soliloquies” are one’s (silent) behaviors, i.e. what one is doing, and that one’s observation of them is essentially the same as one’s observation of other people’s utterances.

“One of the things often signified by ‘self-consciousness’ is the notice we take of our own unstudied utterances, including our explicit avowals, whether these are spoken aloud, muttered or said in our heads. We eavesdrop our own voiced utterances and our own silent monologues. In noticing these we are preparing ourselves to do something new, namely to describe the frames of mind which these utterances disclose. But there is nothing intrinsically proprietary about this activity. I can pay heed to what I overhear you saying as well as to what I overhear myself saying, though I cannot overhear your silent colloquies with yourself. Nor can I read your diary, if you write it in cipher, or keep it under lock and key.” (p.165)

As the above quote indicates, Ryle emphasizes the importance of a type of unstudied utterances

which he calls “avowals”, whose examples include utterances beginning with ‘I want’, ‘I hope’, ‘I intend’, ‘I dislike’, ‘I am depressed’, ‘I wonder’, ‘I guess’.

“[T]heir grammar makes it tempting to misconstrue all the sentences in which they occur as self- descriptions. But in its primary employment ‘I want’ is not used to convey information, but to make a

request or demand. It is no more meant as a contribution to general knowledge than ‘please’.” (p.164)

Ryle believes that the utterances he calls ‘avowals’ express one’s mental states rather than report them, just as one’s smile expresses one’s feeling of happiness. Just as one can know that a woman feels happy by seeing that she is smiling, one can know that one feels happy by seeing that one is smiling. Likewise, just as one knows that a woman feels happy by hearing her saying, “I am happy!”, one knows that one feels happy by hearing one’s silently saying to oneself, ‘I am happy!’.

Two Theses

(Thesis I) The introspection of current conscious experience is possible, important, necessary for a full life, and central to the development of a full scientific understanding of the mind.

(Thesis II) Such introspection is highly untrustworthy.

Given that Schwitgebel accepts the possibility and importance of introspection, he is different from Ryle. What he questions is not introspection itself but its reliability.
