## Claude · 2026-06-11T05:10

Kang's paper tackles two textual puzzles about Wittgenstein's *Prototractatus* (PT) — the manuscript von Wright found in Vienna in 1965, an early version of the *Tractatus* whose remarks carry different numbers and aren't arranged in numerical order. The puzzles are *how* PT was composed (since it's largely assembled from Wittgenstein's other manuscripts rather than written fresh) and *when* it was composed (it bears no date).

On structure (§II), Kang's key observation is that seven horizontal lines inserted among the remarks (pp. 28, 52, 60, 64, 70, 71, 78) mark section boundaries, since an abrupt change of topic or numbering always occurs across them. Working through the sections, he proposes the following layout: pp. 3–28 form the "Core-PT," containing the framework remarks (major numbers up to 6) that mostly *cannot* be traced to surviving manuscripts; pp. 28–52 derive from a manuscript that already contained a revision of NL and NB 1–2 (subdivided as NL → missing Norway manuscript → NB 1–2 plus work following NB 2); pp. 52–70 are three thematically organized sections on logic (autonomy of logic, logical constants, logical propositions); pp. 70–1 and 71–8 collect concluding major remarks and "side-topics" (science, ethics, probability); and pp. 78–103 come from later manuscripts, ending with 1917 material following NB 3. A recurring methodological claim is that PT was copied not from the source manuscripts directly but from *intermediate, already-revised* manuscripts — he supports this with evidence of prior revision in remarks like PT 4.026 and 4.0231, and with the list of Wittgenstein's manuscripts in his sister's recently found letter.

On dating (§III), Kang rejects both standard proposals. Von Wright dated PT to summer 1918 (just before the *Tractatus*), relying on the Pinsent dedication; Kang argues the dedication was a later addition, since Ellen Pinsent's letter announcing the death is dated 6 July 1918, leaving implausibly little time to compose both PT and the *Tractatus*. McGuinness — whose three-stage hypothesis (first 71 pages written Oct 1915–March 1916) became the scholarly consensus — gets the more sustained refutation. Kang's decisive argument comes from Core-PT: it already contains the developed idea of the *general form of proposition* and the *formal series* (operation N, the generalized Sheffer stroke), ideas Wittgenstein demonstrably had *not yet* reached even at the end of NB 3 (early 1917). Since Core-PT immediately precedes p. 28 without a page break, the whole first 71 pages must postdate NB 3, not predate it. He cites PT 5.32 against the tentative NB 3 entries of May 1916 as especially telling.

Kang's own (admittedly speculative) conjecture is that Wittgenstein wrote the 1917 manuscripts and the Core-PT source during Jan–July 1917, then composed PT itself during the relatively quiet stationing at Czernowitz, Sept 1917–Feb 1918.

His conclusion is conciliatory: both predecessors were half-right. Von Wright's *general* characterization of PT as an immediate predecessor of the *Tractatus* holds (because on Kang's dating PT was written when Wittgenstein's view was already mature, so it doesn't diverge much from the final work), even if the specific 1918 date fails. And McGuinness deserves credit for spotting that PT's remarks are drawn from the manuscripts roughly in chronological order — a pattern Kang's section-by-section analysis reinforces, supporting the hope that PT can be used to reconstruct the order in which Wittgenstein introduced his ideas.

## Claude · 2026-06-11T05:21

A few distinct stakes, some bigger than the paper's modest framing lets on.

The most consequential is what the dating does to the *developmental* reading of early Wittgenstein. The whole "road to the *Tractatus*" enterprise depends on a reliable chronology of the surviving manuscripts, and there's a frustrating gap between the notebooks and the finished book. McGuinness's proposal was attractive precisely because it closed that gap — placing the first 71 pages of PT in 1915–16 gave scholars a *continuous* archive spanning the entire early period. But that placement has a sting in its tail: if PT's core predates the *Tractatus* by roughly two years, then the differences between PT and the *Tractatus* (especially the famous renumbering) invite reading a substantive doctrinal evolution *into* that interval. This is exactly what Kremer's "Contextualism and Holism" paper does — it treats the PT-to-*Tractatus* numbering changes as evidence of a real shift toward contextualism and holism. Kang's redating cuts the ground out from under that. If PT was written late 1917–early 1918, when Wittgenstein's view was already mature, then PT ≈ *Tractatus* doctrinally, and you can't mine their differences for a two-years'-development story. So the paper is, indirectly, an intervention in a live interpretive dispute about whether there's a meaningful philosophical transition captured at the very end of the early period.

Second, and more positively, the dating argument is really an argument about *when a core Tractarian doctrine was born*. Kang's decisive evidence is that Core-PT already contains the developed apparatus of the general form of proposition — operation N, the generalized Sheffer stroke, the formal-series treatment of generality — which Wittgenstein demonstrably had not reached even at the close of NB 3. So in the course of dating a manuscript he's effectively dating the emergence of one of the hardest and most central ideas in the *Tractatus* (proposition 6), placing the breakthrough in 1917. That's a contribution to the genesis of the doctrine, not just to manuscript bookkeeping.

Third, there's a methodological and archival payoff. The "intermediate revised manuscript" thesis — that PT was copied not from the source notebooks directly but from already-revised summaries — together with the use of the horizontal lines, numbering patterns, and source-tracing, is a clean model of textual reconstruction. It also does real work recovering the shape of the *lost* archive: the missing Norway manuscript, the post-NB 2 summary, the 1917 manuscripts. For anyone trying to reconstruct what Wittgenstein actually had on his desk, that matters.

The honest qualification is that this is importance *within a niche* — genetic/developmental scholarship on the early Wittgenstein, not the interpretation of the *Tractatus*'s content as such. And there's a nice irony worth flagging: Kang's conclusion simultaneously *lowers* PT's value as an independent philosophical document (it's not a window onto an earlier, different view) while *raising* its value as a chronological index — by vindicating McGuinness's insight that PT's remarks are arranged roughly in the order of their source manuscripts, Kang makes PT usable as an instrument for tracing the sequence in which Wittgenstein introduced his ideas, just a more tightly constrained one than the consensus assumed.
