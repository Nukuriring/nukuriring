# Owning Up, Not Looking Through: Walton, Cavell, and the Knowledge-Telos of Self-Knowledge

*Draft v1 — working paper. Citations to Walton's "Transparent Pictures" and Cavell's* The World Viewed *follow the pagination recorded in the project knowledge bases (Critical Inquiry 1984; Harvard enlarged ed. 1979) and should be re-verified against the originals before submission.*

## Abstract

The philosophy of self-knowledge is organized around a tacit demand: that whatever is distinctive about a subject's relation to her own mind be cashed out as a form of *knowledge* with superior epistemic standing. I call this the knowledge-telos. It is shared across otherwise opposed camps — the empiricist and agentialist readings of the "transparency method," and even the expressivist accounts that were supposed to escape it — and it is what makes the field treat the suggestion "this is not knowledge" as a *reductio*. This paper does not propose a new account of self-knowledge to set beside the existing eight. It offers a diagnostic instrument and a route to using it. The route runs through an unlikely place: Kendall Walton's account of photographic transparency, which rigorously exhibits a "looking-through" relation that is genuine contact with the world and yet not a relation of knowledge. Walton furnishes an existence-proof — that a first-personal relation need not be a knowledge-relation is coherent and instantiated — and a contrast that isolates what the first-person relation to one's own attitudes is *not*. A Cavellian diagnosis then identifies the grip of the knowledge-telos as a species of the skeptical impulse, whose standing alternative is not better knowing but *acknowledgment*. The upshot is a positive characterization of first-person access to one's avowable attitudes as a stance of owning rather than a mode of detection — and, as a downstream consequence, the dissolution of the long dispute over what *warrants* the transparency method.

## 1. Introduction

Ask yourself whether you believe that it will rain tomorrow. Gareth Evans famously observed that you will answer this question not by turning your attention inward, onto some inner item, but by attending to the world — to the clouds, the forecast, the season (Evans 1982: 225). "In making a self-ascription of belief," he wrote, "one's eyes are, so to speak, or occasionally literally, directed outward — upon the world." This is the *transparency method*: you settle a question about your own mind by considering the worldly matter that mind is about. You look, as it were, *through* the belief to what the belief is of.

There is a second, unrelated use of "transparency" in recent philosophy. In "Transparent Pictures: On the Nature of Photographic Realism," Kendall Walton argues that photographs are *transparent* in a strict and literal sense: we see the world *through* them, as we see things through telescopes, microscopes, and mirrors (Walton 1984: 251). To look at a photograph of your great-grandmother is to *see her* — with photographic assistance, across the gulf of time — and not merely to see a representation of her.

The coincidence of vocabulary is, on its face, just a coincidence; the two "transparencies" belong to different sub-disciplines and have never been brought into contact.[^coincidence] This paper brings them into contact, but not in the way one might first expect. I will not argue that the mind is "transparent" in Walton's perceptual sense, nor that Evans's metaphor can be cashed out as a kind of inner photography. My claim is almost the reverse. Walton's account is valuable here precisely because it gives us a *rigorous and worked-out case* of a looking-through relation that is genuine contact with its object and yet has, as Walton insists, "rather less to do with acquiring knowledge … than has sometimes been supposed" (Walton 1984: 269). With that case in hand, we can measure the first-person relation against it and find that the first-person relation is neither what Walton describes (perceptual contact) nor what the self-knowledge literature assumes it must be (a mode of knowledge). It is a third thing. Naming that third thing is the work of Stanley Cavell's concept of *acknowledgment*.

The argument has the following shape. In §2 I identify the organizing presupposition of the self-knowledge debate — the *knowledge-telos* — and show that it is shared even by the parties who appear to oppose one another most sharply. In §3 I take the Waltonian detour, extracting from it an existence-proof and a contrast, while taking care not to attack a perceptual reading of Evans that no one holds. In §4 I draw on Cavell to diagnose the knowledge-telos as a form of the skeptical impulse and to supply its alternative. In §5 I give the positive characterization: first-person access to one's avowable attitudes is a matter of *owning* them, not of detecting them. In §6 I show that the venerable dispute over what *warrants* the transparency method — reliability or responsibility — dissolves once the knowledge-telos is dropped. §7 answers objections, including the most pressing one, that I have merely relabeled expressivism.

Two prefatory notes on what this paper is and is not. It is not a new account of self-knowledge competing with acquaintance, inner sense, self-interpretation, transparency, reasons, rationalism, agentialism, and expressivism (the eight catalogued by Gertler 2024). It is a diagnosis of a presupposition those accounts share, together with a positive proposal that is, in its content, close to the agentialist and expressivist poles. Its novelty lies in the *route* — the Waltonian detour and the Cavellian diagnosis — and in the etiological claim that the field's allegiance to the knowledge-telos is a skeptical symptom. Where the positive view overlaps existing positions, I will say so plainly and mark the difference.

## 2. The knowledge-telos

Begin with the question the literature takes itself to be answering. Since Descartes, most philosophers have held that a subject's epistemic relation to her own mental states differs markedly from her relation to the external world and to other minds, and the discipline of self-knowledge is the attempt to say *how* it differs and *what epistemic status* the difference confers (Gertler 2024: §0). The candidate marks of distinctiveness — special security, a special first-personal method, the involvement of agency, first-person authority — are, with one partial exception, marks of a distinctive *epistemic standing*. Even where agency enters, it is typically enlisted in the service of epistemology: as Gertler notes, most who emphasize our agency over our attitudes "also claim the agential relation *grounds* a special epistemic relation" (Gertler 2024: §1).

Call the *knowledge-telos* the assumption that the philosophically load-bearing fact about first-person access is its standing as *knowledge* with superior credentials — that a successful account must vindicate our self-ascriptions as especially secure, warranted, or authoritative *items of knowledge*. The knowledge-telos is not a thesis any one camp defends; it is the frame within which the camps conduct their disagreements. Consider how widely it is shared.

The empiricist reading of the transparency method holds that the procedure Evans described yields *empirical* self-knowledge because it is reliable or safe. Alex Byrne's "doxastic schema" — from the premise *p*, infer "I believe that *p*" — is defended on the ground that following it is self-verifying and that the resulting self-ascriptions could not easily have been false (Byrne 2005, 2018). The credential is externalist warrant; the output is *knowledge*. The agentialist reading, by contrast, holds that transparency works because we are *responsible* for our attitudes: Richard Moran argues that a rational subject has an "epistemic right" to assume her attitudes are shaped by her reasons, and that this entitlement is what licenses answering "do I believe *p*?" by considering the reasons that bear on *p* (Moran 2001). The ground is normative rather than reliabilist — but the payoff is once again that the resulting self-ascriptions amount to *knowledge*.

Even the accounts framed in opposition to this whole picture keep the telos. Expressivism holds that avowals such as "I'm in pain" or "I believe it's raining" resemble *expressions* of the states they ascribe rather than reports based on evidence (Bar-On 2004). One might expect expressivism to abandon the claim that avowals express knowledge; the early "simple" expressivism did exactly that, treating avowals as neither true nor false. But the live contemporary versions insist on the contrary. Dorit Bar-On argues that avowals can be "genuine and privileged self-*knowledge*" despite being groundless; Lucy Campbell builds an account on which an avowal "I want water" *expresses knowledge* of the desire, grounding first-person privilege in a "rational response" relation (Bar-On 2004: 405; Campbell 2020). The position that genuinely exits the knowledge-telos — simple expressivism — is treated throughout the literature as the option to be *avoided*, precisely because it surrenders the knowledge-claim.

That last fact is the telling one. The field's reflexive treatment of "then it isn't knowledge" as a cost to be minimized, or a *reductio* to be escaped, *is* the knowledge-telos at work. And the reflex is not idiosyncratic; it structures even the meta-level descriptions of the debate. Brie Gertler, defending the empiricist side, characterizes the empiricism-versus-agentialism quarrel as a disagreement "about the epistemic *basis* for self-knowledge, not about … the method" (Gertler 2018). This is exactly right as a description, and I will lean on it: both camps agree that the transparency method delivers *knowledge*, and they dispute only what *warrants* that delivery. The shared premise — that there is a knowledge-output whose warrant is in question — is the knowledge-telos, stated by one of its own adherents. It is this shared premise, and not any party's preferred warrant, that I mean to put in question.

## 3. A detour through photographic transparency

Why route an argument about self-knowledge through the philosophy of photography? Because Walton supplies something the self-knowledge literature lacks: a rigorous, defended example of a "looking-through" relation that is unmistakably a relation of *contact* with the world and just as unmistakably *not* a relation of knowledge. Having the example before us does two things, one modal and one diagnostic.

Walton's thesis is that to see a photograph of an object is, literally, to see the object — "with photographic assistance," in the same generic sense in which we see things through mirrors and telescopes (Walton 1984: 251). Paintings, by contrast, are *opaque*: in front of a portrait of Henry VIII we see only a representation of him (1984: 253). What grounds the difference is not how the picture looks but how it is *caused*. A photograph is connected to its scene by a "purely mechanical" causal process that is independent of anyone's beliefs: the image is counterfactually dependent on the scene even with the photographer's beliefs and intentions held fixed, so that "the artist draws his hallucination" while "the camera bypasses the photographer's hallucination and captures what is in the jungle" (1984: 261, 264). Seeing-through requires only that one's visual experience depend on the scene in this belief-independent way.

The point I want is what Walton says next. Transparency, he insists, is independent of accuracy, of detail, of information, and even of reliability. We see our long-dead relatives through old snapshots though we learn nothing new from them (1984: 251). We can see through a distorting or badly exposed photograph. Most strikingly, Walton imagines a mirror whose surface flexes constantly and unpredictably, so that it is "not a knowledge-producing mechanism": beliefs formed through it would not count as knowledge, and yet, he says, "this does not mean that he does not *see* the reflected things" (1984: 259). And he draws the general moral explicitly: "Perceptual contact with things has rather less to do with acquiring knowledge about them than has sometimes been supposed" (1984: 269). Here, fully worked out, is a *looking-through relation that is genuine perceptual contact and yet not a relation of knowledge.*

The first thing this gives us is an **existence-proof**. The knowledge-telos assumes that the distinctive first-person relation, *if it is to be anything of philosophical interest*, must be a relation of knowledge — so that to deny it the status of knowledge is to deny that there is anything distinctive there at all. Walton's transparency shows this assumption to be *optional*. There is at least one important, non-degenerate relation — perceptual contact — that is worth a great deal to us (we cherish the photographs of our dead) and yet is not constituted by warrant, reliability, or knowledge. So a relation's failing to be a relation of knowledge is no proof that it is nothing, or nothing distinctive. The conceptual room the knowledge-telos denies is real.

The second thing is a **contrast**, and here I must be careful to avoid an error that would sink the argument. I do *not* claim that Evans, Byrne, or Moran ever held that the mind is perceptually transparent in Walton's sense, or that the self-ascription of belief is a kind of inner photography. They held no such thing; Evans's "looking through" is a metaphor for *declining* to attend to one's belief and attending to the world instead, which is nearly the opposite of Walton's relation, in which one ends up in perceptual contact with the photographed object. The coincidence of the word "transparency" is just a coincidence, and an argument that measured Evans's method against Walton's marks and pronounced it a *failure* would be attacking a position no one occupies.[^strawman]

The contrast does its work differently. Walton gives us a clear specimen of one kind of looking-through — perceptual contact, belief-independent, with the world on the far side. Set the first-person relation beside it. The first-person relation is *not* this: you do not perceive your belief; there is no belief-independent causal channel by which your believing reaches your awareness as the jungle reaches the film; indeed Evans's whole point is that you do not attend to the belief at all. So whatever the first-person relation is, it is not Waltonian contact. And — this is the burden of §§4–5 — it is also not, as the knowledge-telos assumes, a mode of knowledge-access. It falls on neither side of the contrast Walton's case makes vivid. The detour's payoff is triangulation: by fixing one corner (contact-without-knowledge) with precision, Walton lets us see that the first-person relation sits at a corner of its own. What occupies that corner is the topic of the next two sections.

## 4. Knowing and acknowledging

Cavell's lifelong subject is skepticism, and his characteristic move is to refuse the skeptic's question rather than to answer it. The skeptic about the external world, or about other minds, demands *certainty* — a securing of our relation to the world or to others that would convert it into indubitable *knowledge*. Cavell's claim is that this demand misdescribes the relation we actually stand in, and that the craving to convert that relation into knowledge is itself the source of the skeptical predicament. The truth the skeptic distorts is that our fundamental relation to the world and to others is not one of *knowing* but of *acknowledging* (Cavell 1969; 1979).

The contrast is sharpest in Cavell's treatment of other minds. I do not, on his view, *infer* or *detect* that you are in pain behind the screen of your behavior, holding my conclusion hostage to a justification that the skeptic can always reopen. Your pain makes a *claim* on me that I can meet or evade; I *acknowledge* it or fail to. Acknowledgment is not a better or more certain kind of knowing; it is a different category of response, one in which the relevant failure is not *error* but *avoidance*. As he puts the distinction in *The World Viewed*, drawing on Wittgenstein: "a true statement is something we know or do not know; a *truthful* statement is one we must *acknowledge* or fail or refuse to acknowledge" (Cavell 1979: 157). Truth turns on evidence and is *known*; truthfulness turns on our *acceptance* and must be *acknowledged*. And acknowledgment, far from being the abandonment of knowledge, is for Cavell its ground — "the home of knowledge," as he says of the way a modernist painting manifests what is true of all painting by *acknowledging* its conditions (1979: 110).[^home]

Now the diagnosis. If the demand that our relation to the world *be* knowledge is the skeptical reflex, then the knowledge-telos in the philosophy of self-knowledge is that same reflex turned inward, onto the subject's relation to her own mind. The field's insistence that first-person access must be vindicated as a species of secure knowledge — its treatment of "this is not knowledge" as a *reductio* — is not a neutral methodological constraint but the very craving Cavell diagnoses: the wish to *secure* a relation that is, in its nature, not a matter of securing. And the alternative the diagnosis points to is the same one Cavell offers elsewhere: not better self-knowing, but *self-acknowledgment*.

I must be candid about the status of this transposition. Cavell himself directs acknowledgment chiefly at the skeptic about *other* minds and the external world, not at the problem of self-knowledge; the extension to the first person is my step, not his.[^moran-cavell] I take encouragement, but not proof, from a passage near the end of *The World Viewed* where Cavell does speak of self-knowledge in just these terms — "The knowledge of the self as it is always takes place in the betrayal of the self as it was … the form of self-revelation" (1979: 160) — casting self-knowledge as a matter of honesty and owning rather than of epistemic access. That register is existential (it concerns a confession of love), and I lean on it as resonance rather than as evidence that Cavell had worked out the epistemology I am about to propose. What the transposition must earn, it must earn on its own merits, to which I now turn.

## 5. Self-knowledge as acknowledgment

Here is the positive proposal, stated for its proper domain. For a subject's *avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes* — paradigmatically her beliefs and intentions — first-person access is not a mode of *detecting* a standing inner fact but a stance of *owning* it: a first-personal answerability for the attitude, in which the subject stands behind the attitude as hers to defend, revise, and act on. I will say that the subject *acknowledges* the attitude. Three features individuate this stance and distinguish it from a detection-relation.

First, its characteristic *failure* is not misperception or unreliability but *self-deception* — evasion, disavowal, bad faith. When self-knowledge of an attitude goes wrong, the typical shape of the going-wrong is not that the subject scanned an inner object and misread it, as one might misread a gauge; it is that the subject would not *own* what is hers. This is a structural fact about the domain, and it is a fact the detection models systematically mislocate. Walton's perceptual relation has its own signature of error — one confuses things that are genuinely *similar* (an eighty-five-foot tree with an eighty-four-foot one, not with the word "tree") — and the shape of that liability is part of what makes the relation *perceptual* (1984: 270–271). The signature of first-person error has a different shape entirely: it tracks the topology of *what one can face about oneself*, not the similarity-space of inner items. A relation whose failures are failures of honesty is not a relation of detection.

Second, the stance is *groundless* in the expressivists' sense: the subject who avows her belief offers no evidence and passes through no intervening judgment, because owning a commitment is not the conclusion of an inquiry into whether one has it. Third, the stance is *first-personal* in a way no third party can occupy: another can *attribute* an attitude to me, on whatever evidence, but only I can *own* mine. These three features are not stipulated; they are recovered from the phenomena, and the next task is to show that the phenomena the knowledge-telos was meant to capture survive — indeed are better placed — under this redescription.

I claim only that the explananda are *preserved*, not that they are derived from nothing; the rival accounts likewise help themselves to their organizing intuitions, and I help myself to no more. Take the four standard data in turn. The *asymmetry* between self and others becomes an asymmetry of answerability and authorship: I own my attitudes, where others can only attribute them — which is Moran's genuine insight, now freed from the requirement that the asymmetry be an asymmetry of *knowledge*. *First-person authority* — the presumption of truth that attaches to "I believe it's raining," and the impropriety of meeting it with "no, you don't" — becomes the standing of the one who *owns*: to answer an avowal with "no, you don't" is not to correct a measurement but to refuse the subject her authorship, a category-mistake about answerability rather than a rival report. This is, almost verbatim, Cavell's truth/truthfulness point (1979: 157). *Moore's paradox* — the peculiar irrationality of "it's raining, but I don't believe that it is" — becomes the incoherence of *disavowing what one is in the act of asserting*: since to assert *p* is to own the commitment to *p*, to deny that commitment in the same breath is incoherent at the level of owning, not at the level of evidence. And the *groundless immediacy* of avowal is, as we have seen, native to owning rather than something an account must labor to permit.

Two of these redescriptions deserve a defense, because each lands near a standing objection. The asymmetry claim faces the charge, pressed by Reed (2010), Borgoni (2015), and especially Doyle (2019), that *detached* self-observation does not preclude ownership and can even be *enlightening* — that coming to understand a recalcitrant attitude by treating it, for a time, as an object of inquiry is not alienation but a route to genuine self-understanding. I grant the cases entirely. But notice *which* cases they are: they are cases of attitudes the subject *cannot presently own* — the recalcitrant, the self-deceived, the akratic. That is exactly the breakdown profile predicted by the present view, on which the characteristic failure of first-person access is the failure to own. Detached self-observation is the *remedial supplement* one reaches for when acknowledgment has broken down; its usefulness in the breakdown cases is no evidence that acknowledgment is not the *default and constitutive* mode for avowable attitudes in good order. Doyle describes the convalescence, not the health.

The authority claim, in turn, lands near the constitutive and performative accounts of Coliva (2012) and Bilgrami (2006), which already explain first-person authority without any appeal to Cavell: on Coliva's view, to judge "I believe that *p*" is to *undertake* the very commitment one ascribes, so the self-ascription is authoritative because it is, in effect, self-fulfilling. Coliva is the closest analytic kin to the present proposal, and I do not pretend the performativity is my discovery. The difference is this: Coliva's performative is still aimed at making the self-ascription *true* — it secures a knowledge-output by a different mechanism, and so remains within the knowledge-telos. Acknowledgment *subsumes* the performativity — owning a commitment is indeed something one *does*, not something one reads off — while *dropping* the requirement that the point of the doing is to render a second-order belief true. What one does in avowing is not manufacture a truth-maker for "I believe *p*"; it is stand behind *p* as one's own.

This last difference is also what separates the present view from the position it most resembles in the recent literature. Lucy Campbell's "expression without expressivism" (Campbell 2022) unifies the expressivist insight that self-ascriptions are avowals with an agency-inflected, broadly Moranian epistemology — and does so explicitly *to preserve* self-knowledge as a distinctive *epistemic* kind. Campbell stays inside the knowledge-telos; that is the announced point of her title's second half. The present proposal is the contrary move: it keeps the expression and the agency but *relocates the phenomenon off the axis of knowledge altogether*. The two accounts therefore agree about a great deal at the level of phenomena and disagree about the one thing this paper is about. I take Campbell's account to be the strongest available *foil* — the best version of the unification that declines to leave the knowledge-telos — and also, in one respect, a *debtor's opportunity*: Campbell herself flags that, to *explain* rather than merely *presuppose* first-person privilege, her "rational response" relation must ultimately be analyzed in non-epistemic terms, and marks this as unfinished business. Acknowledgment is a candidate for exactly that non-epistemic analysis. Where Campbell unifies in order to save the knowledge-telos, I supply the non-epistemic story she leaves open and let the telos go.

A word on scope, since it bears on what follows. I have restricted the proposal to avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes. I do not extend it to sensations. The acquaintance theorists are very plausibly right that one's relation to a present pain is not a matter of *owning* it — there is, as Hill puts it, "no appearance/reality distinction in the case of sensations" (Hill 1991: 127), and a model of answerability is a poor fit for an itch. A hybrid is therefore both available and, I think, correct: acquaintance for the sensory, acknowledgment for the attitudinal. This is not an embarrassment but a sharpening. The contested ground in the self-knowledge debate — the ground on which the empiricist and agentialist readings of transparency actually fight — *is* the attitudes, and it is there that the present proposal is meant to bite.

## 6. A consequence: the dissolution of the warrant-dispute

Return now to the disagreement we began from. Byrne and Moran, the empiricist and the agentialist, conduct what Gertler accurately described as a dispute over the *epistemic basis* of the transparency method: granted that the method delivers self-knowledge, what *warrants* the delivery — reliability or responsibility? My claim is that this dispute presupposes the knowledge-telos and lapses with it.

If first-person access to one's attitudes is acknowledgment rather than a knowledge-output, then there is no knowledge-output whose warrant is in question, and the dispute about the warrant has no subject matter. What remains, once the shared presupposition is withdrawn, is two accurate descriptions of two *different* things. Byrne has described a genuine and reliable *correlation*: from the world's being a certain way, one can move, safely, to a true second-order self-ascription. That is a real phenomenon — a kind of self-*tracking* — and Byrne's externalism describes it correctly. But it is a description in which the *agent is bracketed for the purpose of warrant*: the credential is safety, and safety is indifferent to whether the subject can make first-personal sense of the transition, which is just why Boyle could object that the inference "won't seem reasonable to the thinker" (Boyle 2011). What Byrne describes is the operation of a reliable detector, and a detector — however accurate — has no *authority*, only a track record. Moran, by contrast, has described the real phenomenon of *owning*: the subject as the responsible author of her attitudes. His error is not in the description but in the certification: having located the distinctive thing in responsibility and commitment "as much as" in cognitive access (Moran 2001: 64), he nonetheless insists that the upshot be classified as *knowledge*, and it is that residual certification, not the agential insight, that keeps him inside the warrant-dispute and exposes him to the charge that his view is "too demanding" (O'Brien 2003).[^obrien]

So the dispute is partly verbal and partly substantive, and the diagnosis sorts the parts. It is *verbal* to the extent that the two camps describe two different real phenomena — reliable tracking and responsible owning — and talk past one another in calling both "self-knowledge." It is *substantive* to the extent that each claims to have characterized *the distinctive first-person relation* — and there the dispute does not need to be adjudicated so much as dissolved, because the distinctive first-person relation is neither tracking nor a warranted knowledge-output but acknowledgment, of which Moran's "owning" is the correct description shorn of its epistemic certification. The long argument over the warrant of transparency was a real argument about a presupposition that, once seen as a presupposition, one can decline.

## 7. Objections and replies

**You have merely relabeled expressivism.** This is the objection to take most seriously, since the positive proposal of §5 sits very close to Bar-On's neo-expressivism and to Campbell's "expression without expressivism." Three things distinguish the present paper from a relabeling. First, its primary product is not an account of avowals — a class of speech acts — but a *diagnosis of a debate*: the claim that the empiricist and agentialist readings of transparency are two distortions of one relation, and that the field's allegiance to the knowledge-telos is a skeptical symptom. That diagnostic claim is not made by expressivism, which appears in the literature as one account among several rather than as an instrument trained on the others. Second, the positive view is not, like Bar-On's, a thesis that avowals nonetheless express *knowledge*; it is the contrary thesis that the first-person relation is not on the knowledge axis at all — which is exactly the step Campbell's "without expressivism" declines to take and which her own unfinished business invites. Third, the route is new: no one has used Walton's photographic transparency to make the conceptual room, or Cavell's acknowledgment to occupy it. The novelty I claim is the diagnostic instrument and the route to it, not the discovery that self-ascriptions are avowals, which is Bar-On's.

**You have abandoned knowledge and so changed the subject.** I have not abandoned knowledge; I have re-grounded it. On the Cavellian picture acknowledgment is "the home of knowledge," not its rival: downstream of acknowledging an attitude one may of course have a true and even a warranted second-order belief about it, and nothing in the view denies this. What the view denies is that this second-order belief, or its warrant, is the *distinctive* and *fundamental* first-person fact, or the source of first-person authority. The explananda that made self-knowledge look special — asymmetry, authority, the Moorean phenomena, groundless immediacy — are all preserved (§5), and preserved without the over-intellectualization the knowledge-telos imposes. The subject has been re-grounded, not changed.

**The detour proves nothing; an analogy with photographs cannot settle anything about the mind.** Agreed — and the argument does not rest on an analogy. Walton's role is strictly limited to the two functions of §3: an existence-proof that a looking-through relation need not be a knowledge-relation (which licenses *taking seriously* a non-knowledge first-person relation, against the knowledge-telos's reflex that there could be no such thing), and a contrast that isolates what the first-person relation is not. The positive identification of that relation as acknowledgment is carried by Cavell and by the phenomenology of owning in §5, not by any claim that minds are relevantly like cameras. Indeed the disanalogies are essential to the view: Walton's transparency is grounded in the *absence* of the subject from the causal chain — the camera works because no believing mind stands between us and the scene — whereas the first-person relation is constitutively a relation of the subject's *presence*. I borrow from Walton only the decoupling of contact from knowledge, never the subject-absence that grounds it in his case; that is why the view does not import the very alienation the agentialists warn against.

**Acknowledgment is freighted with existential drama that mundane self-ascription lacks.** Cavell's examples are confessions and crises; "I believe it will rain" is no dark night of the soul. The reply distinguishes the *form* from the *intensity*. The form — owning, answerability — is present in every case, however trivial, which is why even the flattest Moorean conjunction ("it's raining but I don't believe it") registers as a failure of *owning* and not of *evidence*. The existential cases are where the form becomes most visible, not the only cases where it operates. The drama illustrates the structure; it does not constitute it.

**Self-deception and recalcitrance show that we sometimes know our attitudes only by observing ourselves.** This is Doyle's point again, and it has been answered in §5: the cases in which detached self-observation is needed and illuminating are precisely the cases in which acknowledgment has *failed*, and the need for a remedial, third-personal supplement when owning breaks down is no evidence that owning is not the constitutive mode in the central, non-pathological cases. A theory of vision is not refuted by spectacles.

## 8. Conclusion

The philosophy of self-knowledge has been conducted, almost without exception, under the assumption that what it studies must turn out to be a kind of knowledge — that the first-person relation, to be worth anything, must be vindicated as secure, warranted, authoritative cognition. I have called this the knowledge-telos and argued that it is a presupposition rather than a result, and a suspect one: a Waltonian existence-proof shows the room it denies to be real, and a Cavellian diagnosis identifies its grip as a form of the skeptical impulse, whose standing alternative is not better knowing but acknowledgment. For our avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes, first-person access is owning, not looking through; its failures are failures of honesty, not of detection; and the old dispute over what *warrants* the transparency method dissolves, leaving two accurate descriptions of two different things and one distinctive relation that is neither.

I have kept the claims deliberately modest. The positive view is close kin to the agentialist and expressivist proposals already on the table; what is new is the route — the detour through photographic transparency and the diagnosis through acknowledgment — and the redescription of the warrant-dispute as a symptom rather than a problem. A more ambitious version of the argument would press the diagnosis past the transparency theorists to the framing assumptions of the whole field, including the skeptical denials of distinctiveness that are the knowledge-telos's disappointed mirror image; but that is another paper. It is enough, here, to have shown that the first relation each of us bears to her own mind may be less like seeing through a clear pane and more like standing behind one's word.

## Notes

[^coincidence]: The two literatures are kept rigorously apart even by those best placed to connect them. The standard reference works on the self-knowledge "transparency method" do not mention Walton, and the recent dedicated volume *New Perspectives on Transparency and Self-Knowledge* (Andreotta and Winokur, eds.) catalogues the method's extensions — to memory, intention, credence, and the rest — without any appearance of photographic transparency.

[^strawman]: This is worth flagging because an earlier and cruder version of the present argument did commit the error: it measured the "transparency method" against Walton's constitutive marks of perceptual transparency, found that it failed them, and concluded that the method's "transparency" was an unearned metaphor. That argument is a non sequitur, since no party to the self-knowledge debate asserts perceptual transparency of the mental in the first place. The detour earns its keep only as existence-proof and contrast, not as demolition.

[^home]: The phrase occurs in Cavell's discussion of modernist painting, where "being acknowledgment, it is knowledge, of itself and of its world" (1979: 110). I take the general moral — that acknowledgment grounds rather than competes with knowledge — and not the medium-specific aesthetics, and I flag that the transfer trades on a broad rather than a technical sense of "knowledge."

[^moran-cavell]: There is a real risk of being scooped here that I want to confront directly. Moran's own work is influenced by Cavell, and Moran has written on Cavell's treatment of skepticism and other minds. But those essays apply acknowledgment to the *other-minds* problem, not to a reconstrual of Moran's *self-knowledge* view; the present extension is not executed there. Closer still is Renaudie (2015), who brings Cavell to bear on self-knowledge and the transparency model — but in the key of *opacity* and self-concealment, the opposite valence from the *owning* developed here. The nearest precedent for the bare thesis that self-knowledge is not a relation of epistemic access is Rödl (2007), who reaches it through Kant and the theory of self-consciousness, treating self-knowledge as "knowledge from spontaneity" rather than abandoning the category of knowledge; I differ both in route (Walton and Cavell rather than the transcendental deduction) and, arguably, in category (a stance of answerability rather than a non-receptive species of knowledge).

[^obrien]: O'Brien's objection is that Moran requires the subject to *recognize* that her reasons for expecting war are evidence that she believes war is coming — a reflective grasp of her own deliberation that ordinary self-attribution does not seem to need. I do not claim that acknowledgment automatically discharges this demand; the reflective character of owning a belief on the basis of one's reasons may inherit some of it. The narrower and defensible point is that the *over-demandingness* is generated by the requirement that the deliverance be certified as *knowledge*, and that lifting the certification is what relieves the pressure.
