# Owning Up, Not Looking Through: Walton, Cavell, and the Knowledge-Telos of Self-Knowledge

*Draft v2 — working paper. Revised for analytic regimentation and to incorporate the "convention-as-nature / myth" argument (§5). Citations to Walton's "Transparent Pictures" (Critical Inquiry 1984) and Cavell's* The World Viewed *(Harvard enlarged ed. 1979) follow the pagination recorded in the project knowledge bases and should be re-verified against the originals before submission.*

## Abstract

The philosophy of self-knowledge is governed by a presupposition I call the *knowledge-telos*: that whatever is distinctive about a subject's relation to her own mind must be vindicated as a form of knowledge with superior epistemic standing. The telos is shared across the empiricist and agentialist readings of the "transparency method" and even by the expressivisms that were meant to escape it; it is what makes the field treat "this is not knowledge" as a *reductio*. This paper argues that the telos is optional and, in its characteristic form, a skeptical symptom; and it offers a positive replacement for the avowable attitudes. The argument proceeds by two detours. The first, through Kendall Walton's account of photographic transparency, supplies an existence-proof — a looking-through relation can be genuine contact and yet not a relation of knowledge — and a contrast that isolates what the first-person relation is not. The second, through Stanley Cavell, supplies the diagnosis: the demand that a relation *be* knowledge rather than be *acknowledged* is the skeptical impulse, and the specifically transparency-shaped form of the knowledge-telos — the model on which self-ascription is an automatic, agent-bracketed mechanism — is the *naturalization of a stance into a nature*, which is *myth* in Cavell's exact sense and, by his own account, "the price of skepticism." The positive proposal is that first-person access to one's avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes is *acknowledgment*: a stance of owning, not a mode of detection. A corollary is that the long dispute over what *warrants* the transparency method dissolves.

## 1. Introduction

Asked whether you believe it will rain tomorrow, you answer by attending not to your mind but to the sky. Gareth Evans made the observation canonical: "in making a self-ascription of belief, one's eyes are, so to speak, or occasionally literally, directed outward — upon the world" (Evans 1982: 225). This is the *transparency method*: a question about one's own attitude is settled by considering the worldly matter the attitude is about. One looks, the metaphor runs, *through* the belief to what it is of.

The word "transparency" has a second home. In "Transparent Pictures," Kendall Walton argues that photographs are transparent in a literal sense: we see the world *through* them, as we see things through telescopes and mirrors (Walton 1984: 251). The coincidence of vocabulary across the two literatures is, on its face, merely lexical, and to my knowledge it has never been exploited.

This paper exploits it, but inversely. I do not argue that the mind is perceptually transparent in Walton's sense. I argue that Walton's account, because it furnishes a rigorous specimen of a looking-through relation that is *contact without knowledge*, lets us locate the first-person relation by triangulation — as neither perceptual contact (Walton's relation) nor knowledge-access (the self-knowledge literature's assumption) but a third thing, which Cavell's concept of *acknowledgment* names.

The central claim can be stated as a thesis and an explanation. The thesis: (T) for a subject's avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes, first-person access is constituted by *acknowledgment* — a first-personal stance of owning the attitude — and not by any relation of knowledge, detection, or epistemic access. The explanation: the field has not seen (T) because it is in the grip of the *knowledge-telos*, the assumption that the distinctive first-person relation must be a relation of knowledge; and that grip is, I will argue, a form of the skeptical impulse Cavell diagnoses.

I want to be explicit about the paper's ambition, since precision about it forestalls a predictable misreading. The positive content of (T) lies close to the agentialist (Moran) and expressivist (Bar-On, Campbell) accounts already in the field; I do not claim to have discovered that self-ascriptions are avowals or that authorship matters. What I claim is new is (i) the *route* — the use of Walton to establish the optionality of the telos and the use of Cavell's *myth* to diagnose its grip; (ii) the *etiological* thesis that the telos is a skeptical symptom; and (iii) the structural identification of acknowledgment as the single operation that answers naturalized convention at the level of the medium and naturalized agency at the level of the mind. Where (T)'s content overlaps an existing view I will mark the overlap and the difference rather than paper over it.

I give the argument in regimented form at the end of §2, then defend its premises in turn: P1 in §2, P2 in §3 (Walton), P3 in §4 (Cavell, the diagnosis), P4 in §5 (Cavell, the myth), P5 in §6 (the positive view). §7 draws the consequence for the warrant-dispute; §8 answers objections; §9 concludes.

## 2. The knowledge-telos

Let me first fix the target with some care, because the argument turns on its being a genuine and *shared* commitment rather than a thesis of one party.

**Definition.** The *knowledge-telos* (hereafter the telos) is the conjunction of two claims: (a) the *explanandum* of a theory of self-knowledge is a relation of *knowledge* — true, warranted, second-order representation of one's own states; and (b) a theory *succeeds* to the extent that it vindicates this relation as epistemically superior (more secure, better warranted, or more authoritative) to ordinary empirical knowledge. On the telos, to deny that the distinctive first-person relation is a relation of knowledge is not to redescribe it but to deny that there is anything distinctive to theorize.

That the telos is *shared* is shown by surveying the camps that otherwise divide the field.

(i) The empiricist reading of transparency holds that the method yields knowledge because it is reliable. Byrne's "doxastic schema" — from *p*, infer "I believe that *p*" — is defended as *self-verifying* and *safe*: such self-ascriptions could not easily have been false (Byrne 2005, 2018). The credential is externalist warrant; the explanandum is knowledge.

(ii) The agentialist reading holds that the method works because the subject is *responsible* for her attitudes. Moran argues that a rational subject has an "epistemic right" to assume her attitudes answer to her reasons, and that this entitlement licenses the transparency procedure (Moran 2001). The ground is normative, not reliabilist; but the payoff is, again, that the self-ascriptions are *knowledge* (Moran 2001: 64, treats the deliverances as known, even while locating their distinctiveness in "responsibility and commitment").

(iii) Even expressivism, framed against this whole picture, keeps the telos. The "simple" expressivism that abandons the knowledge-claim — treating avowals as neither true nor false — is universally treated as a position to be *avoided*; the live versions insist that avowals are or express *knowledge*. Bar-On holds that avowals are "genuine and privileged self-*knowledge*" despite their groundlessness (Bar-On 2004: 405); Campbell builds an account on which the avowal *expresses knowledge* of the state (Campbell 2020, 2022).

The decisive evidence is meta-level. Gertler, defending the empiricist side, characterizes the empiricism–agentialism dispute as one "about the epistemic *basis* for self-knowledge, not about … the method" (Gertler 2018). This is exactly the telos seen from inside: both camps agree there is a *knowledge-output*; they dispute only its *warrant*. It is the shared assumption of a knowledge-output, not either camp's account of its warrant, that I put in question.

**The master argument.** With the target fixed, here is the argument of the paper.

- **P1.** Every major account of self-knowledge embraces the telos, and the empiricist–agentialist dispute over the transparency method is a dispute over the *warrant* of an assumed knowledge-output (this §).
- **P2.** A looking-through relation can be genuine contact and yet *not* a relation of knowledge; this is coherent and instantiated (Walton's photographic transparency). Hence the telos's identification of "the distinctive first-person relation" with "a relation of knowledge" is an *optional* commitment, not a conceptual necessity; and the first-person relation, measured against Walton's, is *not* perceptual contact (§3).
- **P3.** The demand that a relation *be* knowledge — that it be secured as warranted certainty rather than *acknowledged* — is the skeptical impulse; transposed to the first person, the telos is that impulse, and its standing alternative is *self-acknowledgment* (§4).
- **P4.** The specifically transparency-shaped form of the telos — the *detector-model*, on which self-ascription is an automatic, agent-bracketed mechanism — is the *naturalization of a stance into a nature*: a *myth* in Cavell's precise sense, which by his account is "the price of skepticism." So the detector-model is not a neutral rival but the skeptical disowning of first-person agency (§5).
- **P5.** First-person access to avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes is better characterized as *acknowledgment* (owning) than as knowledge-access: its failure-signature is self-deception rather than misperception; it is groundless and ineliminably first-personal; and it *preserves* the standard explananda without the over-intellectualization the telos imposes (§6).
- **C.** Therefore (i) the telos should be rejected for the avowable attitudes in favor of (T); and (ii) the warrant-dispute dissolves, leaving reliable *tracking* (Byrne) and responsible *owning* (Moran, shorn of its epistemic certification — i.e., acknowledgment) as correct descriptions of two different things, neither of which is a warranted knowledge-output (§7).

The argument is valid: P2 establishes the telos is dispensable, P3–P4 that it is positively suspect, P5 that the dispensing has a determinate and adequate replacement, and C follows by detaching the telos from the data it was introduced to explain. The work is in the premises.

## 3. P2: the Waltonian detour — contact without knowledge

Walton's thesis is that to see a photograph of *x* is, literally, to see *x*, with photographic assistance (Walton 1984: 251); paintings, by contrast, are *opaque* — before a portrait one sees only a representation (1984: 253). What grounds transparency is not appearance but the *manner of causation*: a "purely mechanical," belief-independent process connects scene to image, such that the image counterfactually depends on the scene even with the maker's beliefs held fixed — "the camera bypasses the photographer's hallucination and captures what is in the jungle" (1984: 261, 264).

The premise I need is what Walton says about the relation of this contact to knowledge. He severs them. Transparency is independent of accuracy, detail, and information; we see the dead through old snapshots though we learn nothing (1984: 251, 273). His sharpest case is a mirror whose surface flexes unpredictably, so that it "is not a knowledge-producing mechanism": beliefs formed through it are not knowledge, "but this does not mean that he does not *see* the reflected things" (1984: 259). The moral is stated in propria persona: "Perceptual contact with things has rather less to do with acquiring knowledge about them than has sometimes been supposed" (1984: 269). Here, fully worked out, is a looking-through relation that is *contact* and *not knowledge*.

This yields P2 in two steps.

*The existence-proof.* The telos assumes that a relation must be a relation of knowledge to be a distinctive relation worth theorizing; "it is not knowledge" is treated as equivalent to "it is nothing of interest." Walton's transparency refutes the equivalence: here is a non-degenerate, humanly important relation — we prize the photographs of our dead — that is constituted neither by warrant nor by reliability nor by knowledge. So a relation's failing to be knowledge does not show it to be nothing. The conceptual room the telos denies is actual.

*The contrast.* I must now block a tempting but fallacious version of the argument. One might try to argue: the self-knowledge "transparency method" is called transparency; measured against Walton's marks of perceptual transparency it satisfies none; therefore its "transparency" is a sham. This inference is invalid, because no party to the self-knowledge debate asserts perceptual transparency of the mental in Walton's sense.[^strawman] Evans's "looking through" is a metaphor for *declining to attend to* the belief and attending to the world instead — nearly the opposite of Walton's relation, in which one ends in perceptual contact with the object on the far side. The lexical coincidence is just that.

The contrast does its work without that fallacy. Walton fixes one corner of the space with precision: a looking-through that is *perceptual contact*, belief-independent, with the world on the far side. The first-person relation does not occupy that corner — one does not perceive one's belief; there is no belief-independent channel by which one's believing reaches awareness as the jungle reaches the film. So whatever the first-person relation is, it is not Waltonian contact. P2 thus delivers a *disjunctive* result that the next sections discharge: the first-person relation is neither perceptual contact nor, as I shall argue, knowledge-access. Walton does not tell us what it *is*; he tells us, with unusual precision, two things it is not, and he proves that the second of these ("not knowledge") is not the dead end the telos assumes.

## 4. P3: the Cavellian diagnosis — knowing and acknowledging

Cavell's lifelong response to skepticism is to refuse the skeptic's demand rather than meet it. The skeptic about the external world or about other minds craves *certainty* — a securing of our relation to the world or to others that would convert it into indubitable *knowledge*. Cavell's claim is that this craving misdescribes the relation we are actually in, and that the relation's truth is not *knowing* but *acknowledging* (Cavell 1969; 1979a).

The contrast is sharpest for other minds. I do not *infer* your pain across the screen of your behavior, holding my verdict hostage to a justification the skeptic can reopen; your pain makes a *claim* on me that I meet or evade. Acknowledgment is not a more secure species of knowing but a different category of response, whose characteristic failure is not *error* but *avoidance*. In *The World Viewed* Cavell fixes the distinction, after Wittgenstein, in a sentence I will use repeatedly: "a true statement is something we know or do not know; a *truthful* statement is one we must *acknowledge* or fail or refuse to acknowledge" (1979b: 157). Truth turns on evidence and is *known*; truthfulness turns on acceptance and must be *acknowledged*. And acknowledgment is not the abandonment of knowledge but, in Cavell's phrase, "the home of knowledge" — its ground, not its competitor.[^home]

P3 is the transposition of this contrast to the first person. *If* the demand that our relation to the world be knowledge is the skeptical reflex, *then* the knowledge-telos — the demand that the subject's relation to her *own mind* be knowledge — is that same reflex turned inward; and its alternative is not better self-*knowing* but *self-acknowledgment*.

The conditional is doing honest work here, and I flag two things about it. First, the antecedent is Cavell's; the consequent — the application to self-knowledge — is my extension. Cavell directs acknowledgment against skepticism about *other* minds and the external world, not against the problem of self-knowledge.[^moran-cavell] Second, the transposition is not licensed merely by analogy; it is licensed by P4 and P5, which show independently that the first-person relation has the marks of acknowledgment (a stance whose failure is avoidance) rather than of knowing (a representation whose failure is error). §4 establishes only that *there is a standing alternative to the telos* with a worked-out shape; §§5–6 establish that the alternative *fits*. I take encouragement, but claim no proof, from a passage where Cavell himself casts self-knowledge in these terms — "The knowledge of the self as it is always takes place in the betrayal of the self as it was … the form of self-revelation" (1979b: 160) — while noting that its register is existential rather than epistemological.

## 5. P4: transparency as naturalized convention — the myth in the method

The Cavellian diagnosis acquires its teeth from a feature of his account of film that has no analogue in Walton, and that an interlocutor rightly pressed on me: Cavell's treatment of cinematic *convention*. The premise P4 needs is that transparency, in the mode that matters, is the *naturalization of convention* — and that this naturalization is what Cavell calls *myth* and ties directly to skepticism.

Take Cavell's example of the arrival of color (Cavell 1979b, ch. 13). One might expect color to be a *convention* in the ordinary sense: a stylistic resource the filmmaker selects and the audience registers *as* a selection, the way one registers a painter's palette. Cavell denies this. When color becomes a *constitutive* condition of the medium rather than mere "packaging," it ceases to present itself as a convention at all; it reorganizes the *world* the film projects — de-theatricalizing and de-psychologizing it, replacing the "Once upon a time" of black-and-white drama with the "What if one day…" of an open futurity. Color, in this mode, is not read *as* an artifice laid over a world; it functions as a *condition of a world*, which is to say, as a kind of *nature*. The same holds of the cinematic *type* — the Woman, the Dandy, the Military Man — which is, Cavell says, "not the kind [of character] an author creates, but the kind that certain real people are" (1979b, chs. 4–5). The convention (the type) operates not as a recognized stipulation, as the soliloquy operates in the theater, but as a *natural kind*.

This naturalizing of convention is, for Cavell, the work of *myth*: an inherited wish an art satisfies so completely that the satisfaction passes for nature. Film's founding myth, in the Bazinian phrase Cavell adopts, is the "myth of total cinema" — the world "re-created in its own image," delivered "automatically," so that we "view the world unseen" (1979b: 39–40).[^myth] And Cavell ties this mythic mode directly to skepticism: viewing the world *unseen* — having its presence without one's own answerable presence to it — is, he says, "exactly the price of skepticism" (1979b: 40; compare the screen that "screens me from the world it holds … makes me invisible," 1979b: 24). The structure is the same one isolated in §4: the skeptical wish is the wish for the world's givenness *minus the subject's exposed presence to it*, and myth is that wish materialized — convention functioning as nature, the made passing for the given, the subject absented.

Two cases must now be distinguished, and distinguishing them is what licenses the transfer to self-knowledge. In Walton's photographic transparency there is *genuine nature*: the mechanical, belief-independent process really does proceed without a believing subject in the causal chain; no convention is in play, and the limit case is not mythic but simply mechanical. In Cavell's cinematic transparency there is *naturalized convention*: a made form — color, type, genre — comes to function with the givenness of nature. Myth is the second case, not the first; its danger is the mistaking of a naturalized convention *for* a nature, and with it the absenting of the subject whose convention it is.

Now apply this to the detector-model of self-knowledge — Byrne's reliable, agent-bracketed schema, on which "I believe *p*" issues automatically from *p*. Granting the argument of §6 to come — that the first-person relation is in fact an owned *stance* — the detector-model is the naturalization of that stance. It takes what is *done* (the owning of a commitment) and presents it as what *happens* (a mechanism that runs, the agent bracketed for the purpose of warrant). That is precisely the structure of myth: a convention — here the agential stance of acknowledgment — functioning as a nature. And since the mythic mode is, by Cavell's own account, "the price of skepticism," the detector-model is not a neutral competitor among accounts of self-knowledge but the *skeptical disowning of first-person agency*: the wish to have one's own attitudes *given* to one, viewed unseen, automatically, rather than *owned* by one. The temptation that the word "transparency" carries from the cinema into the philosophy of mind is the temptation to mistake a naturalized convention for a nature — at the medium in the first case, at the self in the second.

This furnishes the positive view with its sharpest formulation, and completes the diagnosis. Cavell's standing answer to myth is not a counter-myth but *acknowledgment*: the modernist work *acknowledges* its conditions — owns *as* conditions what the mythic work lets pass *as* nature (1979b: 110, 146). Self-acknowledgment is the very same operation performed on one's own attitudes: it owns *as* a stance what the detector-model lets pass *as* a mechanism. The concept that names the cure at the level of the medium names it again at the level of the mind, and for the same reason — in both, acknowledgment is the un-naturalizing of a convention, the replacement of "this is simply given" with "this is mine to own."[^rhyme] P4 is thus not a flourish appended to the diagnosis; it converts the diagnosis from the modest claim that the telos is *optional* (P2) and *suspect* (P3) into the stronger claim that its transparency-shaped form is *positively a skeptical myth*, and it identifies the positive view of §6 as the determinate operation that dispels it.

## 6. P5: the positive view — self-knowledge as acknowledgment

The proposal, stated for its proper domain: for a subject's *avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes* — paradigmatically beliefs and intentions — first-person access is not the detection of a standing inner fact but a stance of *owning* it, a first-personal answerability for the attitude as hers to defend, revise, and act on. I argue for this by its marks and by its preservation of the data.

**Three marks distinguish owning from detection.**

(1) *Failure-signature.* The characteristic failure of first-person access to an attitude is not misperception but *self-deception* — evasion, disavowal, bad faith. This is a structural datum, and it discriminates the models. A detection-relation has the failure-signature of *misreading*: one confuses items that are genuinely similar, as one misreads a gauge; indeed Walton makes the *shape* of a relation's liability to error criterial for its being perceptual — perceptual confusions track real similarity (an eighty-five-foot tree confused with an eighty-four-foot one, not with the word "tree") (1984: 270–271). First-person error does not have this shape. Its liabilities track *what one can face about oneself*, not a similarity-space of inner items. A relation whose failures are failures of honesty is not a relation of detection.

(2) *Groundlessness.* The subject who avows her belief offers no evidence and passes through no intervening judgment, because owning a commitment is not the upshot of an inquiry into whether one has it. (This mark the present view shares with expressivism; I claim no novelty for it.)

(3) *Ineliminable first-personality.* Another may *attribute* an attitude to me on evidence; only I can *own* mine. The asymmetry is one of authorship, not of epistemic access.

**Preservation of the explananda.** I claim that the data that made self-knowledge look special are *preserved* under (T), not that they are derived from nothing; the rival accounts likewise assume their organizing intuitions, and I assume no more.[^preserve] (a) *Asymmetry* becomes the answerability/authorship asymmetry of mark (3) — Moran's insight, freed from the demand that the asymmetry be epistemic. (b) *First-person authority* — the presumption of truth on "I believe it's raining," the impropriety of "no, you don't" — becomes the standing of the one who owns: to meet an avowal with "no, you don't" is not to correct a measurement but to refuse the subject her authorship, which is Cavell's truth/truthfulness point exactly (1979b: 157). (c) *Moore's paradox* — the irrationality of "it's raining but I don't believe it" — becomes the incoherence of disavowing what one is in the act of asserting: to assert *p* is to own the commitment to *p*, so to deny that commitment in the same breath is incoherent at the level of owning, not of evidence. (d) *Groundless immediacy* is native to owning, as in mark (2).

**Two defenses.** Marks and preservations aside, two standing objections target this section's claims directly.

The asymmetry claim faces the charge, pressed by Reed (2010), Borgoni (2015), and most sharply by Doyle (2019), that *detached* self-observation does not preclude ownership and can be *enlightening* — that coming to understand a recalcitrant attitude by treating it, for a time, as an object of inquiry is not alienation but a route to self-understanding. I grant the cases without reservation, and observe which cases they are: cases of attitudes the subject *cannot presently own* — the recalcitrant, the self-deceived, the akratic. That is exactly the breakdown profile predicted by mark (1), on which the signature failure of first-person access is the failure to own. Detached self-observation is the *remedial supplement* one reaches for when owning has failed; its value in the breakdown cases is no evidence that owning is not the constitutive mode in the cases in good order. A theory of vision is not refuted by spectacles.

The authority claim lands near the constitutive/performative accounts of Coliva (2012) and Bilgrami (2006), which already explain first-person authority without Cavell: for Coliva, to judge "I believe that *p*" is to *undertake* the commitment one ascribes, so the self-ascription is authoritative because self-fulfilling. Coliva is the closest analytic kin to (T), and I do not claim the performativity as my discovery. The difference is decisive for this paper's thesis: Coliva's performative is still aimed at making the self-ascription *true* — it secures a knowledge-output by a different mechanism, and so remains inside the telos. Acknowledgment *subsumes* the performativity (owning a commitment is indeed something one does, not something one reads off) while *dropping* the requirement that the point of the doing is to render a second-order belief true. What one does in avowing is not manufacture a truth-maker for "I believe *p*"; it is stand behind *p* as one's own.

This same difference separates (T) from the position it most resembles in the recent literature. Campbell's "expression without expressivism" (Campbell 2022) unifies the expressivist insight that self-ascriptions are avowals with a broadly Moranian, agency-inflected epistemology — explicitly *in order to preserve* self-knowledge as a distinctive *epistemic* kind. Campbell stays inside the telos; that is the announced burden of her title. (T) is the contrary move: keep the expression and the agency, relocate the phenomenon *off* the axis of knowledge. The two views therefore agree about much at the level of phenomena and disagree about the one thing at issue here. I take Campbell's account to be the strongest *foil* — the best unification that declines to leave the telos — and also a debtor's opportunity: Campbell herself flags that, to *explain* rather than presuppose first-person privilege, her "rational response" relation must finally be analyzed in non-epistemic terms, and marks this as unfinished. Acknowledgment is a candidate for exactly that analysis. Where Campbell unifies to *save* the telos, (T) supplies the non-epistemic story she leaves open and lets the telos go.[^rodl]

**Scope.** I restrict (T) to the avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes and do not extend it to sensations. The acquaintance theorists are plausibly right that one's relation to a present pain is not a matter of *owning* it — there is "no appearance/reality distinction in the case of sensations" (Hill 1991: 127) — and answerability is a poor model for an itch. A hybrid is therefore both available and, I think, correct: acquaintance for the sensory, acknowledgment for the attitudinal. This is a sharpening, not a retreat: the attitudes are precisely the ground on which the empiricist and agentialist readings of transparency actually contend, and it is there that (T) is meant to bite.

## 7. The consequence: the warrant-dispute dissolved

I can now discharge clause (ii) of the conclusion. Byrne and Moran conduct, in Gertler's accurate description, a dispute over the *epistemic basis* of the transparency method: granted that it delivers self-knowledge, what *warrants* the delivery — reliability or responsibility? If (T) is correct, this dispute rests on the telos and lapses with it, in a determinate way.

If first-person access to the attitudes is acknowledgment rather than a knowledge-output, then there is no knowledge-output whose warrant is in question, and the question of its warrant has no subject matter. What remains, once the shared presupposition is withdrawn, is two correct descriptions of two *different* things. Byrne has described a genuine and reliable *correlation*: from the world's being thus, one can move safely to a true second-order self-ascription — a real phenomenon of self-*tracking*, correctly modeled by his externalism. But it is a description in which the agent is *bracketed for the purpose of warrant*: the credential is safety, and safety is indifferent to whether the subject can make first-personal sense of the transition — which is exactly why Boyle could object that the inference "won't seem reasonable to the thinker" (Boyle 2011). A reliable detector, however accurate, has no *authority*, only a track record. Moran, by contrast, has described the real phenomenon of *owning*: the subject as responsible author of her attitudes. His error is not in the description but in the certification — having located the distinctive thing in responsibility and commitment "as much as" in cognitive access (Moran 2001: 64), he nonetheless requires the upshot to be classified as *knowledge*, and it is that residual certification, not the agential insight, that keeps him inside the warrant-dispute and exposes him to the charge of being "too demanding" (O'Brien 2003).[^obrien]

So the dispute is partly verbal and partly substantive, and the diagnosis sorts the parts. It is *verbal* insofar as the two camps describe two real but different phenomena — reliable tracking and responsible owning — and mislabel both "self-knowledge." It is *substantive* insofar as each claims to have characterized *the distinctive first-person relation* — and there the dispute is not adjudicated but dissolved, because the distinctive relation is neither tracking nor a warranted knowledge-output but acknowledgment, of which Moran's "owning" is the correct description once its epistemic certification is removed. The decades of argument over the warrant of transparency were a real argument about a presupposition that, once seen as a presupposition, one can simply decline.

## 8. Objections and replies

**Objection 1 (against P5): you have merely relabeled expressivism.** This is the objection to take most seriously, since (T)'s content lies near Bar-On and Campbell. Three differences answer it. First, the paper's primary product is not an account of avowals but a *diagnosis of a debate* (P1, P4): the claim that the empiricist and agentialist readings are two distortions of one relation and that the telos is a skeptical myth — a claim expressivism, appearing as one account among several, does not make. Second, (T) is not, like Bar-On's thesis, that avowals express *knowledge*; it is that the relation is not on the knowledge axis at all — the very step Campbell's "without expressivism" declines and her own unfinished business invites. Third, the route is new: no one has used Walton to make the conceptual room or Cavell's *myth* to diagnose the telos's grip. The novelty claimed is the diagnostic instrument and its route, not the datum that self-ascriptions are avowals.

**Objection 2 (against C): you have abandoned knowledge and changed the subject.** I have re-grounded knowledge, not abandoned it. On the Cavellian picture acknowledgment is "the home of knowledge": downstream of acknowledging an attitude one may of course hold a true and even warranted second-order belief about it, and nothing in (T) denies this. What (T) denies is that this second-order belief, or its warrant, is the *distinctive* and *fundamental* first-person fact or the source of authority. The explananda are preserved (§6), and preserved without the over-intellectualization the telos imposes. The subject has been re-grounded, not changed.

**Objection 3 (against P2): the detour proves nothing; an analogy with photographs cannot settle anything about the mind.** Agreed, and the argument rests on no such analogy. Walton's role is exhausted by the two functions of §3 — an existence-proof that a looking-through relation *need not* be a knowledge-relation, and a contrast isolating what the first-person relation is *not*. The positive identification is carried by Cavell (§§4–5) and by the marks of §6, not by any claim that minds resemble cameras. Indeed the disanalogy is load-bearing: Walton's transparency is grounded in the *absence* of the subject from the causal chain, whereas the first-person relation is constitutively the subject's *presence* — which is why (§5) the detector-model's importation of Waltonian automatism into the self is precisely the error (naturalized agency, myth), not the model.

**Objection 4 (against P4): "myth" and "the price of skepticism" are Cavell's terms of art for film; transposing them to Byrne's schema is a pun.** The transposition is a reconstruction, and I have flagged it as such; but it is not a pun, because the *structure* transposed is topic-neutral. Myth, in the sense used, is *naturalized convention* — a made form (a stance, a genre) functioning as a given nature, with the subject whose form it is absented. That structure is instantiated by the detector-model exactly: an owned stance presented as an agent-bracketed mechanism. The Cavellian vocabulary earns its place by marking a structure the case actually has, not by importing cinematic atmosphere.

**Objection 5 (against P5): acknowledgment is freighted with existential drama mundane self-ascription lacks.** Distinguish the *form* (owning, answerability) from its *intensity*. The form is present in every case, however flat — which is why even the most trivial Moorean conjunction registers as a failure of owning rather than of evidence. The existential cases are where the form is most visible, not the only cases where it operates.

## 9. Conclusion

The philosophy of self-knowledge has assumed, almost without exception, that its subject matter must be a kind of knowledge. I have called this the knowledge-telos and argued that it is a presupposition and a suspect one. A Waltonian existence-proof shows the room it denies to be real; a Cavellian diagnosis shows its grip to be a form of the skeptical impulse; and the specifically transparency-shaped version of it — the model on which we *detect* our attitudes by an automatic, agent-bracketed mechanism — is, in Cavell's exact sense, a *myth*: a stance naturalized into a nature, the made passing for the given, the subject absented. The cure is the operation Cavell already names — *acknowledgment* — which owns as a stance what the myth lets pass as a mechanism, and which is the same operation at the level of the mind that the modernist's acknowledgment of his conditions is at the level of the medium. For our avowable attitudes, first-person access is owning, not looking through; and the long dispute over what warrants the transparency method dissolves, leaving two descriptions of two different things and one distinctive relation that is neither.

The claims have been kept deliberately bounded. The positive view is close kin to the agentialist and expressivist proposals; what is new is the route, the diagnosis, and the identification of acknowledgment as the single un-naturalizing operation that answers myth at both levels. A more ambitious sequel would press the diagnosis past the transparency theorists to the framing assumptions of the whole field — including the skeptical *denials* of distinctiveness that are the telos's disappointed mirror image — but that is another paper. It is enough, here, to have shown that the first relation each of us bears to her own mind may be less like seeing through a clear pane and more like standing behind one's word.

[^myth]: The link "convention functioning as nature = myth" is my regimentation of Cavell, not a sentence of his; but it is well grounded. Cavell's *type* is explicitly a convention that is "not the kind an author creates, but the kind that certain real people are" (chs. 4–5); his *myth* is an inherited wish an art satisfies; and his standing contrast is between *acknowledging* a condition and letting it function unacknowledged. The naturalized-convention reading collects these. Page references in this section (esp. "view the world unseen … the price of skepticism," ≈39–40, and the color material, ch. 13) should be re-verified against the enlarged edition.

[^rhyme]: That acknowledgment is the *same* operation at the level of medium and of mind is my structural claim, not Cavell's; he does not draw it. I rest it not on the word "acknowledgment" but on the operation the word names in both cases — the un-naturalizing of a convention, the conversion of "this is given" into "this is mine to own." If the two operations were merely homonymous the claim would be a pun; the burden discharged in §§5–6 is to show they are one.

[^preserve]: The contrast with the rivals is instructive. Silins (2020) *derives* the irrationality of Moore-paradoxical judgment from the premise that judging *p* gives propositional justification to self-ascribe belief that *p*; the rationalists derive it from rationality-constitution. (T)'s treatment of the same datum is not more deeply derived — it too begins from an identity (asserting/owning) it does not earn from outside — and I therefore advertise *preservation*, not derivation, to avoid overclaiming.

[^rodl]: The nearest precedent for the bare thesis that self-knowledge is not a relation of epistemic access is Rödl (2007), reached through Kant and the theory of self-consciousness; but Rödl treats self-knowledge as "knowledge from spontaneity" rather than abandoning the category of knowledge, and arrives with no Walton and no Cavell. I differ in route and, arguably, in category (a stance of answerability rather than a non-receptive species of knowledge). Cite as ally-at-a-distance; claim only the route.
