# Owning Up: Against the Knowledge-Telos in the Theory of Self-Knowledge

*Draft v4 — working paper. This version rebuilds §§3–7 to meet the red-team's central objection (that §4's premise "warrant is receptive" is refuted by constitutive, basic, and entitlement-based warrants, and that the positive thesis is itself a non-receptive warrant). The repair reformulates the telos's demand and pays for the resulting commitment openly in §6–§7. Self-standing; the Walton/Cavell route is a companion only (n. 1). Citations should be re-verified against primary texts before submission.*

## Abstract

Theories of how a subject knows her own beliefs and intentions disagree about almost everything except one assumption: that what they theorize is *knowledge of one's attitudes as states one must get right*, and that the task is to find the relation that warrants it. I call this the *knowledge-telos*. I argue that the telos is a substantive presupposition, not a neutral statement of the explanandum, and that it is the common source of the field's standing problems. The diagnosis turns on a distinction: a *warrant of an independent state* — the credit a belief earns by being answerable to a fact that obtains anyway — is necessarily *receptive*; but the first-person specialness of self-knowledge (authority, immediacy, non-alienation) is the mark of a relation that is *not* answerability to a found fact. The telos demands one relation be both, and cannot have it; the field's objections are the recurring symptoms of the impossible demand. I argue we should abandon the telos, and that the abandonment carries an ontological lesson it has obscured: for the avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes there is no fully owning-independent fact for a receptive warrant to track, because such attitudes are commitments a subject *sustains* rather than states she *finds*. The positive view — the *owning thesis* — is that first-person access to these attitudes is a stance of answerability that *makes* one's self-ascription true rather than tracking an antecedent fact; the knowledge we have of our own minds is the authoritative registering of an owning, not the detection of an inner object. The view's real commitment, and its real opponent, is thereby located: a constitutivism about the attitudes, against Humean dispositionalism.

## 1. Introduction

A subject's relation to her own beliefs and intentions seems unlike her relation to anyone else's and unlike her relation to the weather. Asked whether she believes it will rain, she does not investigate herself; she can simply *say*, with an authority others extend to her as a matter of course, and without the felt possibility of looking and finding herself mistaken in the way the barometer might be misread. To explain this — the immediacy, the authority, the asymmetry of the first person — is the business of the theory of self-knowledge.

The field is a catalogue of candidate relations: inner observation, self-interpretation, a reliable inference from the world, a rational transition from one's own judgment, an exercise of agency, an expression. Each account is, at bottom, a claim about *which relation* a subject bears to her attitude when she knows it, and each has met an objection that has kept the debate alive without resolution.

I shall argue that the non-resolution is not accidental and that its cause is a presupposition the parties share. The presupposition — the *knowledge-telos* — is that the thing to be explained is *knowledge of one's attitudes as states one must get right*: that a good account must vindicate self-ascriptions as true, warranted, and epistemically superior, and so must find the relation that *warrants* this access to an antecedent inner fact. I argue (i) that the telos is genuinely shared (§3); (ii) that it *generates* the field's problems, because it requires one relation to be at once a *warrant of an independent state* (which is receptive) and the seat of first-person *specialness* (which is non-receptive) — and these cannot be the same relation (§4); (iii) that we should therefore abandon it, and that doing so reveals an ontological point the telos hid — that the avowable attitudes are not the owning-independent states the telos took them for (§5); and (iv) that what is left standing is the *owning thesis*:

> (T) For a subject's avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes, first-person access is a first-personal stance of *owning* the attitude — of answerability for it — which *makes* the corresponding self-ascription true rather than tracking an antecedent fact; the distinctiveness of self-knowledge is in the first instance practical, and the knowledge we have of our attitudes is the authoritative registering of an owning, not the detection of an inner object.

I develop (T) in §6, where the scope restriction is *derived* rather than stipulated, and defend it in §7, where I meet the objection that it changes the subject ("we *do* come to know what we believe") and locate the view's genuine cost: a commitment about the *nature* of the attitudes that a Humean will contest.

The contribution is the diagnosis and what it forces, not the materials. That self-ascription is more like owning than detecting is the shared property of the agentialist and expressivist traditions, and I claim no priority for it. What I claim is that the field's problems are one problem; that the problem is the telos; and that the agentialist and expressivist insights have failed to land only because they were still made to deliver the telos's prize — knowledge of an independent inner state — which their own non-receptive relations cannot deliver and need not.

## 2. The accounts and their objections

I group the accounts by the *kind* of relation each proposes, since the grouping is what the diagnosis exploits, and I restrict throughout to the avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes (belief, intention), returning to sensations under scope (§6).[^kb]

**(a) Receptive relations: observation and inference.** Inner-sense theories treat self-knowledge as quasi-perceptual monitoring (Armstrong 1968; Lycan 1996); self-interpretation theories treat it as inference to the best explanation of one's behaviour and inner speech (Ryle 1949; Carruthers 2011; Cassam 2014). Each credits the subject with self-knowledge by making her suitably *informed about* a state that obtains independently of her registering it. The standing objection is that this abolishes the distinctiveness it was to explain: if knowing my mind is observing or inferring, it is the same in kind as knowing yours, the asymmetry is "at most a matter of degree" (Ryle 1949), and Boghossian's (1989) trilemma follows — inner observation cannot reveal a relationally individuated attitude's relational features, inference is not privileged, and access "on the basis of nothing" is not access. Moran (2001) adds that to know one's own attitude by self-observation is to be *alienated* from it.

**(b) A reliable transition: empiricist transparency.** Evans (1982) noted that we answer "do I believe *p*?" by considering *p*; Byrne (2005, 2018) regiments this as inference on a self-verifying, *safe* schema. Boyle (2011) objects that a reliable transition "will not seem reasonable to the thinker," since *p* does not in general support "I believe *p*"; Shah and Velleman (2005) that considering *p* may *produce* a belief rather than reveal one; Valaris (2011) and Barnett (2016) that the "inference" cannot function as inference. The relation is creditable but first-personally opaque.

**(c) The attempted middle: reasons and rationality.** Peacocke (1999) grounds the self-ascription in a conscious *judgment* via the conceptual link between judging and believing; rationalists (Shoemaker 1996; Stoljar 2018; Smithies 2019) hold that rational subjects are constitutively not "self-blind." Coliva (2008) objects that a thought's phenomenology does not reveal *whether it is a judgment or a wish*, so the rational base is not accessible enough to do epistemic work; and the constitutive guarantee faces a dilemma (Kornblith 2012; Cassam 2014) between a rationality too demanding to be ours and one too weak to guarantee anything.

**(d) Agency.** Agentialism locates the distinctive thing in our *agency over* our attitudes — "asymmetries of responsibility and commitment" (Moran 2001), the driver not the passenger (McGeer 1996). This captures what the receptive accounts lose. But O'Brien (2003) objects that Moran is "too demanding," requiring a reflective grasp of one's reasons *as evidence that one believes*; the scope objection notes that agency reaches only the reasons-sensitive attitudes; and the *Stanford Encyclopedia*, in its own voice, presses the deepest point: most knowledge consists in the belief's being "controlled by what is known," whereas agency is "control over what is known," so "agentialism is not a genuine competitor unless it explains how agency provides self-knowledge" (Gertler 2024, §3.7).

**(e) Expression.** Expressivism treats an avowal as a groundless *expression* of the state it ascribes, resting on no evidence and no intervening judgment (Bar-On 2004; Finkelstein 2003; Campbell 2022). This too captures immediacy and authority — and meets the objection that a groundless, spontaneous expression is the wrong sort of thing to be a *cognitive achievement*, so either avowals are not knowledge (rejected "simple" expressivism) or expressivism owes an epistemology it has not given. Campbell (2022) titles her view "expression *without* expressivism" precisely to keep the knowledge, and grants that to *explain* rather than presuppose first-person privilege her central notion needs a non-epistemic analysis she does not supply.

Five families, five objections. The usual moral is that the search must continue. I argue the five problems are one problem seen five times.

## 3. The common presupposition

Each account picks a relation and asks it to do two jobs: to *warrant* the subject's self-ascription as knowledge, and to seat the *specialness* — asymmetry, immediacy, authority — that makes self-knowledge a distinctive topic. The first job is set by the telos, and the telos must be stated with more care than is usual, because the care is where the repair lives.

**Definition.** The *knowledge-telos* is the demand that a theory vindicate the subject's self-ascriptions as **knowledge of her attitudes** — where "knowledge of X" carries its ordinary weight: a true belief about X *credited by being answerable to X as something that obtains independently of the believing*. The telos thus builds in an ontological assumption it rarely states: that the attitude self-ascribed is an *antecedent, owning-independent fact* — a state that is there to be gotten right or wrong — so that the theoretical task is to identify the relation by which the subject *secures access* to it. On the telos, "but then it is not knowledge of the state" is decisive against a proposal.

The telos is shared, in three ways. *Structurally*: every account, the agential and expressive included, is offered as an account of self-*knowledge* and defended by exhibiting the warrant its relation supplies — reliability, a conceptual link, constitution, entitlement, a "presumption of truth." *By exclusion*: the one position that drops the telos — "simple" expressivism, on which avowals are neither true nor false — is treated throughout not as an option but as a *reductio*. *By the field's own meta-description*: defending empiricism, Gertler frames the empiricism–agentialism dispute as one "about the epistemic *basis* for self-knowledge, not about … the method" (Gertler 2018) — both camps agree there is a *knowledge-output*; they dispute only its warrant.

The telos presents itself as the truism that self-knowledge is, after all, knowledge. It is not a truism. It is the substantive claim that the distinctive first-person relation is a relation of *securing access to an antecedent inner fact* — and §§4–5 are the argument that this claim is both unsatisfiable and, for the attitudes, false.

## 4. The presupposition generates the problems

Introduce a distinction between two ways a self-ascription might be creditable.

Call a relation **receptive** when the credit of the subject's state depends on that state's being *answerable to*, controlled or constrained by, what it is about — as a perception answers to the scene, an inference to the truth of its premises, a reliable belief to the fact it tracks. Call a relation **non-receptive** when the subject's state is *not* credited by answerability to an antecedent fact — as in deciding, committing, avowing, where the subject does not *conform to* a fact but *institutes* or *stands behind* one.

Now two claims, of which only the first was overstated in earlier drafts.

*The telos's prize requires receptivity.* The telos asks for *knowledge of the attitude as an independent state* — a self-ascription credited by being answerable to an antecedent fact about what one believes. But to be credited by answerability to an antecedent fact *just is* to stand in a receptive relation to it. So the specific epistemic good the telos demands — securing of access to an owning-independent inner fact — is receptive by its own definition. (This is the careful claim. It is *not* the claim, rightly refuted by the red team, that *all* warrant is receptive: constitutive warrant, basic non-evidential warrant, and rational entitlement are non-receptive, and they are genuine. The point is that none of them is a warrant *of an independent state*; each either gives up the independence of the fact or fails to credit the belief by answerability to it. They are not counterexamples to the claim that *the telos's prize* is receptive; they are attempts to reach a different shore.)

*First-person specialness is non-receptive.* The asymmetry, immediacy, and authority that make self-knowledge distinctive are the marks of a relation that is *not* answerability to a found fact. I do not stand to my own belief as a tracker to a tracked fact; that is why there is no felt gap to cross, no genuine "let me check," and why my pronouncement carries an authority no observer's could. The authority of "I believe it's raining" is the authority of one *answerable for* the belief, not the reliability of one who has *measured* it.

The telos demands one relation discharge both: a *warrant of an independent state* (receptive) that is also the *seat of first-person specialness* (non-receptive). No relation is both, and the five objections of §2 are the five ways of failing the demand.

The **receptive** accounts (a)–(b) buy the warrant of an independent state and forfeit the specialness. Observation and inference are answerability-relations, so they make self-knowledge continuous with other-knowledge and the subject a spectator of her own attitude (Ryle; Boghossian; Moran's alienation). Empiricist transparency is the instructive case: a receptive relation in first-personal disguise. Byrne's safety is a tracking-credential, and Boyle's objection is exactly that a tracking-credential leaves the relation first-personally opaque — reliable, but not the subject's own.

The **non-receptive** accounts (d)–(e) seat the specialness and cannot, *on the telos's terms*, deliver its prize. Agency and expression capture immediacy and authority precisely because they are not answerability-relations — but for that reason they do not secure access to an independent state, which is what the telos wants. Hence the agentialist's predicament, stated by the SEP in its own voice: control-*over* is not the control-*by* that *knowledge of an antecedent fact* requires, so agentialism is "not a genuine competitor unless it explains how agency provides self-knowledge." The attempt to answer that demand — to make the non-receptive relation yield knowledge-of-an-independent-state after all — is what drives Moran to require a reflective grasp of one's reasons *as evidence* (O'Brien's "too demanding") and what leaves Campbell with an "open problem" she cannot close while keeping the knowledge-claim. The non-receptive relation is asked to reach the receptive shore and strains visibly in the reaching.

The **middle** accounts (c) are crushed because they try to occupy both shores at once: a *rational* (non-receptive) base certified as a warrant *of the attitude* (receptive prize). Coliva's objection — that the judgment is not *accessible* enough to rationalize the self-ascription — is the demand that the rational base be *received*; the rationalist's dilemma is that a constitution strong enough to *guarantee knowledge of the state* is too strong to be our psychology. The middle is where the two demands are pulled apart most visibly, which is why it is the least stable.

So the field's objections are not five difficulties but one, seen five times: the impossibility of a single relation that is both a warrant of an independent state and the non-receptive seat of first-person specialness. That impossibility is the shadow of the telos. A presupposition that deforms every candidate relation into one of two complementary failures is not a neutral statement of the explanandum; it is the problem.

## 5. Why we should abandon it — and what abandoning it reveals

Three considerations, and a fourth that is the heart of the matter.

First, *the problems dissolve only without the telos.* The receptive/non-receptive tension is a tension only because one relation is required to be both a warrant of an independent state and the seat of specialness. Drop the demand that the special relation secure access to an antecedent fact, and the non-receptive relation is freed to be what it is. No rearrangement *within* the telos can do this, because the tension is the telos's own shadow.

Second, *the telos is a presupposition, not a datum.* What we find pre-theoretically is the special relation — the immediacy, authority, asymmetry. That this relation is the *securing of access to an antecedent inner fact* is already theory, and §4 has shown it to be theory that does not work.

Third, *abandoning it explains more.* The very features the non-receptive accounts captured — groundlessness, immediacy, non-alienation, an authority unlike a good detector's — are an embarrassment on the telos (they make self-knowledge look unlike knowledge) and exactly what one expects of a non-receptive relation once the telos is gone.

The fourth consideration is the one the red team forces into the open, and it converts the case from "the telos is dispensable" to "the telos is *false*, for the attitudes." Ask *why* there is no owning-independent fact for a receptive warrant to track. Because the avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes are not states a subject *finds* in herself but commitments she *sustains*. Whether I believe that *p* is not, like whether I am in pain, settled independently of my owning or disowning it: it is settled in my standing behind *p*, in my treating *p* as true for the purposes of my reasoning and action, in my answerability for it. There is, for such an attitude, no fact about what I believe that is fixed *behind the back of* my owning and that my self-ascription must then be answerable to. The telos's ontological assumption — that the attitude is an antecedent, owning-independent state to be gotten right — is false for precisely the attitudes the debate fights over. And once it is false, the demand for a receptive warrant *of* that state is not merely optional but *inapt*: there is nothing there to receive.

This is the load the view must carry, and I carry it openly: (T) is committed to a *constitutivism* about the avowable attitudes — to their not being fully owning-independent. That is a substantive and contestable claim, and §7 meets its contestant. But notice what the commitment buys. It explains, rather than stipulates, why the scope line falls where it does (§6); it converts the bare epistemological distinction of §4 into an ontological thesis with a locatable opponent; and it answers the SEP's sharpest charge against agentialism — that control-*over* is not control-*by* — not by denying it but by embracing it: for a state one *sustains* rather than *finds*, control-over is the only relation there could be, and the demand for control-by was the telos's error.

## 6. The owning thesis

(T) holds that first-person access to one's avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes is a stance of *owning* the attitude — answerability for it, standing behind it as one's own — which *makes* the corresponding self-ascription true rather than tracking an antecedent fact; the distinctiveness of self-knowledge is in the first instance practical, and the knowledge we have is the authoritative registering of an owning.

**Three marks** distinguish owning from detection, each a datum an objection had turned against the non-receptive accounts.

*(i) The failure-signature is self-deception, not misperception.* When first-person access goes wrong, its shape is the refusal or inability to own what is one's own — the disavowed belief, the unacknowledged motive — not the misreading of an inner item. A relation whose failures are failures of honesty is not a relation of detection.

*(ii) It is groundless.* The subject who avows offers no evidence and passes through no intervening judgment, because owning a commitment is not the conclusion of an inquiry into whether one has it. Groundlessness is not a deficiency in a would-be cognitive achievement; it is the positive mark of a non-receptive relation, as the expressivists saw.

*(iii) It is ineliminably first-personal.* Another can *attribute* an attitude to me on evidence; only I can *own* mine. The asymmetry is of authorship, not of epistemic access.

**The scope line, derived.** §5 lets us *derive* the restriction that earlier drafts stipulated. Sensations are owning-independent states: whether I am in pain is settled behind the back of any owning, there is "no appearance/reality distinction in the case of sensations" (Hill 1991), and one's relation to a present pain is therefore the kind of thing a *receptive* relation (acquaintance) can and should capture. For sensations the telos is *correct*: self-knowledge of pain is knowledge of an antecedent state. The avowable attitudes are different in just the way §5 described — they are sustained, not found — and so they call for owning, not acquaintance. The hybrid (acquaintance for the sensory, owning for the attitudinal) is thus not an ad hoc fence around the convenient cases but a consequence of an ontological difference between sensations and attitudes. The attitudes are the ground the whole dispute is fought on, and it is there that the telos misfires.

**Preservation of the explananda.** The data survive, and are better placed. *Asymmetry* is the authorship asymmetry of (iii). *First-person authority* is the standing of the one who owns: to meet "I believe it's raining" with "no, you don't" is not to correct a measurement but to refuse the subject her authorship. *Moore's paradox* is the incoherence of disavowing what one is in the act of asserting, since to assert *p* is to own the commitment to *p*. *Immediacy* is the absence of a gap: there is nothing to cross because one is not tracking anything.

**Placement.** (T) is what agentialism and expressivism become once the telos is removed. From agentialism it takes owning and removes the burden — imposed by the telos, not the insight — of converting owning into a warrant of an independent state; this dissolves the "epistemic burden" objection (owning need not be control-*by*, because there is no independent state to be controlled by) and O'Brien's "too demanding" (no reflective grasp of one's reasons *as evidence* is needed, since one is not assembling access to a fact). From expressivism it takes groundless avowal and removes the burden of making a groundless expression into access to an inner object; this answers the standing worry and supplies the non-epistemic analysis of first-person privilege that Campbell (2022) leaves open. The nearest kin is Coliva's (2012) constitutivism, on which judging "I believe *p*" *undertakes* the commitment it ascribes; (T) takes her constitutive insight and removes her residual aim of thereby securing a *true self-ascription about an independent attitude* — for there is no such independent attitude; the truth of the self-ascription is conferred by the owning, full stop.

**Recalcitrance.** The objection that detached self-observation does not preclude ownership and can *enlighten* (Reed 2010; Borgoni 2015; Doyle 2019) is granted and located: the cases requiring it are cases one *cannot presently own* — the recalcitrant, self-deceived, akratic — i.e., the breakdown profile predicted by mark (i). Self-observation is the remedial supplement when owning fails; its use there is no evidence that owning is not constitutive in the cases in good order.

## 7. Objections

**Objection 1 — relabeling.** (T)'s content lies near Moran and Bar-On, as granted. But the contribution is the diagnosis (§§3–4) and what it forces (§5): agentialism and expressivism appear in the field as two accounts among the eight, each defended on the telos's terms and each defeated on those terms; (T) is what their insights become once the telos is dropped, and one reaches it only by the diagnosis that the candidates' shared presupposition is the disease. The relabeling charge mistakes the conclusion for the argument.

**Objection 2 — "but we *do* come to know what we believe, and that was the explanandum."** This is the decisive objection, for it charges (T) with explaining the phenomenon away. Regiment it: (O1) self-ascriptions are true and non-accidentally warranted — knowledge; (O2) a theory must explain this; (O3) (T) denies that the fundamental relation is a relation of knowledge; (O4) so (T) denies the datum or fails to explain it. I deny (O4), and the repair of §§4–5 lets me say exactly why without contradiction.

(a) *The datum is kept.* We do come to have true, authoritative second-order beliefs about our attitudes; these are knowledge, and (T) keeps them. (b) *The debt is discharged.* Owning *p* makes "I believe *p*" true — the standing-behind is the truth-maker — and authoritative — warranted by the standing of the one who owns. So the second-order knowledge is real and explained: its truth-maker and its authority are the owning. To know what one believes is to register, in a true and authoritative self-ascription, what one already owns. (c) *The disagreement is located.* (O1)'s phrase "that was the explanandum" packs in the telos's ontology — that the attitude is an antecedent fact one's ascription must answer to. (T) denies this for the attitudes (§5): there is no such fact, so the self-ascription is not *answerable to* one and is not, and need not be, *receptive* knowledge of one. It is knowledge in the only sense the case permits: a true, authoritative, non-accidental self-ascription whose truth the subject confers in owning. (d) *The error cases confirm this.* (T) is not committed to infallibility. Owning makes the self-ascription true *when owning succeeds*; when it fails — self-deception, disavowal, the wishful belief one will not claim — the ascription comes apart from the attitude and is false. These are the actual error cases, and their shape — motivated failure-to-own, not random mis-measurement — is what (T) predicts and the detection model must call coincidence.

This is also where the earlier draft's apparent self-contradiction is dissolved. It seemed that "owning makes the self-ascription true" was itself the *non-receptive warrant* that §4 declared impossible. It is not, because §4 (now correctly stated) does not forbid non-receptive truth-conferral; it denies that the telos's prize — *receptive* knowledge of an *independent* state — can be had non-receptively. Owning is *truth-conferring* and non-receptive, and there is no contradiction in that, because owning makes the ascription true *precisely by not being answerable to a prior fact* — it institutes the truth rather than conforming to it. A relation that confers truth without tracking an independent fact is exactly what a constituted, sustained attitude permits and a found, independent state forbids. §4 and §6 are consistent, and the seam between them is the ontological claim of §5.

**Objection 3 — the constitutivism is false; beliefs are owning-independent dispositional facts (the Humean).** Here is the view's real cost, and the front on which it should be fought rather than the epistemological one. The Humean holds that to believe *p* is to have a dispositional profile — to be disposed to assert *p*, to rely on *p* in reasoning and action, to feel surprise if not-*p* — that obtains whether or not one owns it, so that there *is* an antecedent fact about what one believes, and self-knowledge of it must after all be receptive. I make three replies. First, the view needs only a *weak* constitutivism: not that there is no dispositional profile, but that for the *avowable, reasons-sensitive* attitudes the question *what one believes* is not settled by the profile alone *behind the back of* the subject's owning — the akratic and self-deceived cases, where profile and avowal diverge, are settled not by reading off the disposition but by what the subject can and will own on reflection. Second, the Humean owes an account of first-person authority that the dispositional fact cannot give: if my belief were a disposition I detect, "no, you don't" would be an intelligible correction, and it is not. Third — and this is the dialectical payoff of the repair — the Humean reading *is* the telos's ontology made explicit, so the choice between (T) and Humeanism simply *is* the choice §§3–5 framed; the paper does not beg it but joins it, and joins it where it should be joined, in the metaphysics of the attitudes rather than in the epistemology of access. That the disagreement has been relocated to a real and tractable battleground is itself a result.

**Objection 4 — "owning" is too freighted for the belief that it will rain.** Distinguish the *form* (answerability, standing-behind) from its *intensity*. The form is present in every case, however flat, which is why even the most trivial Moorean conjunction registers as a failure of *owning* rather than of *evidence*. The dramatic cases make the form vivid; they are not the only cases in which it operates.

## 8. Conclusion

The theory of self-knowledge has divided over which relation a subject bears to her own mind and has not converged, because each candidate is asked, by an assumption the candidates share, to be two incompatible things: a warrant of an independent state, which is a relation of answerability to an antecedent fact, and the seat of first-person specialness, which is a relation of authorship that consists in *not* answering to such a fact. The knowledge-telos that imposes this demand is not the truism that self-knowledge is knowledge; it is the substantive claim that one's attitudes are antecedent, owning-independent states to which one's self-ascriptions must conform — and that claim, I have argued, is false for the very attitudes the debate is about. They are not states one finds but commitments one sustains. Abandoning the telos costs nothing we should keep: the knowledge it promised survives as the authoritative registering of an owning, and the features it could not explain — groundlessness, immediacy, non-alienation, a fallibility shaped like self-deception — become what one should expect. What is left is the owning thesis, and a relocated dispute: not "which relation gives us access to the inner fact?" but "are our beliefs and intentions facts we find or commitments we sustain?" To answer the second question is to settle the first, and the wager of this paper is that the first was unanswerable only because the second had been begged. We do come to know our own minds; but the knowing is the registering of an owning already in place, and less like reading a gauge than like standing behind one's word.
