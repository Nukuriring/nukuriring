## 1. THE CENTRAL THESIS (state it precisely)

**Photographs are transparent: we literally see the world through them.** To look at a photograph of X is to *see X itself* — with photographic assistance — in the same generic sense in which we see things through mirrors, telescopes, and microscopes. Paintings are by contrast **opaque**: we do *not* see the depicted object through them, only a *representation* of it.

Critical qualifications Walton insists on:
1. The claim is **literal, not a colorful exaggeration.** "I must warn against watering down this suggestion, against taking it to be a colorful, or exaggerated, or not quite literal way of making a relatively mundane point" [p. 251].
2. It is **not** the claim that the viewer has the *impression* of seeing the object "in the flesh." (He explicitly denies the illusion-of-presence reading.)
3. Transparency is the kind of realism *most distinctive* of photography, but **"Transparency is not the whole story"** of photographic realism [p. 273]. Photography also excels at texture/shadow/perspective and at "revealing reality" (evidential reliability) — but those are *other* faces of realism.
4. Transparency belongs even to "a fuzzy and badly exposed snapshot depicting few details and offering little information" [p. 273]. It is independent of accuracy, detail, and information.

**One-line core:** seeing through photographs is *a new mode of perception*, grounded in a *mechanical (belief-independent) causal connection* between scene and image, which puts the viewer in *perceptual contact* with the world.

## 2. SECTION 1 — The puzzle of photographic realism (the dialectical setup)

**The common-sense datum.** Photographs are widely felt to be *more realistic* than handmade pictures (paintings, drawings, etchings) — Bazin et al. Evidence marshalled by the realist intuition [pp. 246–247]:
- Photographs admitted as court evidence where sketches are not (some courts let reporters *sketch* but not *photograph* proceedings).
- Photographs more useful for **extortion** (an oil painting of Mr X in bed with Mrs Y would cause little consternation).
- Photographic **pornography** "more potent than the painted variety."
- Publishing photographs of disaster victims / private lives provokes **invasion-of-privacy** charges that drawings would not.
- Contrast Goya's *Disasters of War* etchings with Brady-circle Civil War **photographs** (figs. 1–2): hard to resist saying the photographs have an "immediacy or realism which the etchings lack."

**The dissent (Steichen et al.).** Photographs are *unlike* reality; we rarely confuse one with the other; "distortions" of the process; photographer's control → interpretation/falsification; the medium is *expressive*, "inevitably colored by the photographer's personal interests, attitudes, and prejudices."

**Diagnosis:** whether the dissent really collides with the realism claim "depends … on how that claim is to be understood." Much of the literature is **confused** because it conflates distinct theses.

### What is NOT the special thing (Walton clears the ground)
- **Not** continuation of the post-Renaissance quest for realism (perspective, modeling, shading, incidental detail). Photography did master perspective, but that is not what is *distinctive*.
- **Not** illusion / approach to illusion. If realism meant "closer to genuine illusion," **theater** would be *more* realistic than film (real flesh-and-blood persons on stage look more like the people portrayed than plays of light on a flat screen). But Bazin treats **mechanical** origin as crucial, and theater is not mechanical. [Panofsky also contrasts film with theater.]
- Bazin's own view: photographs enjoy special status **simply by being photographs**, by their *mechanical origins*, **regardless of what they look like**: "No matter how fuzzy, distorted, or discolored … it shares, by virtue of the very process of its becoming, the being of the model … it *is* the model" (OPI 15).
- **Complication:** parallel "paradoxical" claims have been made about *handmade* pictures too — Gombrich's child's hobby-horse stick that "*becomes* a horse," Arnheim's wax-museum guards and the wooden robin that "*is* a robin … adds a robin to the inventory of nature." So "the image *is* the object" talk is not unique to photography.

### The one clear difference — and why it's not enough
- "A photograph is always a photograph of **something which actually exists**." Even werewolf/Martian photos are photos of *actual* things (actors, sets, costumes). A painting of Aphrodite done without a model "depicts nothing real" [fn. 8 cites Scruton]. 
- **But this is not the whole story:** the realist intuition holds even when paintings *do* depict actual things and viewers know it. So restrict the comparison to **photographs vs painted portraits of the same real subject** (e.g., photographs of Lincoln vs painted portraits of Lincoln). The claim to be vindicated: photographs of Lincoln are "in some fundamental manner more realistic."

**Walton's promise [p. 251]:** there *is* a fundamental difference; photography *is* special and deserves "supremely realistic"; but the distinctive realism "is not an ordinary one" and "has little to do … with the post-Renaissance quest for realism … or with standard theoretical accounts of realism." Without grasping it "we cannot hope to explain the power and effectiveness of photography."

## 3. SECTION 2 — Photography as an aid to *seeing* (introduce transparency)

The key reframing: think of photography **not just as a picture-making technique but as a contribution to the enterprise of *seeing*.** "The invention of the camera gave us not just a new method of making pictures and not just pictures of a new kind: it gave us a new way of seeing" [p. 251].

- Bazin's comparison of cinema to **mirrors** "points in the right direction."
- **Aids to vision:** mirrors let us see around corners; telescopes/microscopes let us see the too-far / too-small. Photography is an aid too — "an especially versatile one." With a camera we can see around corners, the distant, the small — **and into the past.** "We see long deceased ancestors when we look at dusty snapshots." Viewing Wiseman's *Titicut Follies* (1967) in San Francisco in 1984 *is* to watch events that occurred in 1967 at Bridgewater State Hospital.
- **"Photographs are transparent. We see the world through them."** [p. 251]

**Indirect seeing is still seeing [p. 253].** Walton concedes we may see photographed objects *only indirectly* — but indirectness is common (we see objects via mirror images, via lens-images, via reflected/emitted light; we hear via sounds). He rejects the inference from "indirect" to "not really seeing" — the sense-datum mistake that "all we actually see are sense-data or images or photographs." One can see *through* sense-data / mirror images without noticing them ("in this sense they can be invisible"); even when one attends to the photographic image one may *simultaneously* be seeing and attending to the object photographed.
- **Seeing ≠ learning.** Seeing is often a way of finding out about the world, but sometimes we learn little and value the seeing anyway — paradigmatically, seeing departed loved ones through old photographs (no new information, but "we can see our loved ones again, and that is important to us"). [Foreshadows §8: perceptual contact has less to do with knowledge than supposed.]

## 4. SECTION 3 — Paintings are opaque; the fictional-seeing / real-seeing distinction

**Paintings are NOT transparent.** "We do not see Henry VIII when we look at his portrait; we see only a representation of him. There is a sharp break, a difference of kind, between painting and photography" [p. 253].

**The crucial distinction (used throughout): REAL seeing vs FICTIONAL seeing.**
- It is natural to say a viewer of the portrait "sees" Henry VIII, or that spectators of the Unicorn Tapestries "see unicorns." But there are no unicorns; this "see" is **fictional, not literal.** "Our use of the word 'see,' by itself, proves nothing."
- **Loch Ness example:** a published "monster" photo turned out to be of a *model* (used in a movie). With that information "we change our tune": what we *really* see (through the photo) is the **model**, not the monster. Real seeing, like ordinary seeing, requires that *what exists* be seen.
- **Movie viewers** of the monster film: it is *fictional* that they see the monster (they don't believe it exists); they *actually* see, photographically, the model; and since the film was shot on location, they *really do* see Loch Ness itself.
- **Aunt Mabel example [p. 254]:** "Aunt Mabel is grimacing" (present tense, though she may be long deceased) → it is *fictional* that she is grimacing now and *fictional* that I see her grimacing now; meanwhile I *actually* see, through the photograph, the grimace she made on the past occasion.
- **Default rider:** when, in looking at a picture, it is fictional that one sees something, it is fictional that one sees it **with the naked eye** ("Fictionally one is in the presence of what one sees"), *not* through a photograph/mirror/telescope.
- **Special case — Richard Shirley's film *Resonant* (1969)**, made by filming *still photographs*: sometimes we see the photos' edges (then it's fictional we see the house *through the photographs*); much of it is "fascinatingly ambiguous"; sometimes fictional we see directly, sometimes no fact of the matter. Throughout, the viewer *actually* sees still photographs (via the film) and *actually* sees the woman/house *through* the photographs.

**MASTER DIAGNOSIS [p. 254]:** the confusion infecting writing on photography/film is the **failure to distinguish (a) the special kind of seeing that *actually* occurs (really seeing X through a photograph) from (b) the ordinary kind of seeing that only *fictionally* takes place (fictionally seeing X directly).** "A vague awareness of both, stirred together in a witches' cauldron, could conceivably tempt one toward the absurdity that the viewer is really in the presence of the object."

## 5. SECTIONS 4–7 — Defending transparency against objections (each objection → reply)

### OBJ-1: "A photograph doesn't look like / isn't experienced like the actual scene." [p. 255]
**Reply:** Seeing directly and seeing with photographic assistance are *different modes of perception*; no reason to expect the experiences to be similar (cf. seeing through a microscope / distorting mirror / underwater / odd lighting — still seeing). Snyder & Allen's objections to "a photograph shows us what we would have seen if we had been there" are "well taken but beside the point": the relevant fiction may be not that viewers are *shown* what they'd have seen, but that they are *actually there and see for themselves* — distinct from the *fact* that they really do see via the photograph. **If realism were about how photographs *look*, there'd be no essential difference from painting,** since paintings can be photo-indistinguishable.

**Chuck Close *Self-Portrait* — "the jolt" [p. 255]:** we take it for a photograph, then learn it is a painting. The discovery *jolts* us — a transformation "much deeper and more significant" than learning an etching is really a pen-and-ink drawing; "more like discovering a guard in a wax museum to be just another wax figure." We feel "less 'in contact with' Close." If it is a nude, embarrassment may be *relieved* by learning it's paint not film. **The theory explains the jolt:** first we think we (really) see the person; then we realize it's only *fictional* that we see him. The look may persist (it may still *seem* we're really seeing him with photographic assistance) → residual embarrassment.
- **Confession [fn. 29]:** the example is "not as clear-cut as I implied" — Close often projected a photograph onto canvas and painted over it; if so, *Self-Portrait*'s opacity is "questionable." Point unaffected: had it been painted "in the usual manner" it would *definitely* be opaque, and the jolt would occur for the viewer who comes to believe it was so painted.

**Genuine illusions catalogued [pp. 256–258]:**
- *Self-Portrait* = illusion that we are seeing someone *via photography* (we are fooled at first). NOT the illusion of seeing a person directly standing before us.
- **John de Andrea, *Man with Arms around Woman* (sculpture), photographed [fig. 4]:** we *do* see through the photo, but what we see "is not what it seems to be" — a life-sized sculpture, not people.
- **Movie killing:** detective shoots two thugs; viewer *does* see (via film) an actor draw a gun and pull the trigger, but does *not* see a killing — "the bullets were blanks, and the blood, ketchup." The scene's flatness, fuzzy edges, lack of color don't stop it seeming an actual killing filmed.
- **Douglas Bond, *Ace I* (superrealist painting) [fig. 5]:** obviously *not* a photograph → its photographic character is "more **pretense** than **illusion**." It doesn't *seem* we see through it, but it may be *fictional* that we do (fictional that *Ace I* is a photo through which one sees men before Pasadena City Hall).
- **Taxonomy to keep straight:** ILLUSION (it really seems to us we see through a photo, and we're fooled) vs PRETENSE/FICTION (it's fictional, make-believe, that we see through it) vs really-seeing-through (literal transparency).

### OBJ-2: Accuracy — "photographs falsify colors, distort space (running horse as blur or frozen), can be retouched." [p. 258]
**Reply: accuracy and transparency are INDEPENDENT.**
- We can be deceived seeing *directly*; "If cameras can lie, so can our eyes." Seeing through a distorting mirror / fog / tinted windshield / out-of-focus microscope is still *seeing*. Distortions are no reason to deny we see through photos.
- **Converse case proving independence:** a *theatrical reenactment* of a crime can be perfectly *accurate* (jurors gain only correct information) yet jurors "certainly do not *see* the incident via the portrayal." → accuracy without transparency.
- **Reliability ≠ transparency:** is photography just a "more reliable mechanism"? **Flexible-mirror thought experiment [p. 259]:** imagine mirrors whose shapes change constantly, drastically, unpredictably. People still *see through* them despite the unreliability. Perhaps such a mirror is "not a knowledge-producing mechanism" (true beliefs formed via it may not be *knowledge*) — "But this does not mean that he does not see the reflected things." → **Seeing does not require a knowledge-producing/reliable mechanism.**

### OBJ-3: The "mechanical / automatic / handmade" issue — and Walton's POSITIVE causal account [pp. 261–262]
Some say photos owe their status to "mechanical," "automatic" origins vs "handmade" paintings (Fox Talbot: Lacock Abbey "drew its own picture"; Bazin: "man plays no part"; Arnheim: objects "print their image") [fn. 18].

**Walton AGREES that origin (how it comes about), not look, is the source.** He gives a **causal theory of perception:** *to see something is to have visual experiences caused, in a certain manner, by what is seen.* Lincoln caused both his photograph (and viewers' experiences) *and* his portrait — so causation alone doesn't distinguish. **The difference is in the MANNER of causation:**

> "part of what it is to see something is to have visual experiences which are caused by it **in a purely mechanical manner**. Objects cause their photographs and the visual experiences of viewers mechanically; so we see the objects through the photographs. By contrast, objects cause paintings not mechanically but in a more 'human' way, a way involving the artist; so we don't see through paintings." [p. 261]

### OBJ-4: "Photographs are crafted, not natural; they carry the photographer's interpretation / subjective point of view." (Snyder & Allen "PVR" 151; H. Gene Blocker [fn. 19])
**Reply [pp. 261–262]:** A causal role for *human beings* does NOT rule out seeing. People constantly affect what/how I see — turning lights on/off, blowing smoke in my eyes, making eyeglasses/mirrors/telescopes, *pointing things out*. "Why not say that photographers … show me things and also enable me to see them? Surely that does not mean that I don't really see them."
- I can get a sense of someone else's *interpretation* of what I see and still see it myself (seeing "through their eyes"). Expressiveness does **not** make photographs **opaque.** Kertész's *Distortion #157* is expressive [fig. 6]. **Riefenstahl, *Triumph of the Will*:** she "interprets" / construes the 1934 Nazi Party Congress, yet "we ourselves *do* see Hitler's airplane descending through the clouds, the … marching troops … even if the film fosters misconceptions" (e.g., that the crowds were more enthusiastic than they were). "We can be aware, even vividly aware, of both the medium and the maker without either blocking our view of the object."

### OBJ-5: "Photography uses conventions (perspective system) — a 'language' to be read." [p. 262]
**Reply:** Conventions don't block seeing. Compare a *convention* that certain mirrors be warped (convex mirrors for seeing around corners). The convention must be understood/internalized to "read" the images properly — "Nevertheless, one sees things through the mirrors."

## 6. THE POSITIVE ACCOUNT — counterfactual dependence + BELIEF-INDEPENDENCE (the crux) [pp. 262–266]

### The dinosaur thought experiment
An explorer returns from the jungle with either (A) **photographic** dinosaur-pictures or (B) **dinosaur-sketches** "drawn from life." Both can convince us a dinosaur exists; photos needn't even be *more* convincing (we might trust the drawings more). **The crucial difference:** with the *sketches* we **rely on the picture-maker's belief** in a way we do not with the *photographs*.
- **Sketches:** indicate what was there *by* indicating what the *artist thought* was there. We reason: he set out to draw what he saw → he's a competent, reliable observer → the dinosaur he thought he saw was really there. **Information is secondhand**; we trust his judgment.
- **Photographs:** we need *not* rely on his judgment the same way. Even if he never saw the dinosaur (left the camera on a tripod with an automatic trigger), we may still infer its existence. "In fact, if the photographs do convince us that he believed in the dinosaur, they do so because they convince us that there was a dinosaur, **not the other way around**."
- We *do* make assumptions to trust photos (camera type, no darkroom "monkey business") and may have to trust the photographer's say-so — **but that kind of reliance is compatible with seeing.** Analogy: to trust my own senses I must assume no hallucinogens, no "evil neurosurgeon" feeding misinformation; I might rely on a "beneficent doctor" who assures me the system works — "then I am seeing … notwithstanding my reliance on him."

### Why the counterfactuals hold differently (the heart of it) [p. 264]
Both sets of pictures **counterfactually depend** on the scene: had there been no dinosaur, both pictures (and our experiences of them) would differ. *But why?*
- **Sketches:** a different scene would have changed the sketch *only because* it would have changed the **artist's beliefs** (hence how/whether he sketched).
- **Photographs:** a different scene would have changed the photograph **even if the photographer's beliefs were held fixed.**

**The hallucination test (decisive):** Suppose the picture-maker is **hallucinating** the dinosaur.
- The **artist's** sketches will show a dinosaur anyway (the sketch tracks what he *thinks* he sees; the actual scene is irrelevant).
- The **camera bypasses the photographer's hallucination and captures what is in the jungle** (no dinosaur → no dinosaur in the photo).
> "The artist draws his hallucination; the camera bypasses the photographer's hallucination and captures what is in the jungle." [p. 264]

**Belief-relayed dependence can be hidden:** if a *painting I mistake for a photograph* convinces me, I wrongly think my belief is independent of the maker's and that I see the dinosaur. Unbeknownst to me my belief is (relevantly) dependent on the artist's belief → "I am wrong in thinking I see the dinosaur." (Dependence is about *how the picture* is based on the maker's beliefs, not about the viewer's conscious grounds.)

### THE OFFICIAL FORMULATION [pp. 264–265]
> "**Photographs are counterfactually dependent on the photographed scene even if the beliefs (and other intentional attitudes) of the photographer are held fixed. Paintings which have a counterfactual dependence on the scene portrayed lose it when the beliefs (and other intentional attitudes) of the painter are held fixed.**"

To **see through** a picture, the viewer's visual experiences must depend on the scene in the *belief-independent* (photographic) manner, **not** in the belief-mediated (painting) manner. (Walton **leaves open** whether the viewer must also have *beliefs* about the scene and what links experience to belief — perception need not be belief-laden; cites Dretske, *Seeing and Knowing* [fn. 21].)
- **fn. 22:** in some cases the distinguishing conditional dependence may be on the maker's **visual experience, or thoughts, or intentions**, rather than strictly *beliefs*.
- **fn. 23:** in special cases a photograph may be *causally* but not *counterfactually* dependent on the scene (then you can't *learn* about the scene from it) "**But one still sees the scene through the photograph.** Perception is to be understood in terms of causation rather than counterfactuals, if the former doesn't reduce to the latter." → **The deepest commitment is CAUSAL (mechanical), with counterfactual dependence as the usual diagnostic, not the essence.**

### Science-fiction confirmation: Helen vs the transplant patient [p. 265] (adapted from David Lewis [fn. 24])
- **Helen / the conscientious neurosurgeon:** her eyes are disconnected; the doctor stimulates her optic nerves "in ways corresponding to what *he* sees," feeding correct info she has every reason to trust. Her experiences are caused by the things she seems to see — **yet she does not really see them; "the doctor is seeing for her,"** because her experiences are based on *his beliefs* (differences in scene make for differences in *his beliefs* → differences in her experiences). [= the painting structure.]
- **Contrast — double eye transplant / prosthetic eyes:** this patient **does see.** He relies on the surgeon/donor/manufacturer's *work*, but **not on anyone's *beliefs* about the things seen.** [= the photograph structure.] Cataract patients owe their experiences to others; we all owe ours to those who refrained from destroying our visual organs — "Obviously these facts do not blind us."

### Grice: natural vs nonnatural meaning [pp. 265–267] (Grice, "Meaning," 1957 [fn. 25])
- **Natural meaning (mean_N):** "Spots mean_N (mean naturally) measles"; black clouds mean_N rain *only if* rain actually follows. **mean_N p entails p.**
- **Nonnatural meaning (mean_NN):** the bus bell's three rings mean_NN the bus is full. Tied to *someone's meaning p by it*; **mean_NN p does NOT entail p**; symbols are *intermediaries* whose message we must decode and *judge whether true*.
- **Photographs are bearers of NATURAL meaning** → "photographs are **necessarily perfectly accurate**." If there was no dinosaur, the photo does *not* mean_N there was one, "no matter what it looks like." Knowing enough about the camera/processing, one could infer with "perfect accuracy" about the objects. (One could also infer from a drawing if one knew enough about the *artist* — but the *drawing* does not *mean_N* there was a dinosaur even if there was; "The sketch is not necessarily accurate in this way.")
- **Essential accuracy ≠ never misleading.** **Twiggy example:** a portrait made with a *bowed mirror* makes her look to have a huge paunch. Viewers misled? Their mistake is about what the picture **means_NN** (or what it makes *fictional*, or how she *appears* through it). It still **means_N that she is skinny** (someone who knew about the mirror could ascertain this). To think of photos as necessarily accurate is to treat them as "**especially close to the facts**," not as intermediaries with their own meanings we must decide whether to trust: "**To interpret a photograph properly is to get the facts**" [p. 266].
- Snyder & Allen: how a photo is made "has little to do with how we normally interpret it" (PVR 159). Walton: "some truth in this" (we may take it to mean_NN Twiggy is fat, she may *appear* fat through it, it may make it *fictional* that she is fat) — but none of that forces denying it means_N she is skinny. **Realizing photographs are necessarily accurate "profoundly affects our experience of them," even when we're unsure what they mean_N.**
- **Drawings/paintings are sometimes nonnatural symbols — but not always.** Brueghel's *Children's Games*: he probably did not intend us to learn the games *by recognizing his intention* (so not strictly Gricean nonnatural meaning), yet the beliefs it induces are *based on the painter's beliefs* — enough like nonnatural meaning to differ from photographs.

## 7. DEGREES & MIXED TRANSPARENCY — the distinction cross-cuts photo/non-photo [pp. 267–269]

Transparent/opaque does **not** line up neatly with photograph/non-photograph. There are **degrees** of transparency; a picture can be transparent in some respects, opaque in others; sometimes "no determinate answer."

**Handmade-but-mechanical (possibly TRANSPARENT) pictures [p. 267]:**
- Tracing outlines on transparent paper taped to a window.
- Copying a photograph (perhaps without recognizing its subject); painting over a photograph matching each spot's brightness [fn. 27: 19th-c. practice of painting portraits over photographs].
- Filling a grid with grays from a **directional light-meter** (or estimating brightness by eye).
- **Automatic doodles** done while the mind is elsewhere [fn. 28: examples owed to Robert Howell, George Wilson].
- → "Some such mechanically executed drawings are probably **transparent**."

**Photographs that may be OPAQUE [pp. 267–269]:**
- **Darkroom constructions** (combining negatives, retouching, burning out images, manipulating exposure/contrast, filters) — Uelsmann, *Symbolic Mutation* [fig. 7]. Some say these are essentially paintings (Scruton: the manipulator "has now become a painter" [fn. 30]).
- **Extreme limiting case:** a "photograph" made by exposing paper **dot by dot with a flashlight** to render Lincoln pointillistically = "drawing with a flashlight" → **opaque** (you don't see Lincoln through it).
- **But most constructions are transparent in PARTS / respects:** we see a person through a print however lightly/darkly printed (even if brightness is "falsified"), though we may not see the *state of affairs* of his being illuminated thus. **Combined-negative case:** a photo "showing" Deng Xiaoping conversing with Yasir Arafat, made from two separate negatives → viewer does **not** see the *event of their conversing*, but **does** see Deng and **does** see Arafat.
- Not knowing how a photo was made → not knowing *what* you're seeing through it and what you're not, though you'll likely realize you're seeing *some* of it — "not unlike … seeing a white shape and wondering whether one is seeing a ghost."

**Phenomenology / "slickness" [p. 269]:**
- A construction may *seem* fully transparent even where it isn't; many constructions "appear to be transparent even in respects in which they are not," giving a realism nonphotographic pictures lack.
- Uelsmann's *Symbolic Mutation*: viewer "hardly has the impression of seeing a hand fused with a face" — too obviously two negatives.
- "Slick" photos read as likely manipulated → weakened impression of seeing-through.
- **Why some filmmakers mimic crude home movies** (hand-held, deliberately bad focus — e.g., John Cassavetes' *Shadows*, 1960): they sacrifice the (never-live) illusion of being *face-to-face* with characters **in favor of a more convincing illusion of seeing the characters *through the photographs*.** Reconciles claimed *immediacy* with obvious *contrivance*: emphasizing the medium usually *distances* us, but here it has "**just the opposite effect**." **Paradigms of transparency = casual snapshots and home movies, not professional constructions.**

## 8. SECTION 8 — The deeper theory of perception: CONTACT + structural correspondence [pp. 269–272]

**Emerging conception:** "**to perceive things is to be in contact with them in a certain way.**" A **mechanical** connection (photography) counts as contact; a **humanly mediated** one (painting) does not. And: "Perceptual contact with things has rather **less to do with acquiring knowledge** about them than has sometimes been supposed."

**Belief-independence is NECESSARY but NOT SUFFICIENT — the description-machine objection [p. 270]:** Imagine a machine sensitive to light from a scene that mechanically prints **accurate verbal descriptions** (not pictures). Its printouts are made "just as mechanically as any photographs" — yet they are **not transparent**; reading them, you do not *see* the scene. So mechanical/belief-independent production cannot be the whole story.

**Why not? First (insufficient) move — "seeing-as":** maybe we don't see through descriptions because we don't / can't see them *as* the scene. (One doesn't see Eisenhower through a photo unless one "notices" him in some sense — though one needn't recognize him *as* Eisenhower or even as a person.) But this needs an account of "seeing-as" and of why that matters; and we can't simply decree "only pictures can be transparent" without saying *why* nonpictures can't.

**The real answer — STRUCTURAL CORRESPONDENCE between the perceptual process and the world [pp. 270–272]:**
Investigating things via *pictures* (photos OR drawings) is "strikingly analogous" to investigating them *directly* and **disanalogous** to investigating via *descriptions* — especially regarding **what mistakes one is prone to**:
- **Via descriptions** the confusions track *words/symbols*: "3"/"8" → 85-ft tree misread as 35-ft (but not as 85.00001); house/horse/hearse, cat/cot, madam/madman, intellectuality/ineffectuality.
- **Directly or via pictures** the confusions track *real similarity*: an 85-ft tree confused with an 84-ish/85.00001-ft tree, houses with barns/woodsheds, cats with puppies, near shades of red with each other.

**The deep principle [p. 271]:** "the **structure of the enterprise of perceiving bears important analogies to the structure of reality.** In this sense we perceive the world as it is." NOT that percepts *resemble* objects or that things have the properties we perceive — rather that **the mistakes a perceiver is prone to correspond to genuine similarities among things.** Easily confusable = really similar; *degree of similarity explains likelihood of confusion.* "A process of discrimination counts as **perceptual only if its structure is thus analogous to the structure of the world**." This intimacy "goes a long way toward explaining our feeling of closeness to things which we see through photographs."
- **Descriptions "scramble the real similarity relations"** (house/hearse confusion is about *words*, not houses) → words "get between us and what we are reading about," **blocking our view** as photographs and sense-data do not. So description-investigation "does not qualify as perception."

**Caveats Walton honestly raises [pp. 271–272]:**
- Science may show "confusable" things aren't really similar (perceived color ≠ wavelength; equally-cold-feeling environments differ in temperature/humidity/wind). Response: either accept imperfect correlation, or recognize *response-dependent* properties (visible colors, perceived cold) for which correlations hold. We still *think of* confusable things as similar.
- If similarity is only relative to a **"conceptual scheme,"** that still suffices: we don't think houses especially like horses/hearses, so description-investigation isn't perceptual.
- **Reversal / "we can have it both ways" [fn. 31]:** sometimes we count things as similar *precisely because* they're easily confused (pink paint chips) → discriminative capacities *create* (scheme-relative) similarities, which is the only kind there is on this view.
- **Startling upshot:** description-generating devices **could become transparent** if we used them regularly — we might develop properties like "apparent-via-description-generating-devices houseness," making difficulty-of-discrimination track our (revised) similarities; then "we might **really be seeing through** them." So the picture/description line is not metaphysically rigid — but **as things stand**, fundamental differences "plausibly allow mechanically generated pictures — photographs — to be transparent" while descriptions are not. **"This challenge to the transparency of photographs is defused."**

## 9. SECTION 9 — Conclusion: perceptual contact; what photographic realism is [pp. 272–273]

**Perceptual contact contrasted with TWO non-perceptual accesses [p. 273]:**
1. access mediated by **intervening descriptions** (verbal form), and
2. access **via another person** (a teller).
The everyday contrast "seeing vs being told" **conflates** these. Being *told* a scene = **doubly removed** (the person + the verbal form). Perceptual contact *can* be mediated (mirrors, TV circuits, photographs) — "**this mediation is a means of maintaining contact.** Viewers of photographs are in perceptual contact with the world."

**What is photographic realism? Transparency is not the whole story.** Realism "wears more than one face":
- Photography's skill at texture/shadow/reflection, capturing "the jumbled trivia of ordinary life," perspective.
- Its capacity to **"reveal reality"** — evidential reliability (court, extortion). **But this reliability is NOT an automatic consequence of "mechanicalness"; it derives from our equipment/procedures being *standardized* in certain respects** (not all respects — so we must be selective; e.g., we can say little about absolute illumination because shutter/film speeds and apertures vary).
- **The most distinctively photographic thing = transparency:** its "remarkable ability to put us in perceptual contact with the world, an ability which can be claimed even by a fuzzy and badly exposed snapshot." **"It is this — photography's transparency — which is most distinctively photographic and which constitutes the most important justification for speaking of 'photographic realism.'"**

## 10. ARGUMENT MAP / LOGICAL SKELETON (for quick recall)

1. **Datum:** photographs feel more realistic than handmade pictures.
2. **Clear ground:** it's not look-alikeness, not illusion-of-presence, not the post-Renaissance realism program, not mere accuracy/reliability (each independently dismissed).
3. **Reframe:** photography is an **aid to seeing** → THESIS: *we literally see through photographs* (transparency); paintings are *opaque*.
4. **Tool:** distinguish **really seeing** (through the photo; only the existent can be seen) from **fictionally seeing** (directly, with the naked eye). Most "seeing" talk about pictures is fictional.
5. **Objections rebutted:** looks-unlike (different mode of perception); accuracy/distortion (we're deceived directly too; accuracy ⟂ transparency; reliability not required — flexible mirrors); crafted/subjective/expressive (human causal role compatible with seeing — Riefenstahl); conventions (warped-mirror convention).
6. **Mechanism:** causal theory of perception + the difference is the **manner** of causation: photographs are caused **mechanically / belief-independently**; paintings via the **artist's mind**.
7. **Precise criterion:** **counterfactual dependence on the scene that survives holding the maker's beliefs/attitudes fixed** (hallucination test; dinosaur). Deeper essence is **causal** (fn. 23); counterfactual dependence is the usual diagnostic.
8. **Supports:** Helen (no real seeing — doctor's beliefs mediate) vs prosthetic eyes (real seeing — no belief-mediation); Grice **natural meaning** (mean_N p entails p) → photographs *necessarily accurate* though misleadable (Twiggy/bowed mirror).
9. **Refinement:** transparency comes in **degrees / parts**; cross-cuts the photo/painting line (tracings, light-meter grids transparent; flashlight-pointillism, full constructions opaque/mixed).
10. **Necessary-not-sufficient:** mechanical production isn't enough (description machine) → add **structural correspondence**: a process is perceptual only if its *confusion structure mirrors real similarity*; descriptions scramble it.
11. **Verdict:** perception = **being in (possibly mediated) contact** with the world; photographs maintain that contact. **Transparency is the core, though not the whole, of photographic realism.**

## 12. GLOSSARY of Walton's technical vocabulary

- **Transparent / transparency:** property of a picture such that one literally *sees the depicted object through it.* Photographs paradigmatically; some handmade-mechanical pictures too.
- **Opaque:** picture through which one sees only a *representation*, not the object (standard paintings).
- **Really seeing vs fictionally seeing:** literal perception (only the existent can be seen) vs make-believe seeing (typically: fictionally seeing the object *directly, with the naked eye*).
- **Illusion vs pretense:** being genuinely (if briefly) fooled into taking oneself to see through a picture, vs its being merely *fictional* (make-believe) that one does.
- **Mechanical / "purely mechanical manner" of causation:** belief-independent causal route from scene to image (and to the viewer's experience). The mark of transparency.
- **Counterfactual dependence (belief-fixed):** the image would have differed had the scene differed, *even holding the maker's beliefs/intentions fixed.* Diagnostic of transparency (essence is causal — fn. 23).
- **mean_N / mean_NN (Grice):** natural meaning (factive: mean_N p ⊢ p) vs nonnatural meaning (intentional, non-factive, intermediary). Photographs = natural-meaning bearers → "necessarily accurate."
- **Perceptual contact:** the relation constitutive of perception; can be *mediated* (mirror/TV/photo) without being broken. Opposed to access *via description* and access *via another person*.
- **Structural correspondence / similarity–confusability:** a discriminative process is perceptual only if its pattern of likely confusions mirrors real (or scheme-relative) similarities among things.
