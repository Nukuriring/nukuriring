# Owning Up: Against the Knowledge-Telos in the Theory of Self-Knowledge

*Draft v3 — working paper. A self-standing version of the argument that does not rely on the cross-domain detours (Walton's photographic transparency, Cavell's acknowledgment) developed in the companion paper; here the case is made from inside the self-knowledge literature.[^companion] Citations follow the project knowledge bases and should be re-verified before submission.*

## Abstract

Theories of how a subject knows her own beliefs and intentions disagree about almost everything except one assumption: that what they are theorizing is a kind of *knowledge*, and that the task is to identify the relation that warrants it and confers its special standing. I call this assumption the *knowledge-telos*. I argue that the telos is not an innocent description of the explanandum but a substantive presupposition, and that it is the common source of the field's characteristic problems. The argument is diagnostic: the standing objections to the leading accounts — that observational accounts collapse the self/other asymmetry, that the empiricist's reliable transition "won't seem reasonable to the thinker," that the rationalist over-intellectualizes, that the agentialist is "too demanding" and not a genuine epistemic competitor, that the expressivist cannot make a groundless avowal into a cognitive achievement — are not independent failures but recurrent symptoms of a single structural demand the telos imposes: that *one* relation be at once an adequate *warrant* (a receptive, knowledge-conferring relation) and the seat of first-person *specialness* (a non-receptive, agential relation). These pull apart. I argue that we therefore have good reason to abandon the telos, that abandoning it costs us nothing we should want to keep — the knowledge it promised is preserved as a downstream product — and that the positive account left standing is the *owning thesis*: first-person access to one's avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes is constituted by a stance of answerability, not by any relation of detection, and its distinctiveness is practical rather than epistemic.

## 1. Introduction

A subject's relation to her own beliefs, desires, and intentions seems unlike her relation to anyone else's, and unlike her relation to the weather. Asked whether she believes it will rain, she does not gather evidence about herself; she can simply *say*, with an authority others extend to her as a matter of course, and without the felt possibility of looking and finding herself mistaken in the way she might look and find the barometer misread. To explain this — the immediacy, the authority, the asymmetry between the first and the third person — is the business of the theory of self-knowledge.

The field is a catalogue of candidate relations: inner observation, self-interpretation, a reliable inference from the world, a rational transition from one's own judgment, an exercise of agency over one's attitudes, an expression of them. Each account is, at bottom, a proposal about *which relation* a subject bears to her attitude when she knows it. And each faces a well-known objection that has kept the debate alive for decades without convergence.

My thesis is that this lack of convergence is not accidental, and that its cause is a presupposition the parties share rather than any defect in one party's relation. The presupposition — I call it the *knowledge-telos* — is that the explanandum is a relation of *knowledge*: that a successful account must vindicate the subject's self-ascriptions as true, warranted, and epistemically superior, and so must identify the *warrant* that secures this standing. I shall argue (i) that the telos is genuinely shared (§3); (ii) that it is what *generates* the field's recurring problems, because it demands of one relation two things that pull apart (§4); (iii) that we therefore have good reason to abandon it, and that abandoning it preserves rather than forfeits the data (§5); and (iv) that the account left standing is the *owning thesis*:

> (T) For a subject's avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes, first-person access is constituted by a first-personal stance of *owning* the attitude — of answerability for it — and not by any relation of knowledge, detection, or epistemic access; the distinctiveness of self-knowledge is in the first instance *practical*, and its epistemic features are downstream (§6).

I develop (T) in §6 and defend it in §7, where I meet head-on the objection that it changes the subject — that "we *do* come to know what we believe, and that was what wanted explaining."

I should say at the outset what I take the contribution to be. The materials of (T) — answerability, commitment, avowal — are present in the agentialist and expressivist literatures, and I claim no priority for the bare idea that self-ascription is more like owning than like detecting. The contribution is the *diagnosis*: the demonstration that the field's problems are one problem, that the problem is the knowledge-telos, and that the agentialist and expressivist insights fail to land only because they are still made to serve the telos they should be used to overthrow. (T) is what those insights become once the telos is dropped.

## 2. The accounts and their objections

It will help to lay the accounts out grouped not by their usual labels but by the *kind* of relation each offers, since the grouping is what the diagnosis will exploit. I restrict attention throughout to the avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes — paradigmatically belief and intention — and set aside sensations, to which I return under the heading of scope (§6). For each group I state the relation and the objection it characteristically meets.[^kb]

**(a) Receptive relations: observation and inference.** The oldest picture treats self-knowledge as *inner observation* — a quasi-perceptual "inner sense" that monitors one's states (Armstrong 1968; Lycan 1996) — and its modern successor treats it as *self-interpretation*, an inference to the best explanation of one's behavior and inner speech (Ryle 1949; Carruthers 2011; Cassam 2014). What unites them is that the subject is credited with self-knowledge by being suitably *informed about* a state that is there independently of her registering it. The standing objection is that this *abolishes the very distinctiveness it was meant to explain*. If knowing my mind is observing or inferring, it is the same kind of thing as knowing yours, and the asymmetry is "at most a matter of degree" (Ryle 1949). Boghossian (1989) sharpens the point into a trilemma: since attitudes are individuated partly by their relations to other states and the world, no act of inner observation could reveal those relational features, inference would not be *privileged*, and access "on the basis of nothing" would not be access at all. And Moran (2001) presses the complaint that to know one's own attitude by observing oneself is to be *alienated* from it, related to it as a spectator rather than as its subject.

**(b) A reliable transition: empiricist transparency.** Evans (1982) observed that we answer "do I believe *p*?" by considering *p* itself. Byrne (2005, 2018) regiments this as inference on a schema — from *p*, conclude "I believe that *p*" — and credits the resulting self-ascriptions with knowledge on the ground that the schema is *self-verifying* and *safe*: one could not easily follow it and be wrong. The objections cluster. Boyle (2011) argues that even if the transition is reliable, it "will not seem reasonable to the thinker": *p* does not, in general, support "I believe *p*," so the subject who reasons this way cannot see her conclusion as following, and a warrant she cannot occupy is not yet *her* knowledge. Shah and Velleman (2005) object that considering *p* may *produce* a belief rather than *reveal* a standing one, so the method cannot distinguish a newly formed judgment from the prior disposition it was supposed to detect. Valaris (2011) and Barnett (2016) add that the "inference" cannot be used in hypothetical reasoning and violates ordinary restrictions on inference. The shape of the trouble is constant: the relation is epistemically creditable but *first-personally opaque* — it works, but not in a way the subject can own as her reasoning.

**(c) The attempted middle: reasons and rationality.** A third family tries to locate a warrant that is neither mere observation nor a brute reliable rule, but *rational*. On the reasons account (Peacocke 1999), a conscious *judgment* that *p* gives one a reason to self-ascribe the belief that *p*, via the conceptual link between judging and believing. On rationalist accounts (Shoemaker 1996; Stoljar 2018; Smithies 2019), our standing as rational thinkers *constitutively* guarantees that we are not "self-blind." These accounts feel the pull of both poles and try to hold them together; the objections show the strain. Coliva (2008) objects that a conscious thought's phenomenology does not reveal *whether it is a judgment or a wish*, so the judgment is not suitably *accessible* to rationalize the self-ascription — to do the epistemic work, the rational base must be made knowable, which reintroduces exactly the receptive demand the account hoped to avoid. And the rationalist's constitutive guarantee is caught in a dilemma (Kornblith 2012; Cassam 2014): a conception of rationality demanding enough to exclude self-blindness does not apply to flawed creatures like us, while a conception weak enough to apply to us does not deliver the guarantee.

**(d) Non-receptive relations I: agency.** Agentialism locates the distinctive thing not in any epistemic relation but in our *agency over* our attitudes: the privilege of the first person is "more like the knowledge of a person driving a car as opposed to … her passenger" (McGeer 1996), grounded in "asymmetries of responsibility and commitment" (Moran 2001). This captures the immediacy and the non-alienation the receptive accounts lose. But it faces a battery of objections that all turn on the same hinge — that having located the special relation *off* the epistemic axis, the agentialist must somehow get back *onto* it, because the explanandum was supposed to be *knowledge*. So: O'Brien (2003) objects that Moran is "too demanding," requiring the subject to recognize her first-order reasons *as evidence that she has the belief* — a reflective, quasi-evidential grasp ordinary self-ascription lacks. The scope objection notes that agency covers only reasons-sensitive attitudes, leaving the recalcitrant, akratic, and wishful to be known, embarrassingly, by self-observation after all. And the deepest objection, which the *Stanford Encyclopedia* article presses in its own voice, is that agentialism is *not a genuine competitor* in the theory of self-*knowledge* at all: most knowledge consists in the believed state's being *controlled by* what is known, whereas agency is *control over* what is known, so "agentialism is not a genuine competitor unless it explains how agency provides self-knowledge" (Gertler 2024, §3.7).

**(e) Non-receptive relations II: expression.** Expressivism holds that an avowal — "I believe it's raining," "I'm in pain" — is less a report grounded on evidence than a direct *expression* of the state it ascribes, "groundless" in that it rests on no evidence and passes through no intervening judgment (Bar-On 2004; Finkelstein 2003; Campbell 2022). This, too, captures the immediacy and authority. And it, too, meets an objection generated by the demand that the result be *knowledge*: a spontaneous, evidence-free expression looks like the wrong kind of thing to be a *cognitive achievement*, so either avowals are not knowledge (the rejected "simple" expressivism) or expressivism owes an epistemology it has not supplied. Campbell (2022) feels this precisely; she labels her view "expression *without* expressivism" exactly to keep the knowledge, and concedes that to *explain* rather than presuppose first-person privilege her central notion would have to be given a non-epistemic analysis she does not provide.

Five families, five objections. The usual moral is that each account has a problem of its own and the field must keep searching. I shall argue the opposite: the five problems are one problem, seen five times.

## 3. The common presupposition

What the accounts share is easier to see once they are grouped as above. Each picks a relation and asks it to do two jobs at once: to be the *warrant* by which the subject's self-ascription counts as *knowledge*, and to be the seat of the *specialness* — the asymmetry, immediacy, and authority — that makes self-knowledge a distinctive topic in the first place. The first job is set by the telos.

**Definition.** The *knowledge-telos* is the conjunction: (a) the explanandum of a theory of self-knowledge is a relation of *knowledge* — true, warranted, second-order representation of one's own states; and (b) the theory's task is to identify the relation that *warrants* this knowledge and accounts for its superior standing. On the telos, "but then it is not knowledge" is decisive against a proposal; the surrender of the knowledge-claim is a defeat.

That the telos is shared is visible in three ways. First, in the structure of the accounts: every one of them, including the agential and expressive, is offered as an account of self-*knowledge* and is defended by exhibiting the *warrant* its relation supplies — reliability for the empiricist, a conceptual link for the reasons theorist, constitution for the rationalist, entitlement for Moran, a "presumption of truth" for the expressivist. Second, in the treatment of the one position that drops the telos: "simple" expressivism, on which avowals are neither true nor false and express no knowledge, is uniformly treated not as a live option but as a *reductio* — the thing any acceptable expressivism must avoid. Third, in the field's own meta-descriptions. Defending the empiricist side, Gertler characterizes the dispute between empiricism and agentialism as one "about the epistemic *basis* for self-knowledge, not about … the method" (Gertler 2018). That is the telos stated from within: both camps agree there is a *knowledge-output*; they dispute only its *warrant*.

The telos presents itself as a neutral statement of the explanandum — "self-knowledge is, after all, *knowledge*." It is not neutral. It is the substantive claim that the distinctive first-person relation *is itself* a relation of knowledge, rather than, say, a relation from which knowledge can be *had*. Distinguishing those is the whole of what follows.

## 4. The presupposition generates the problems

Here is the core of the diagnosis. The telos requires a single relation to discharge two roles, and the roles call for relations of opposite character.

Introduce a distinction. Call a relation **receptive** when its epistemic credit depends on the subject's state being *controlled or constrained by* its object — as a perception is credited by the scene's causing it, an inference by the truth of its premises, a reliable belief by its tracking the fact. Call a relation **non-receptive** — *agential* or *expressive* — when the subject's state is not credited by being controlled-by the object: as in deciding, committing, avowing, where the subject does not *answer to* an antecedent fact but *institutes* or *stands behind* one. The distinction is the familiar one between receptivity and spontaneity (Rödl 2007), here applied to the relata of self-ascription.

Two observations about the two roles the telos conflates.

*Warrant is a receptive notion.* On any standard epistemology, what makes a belief knowledge is that it is appropriately *sensitive to* what makes it true — caused by it, inferred from it, reliably covarying with it. A state that owed its standing to nothing about its sensitivity to the fact would not, by those lights, be *warranted* at all. So to satisfy role one — to be a warrant — a relation must be receptive, or at least must borrow its credit from something receptive.

*First-person specialness is a non-receptive notion.* The asymmetry, immediacy, and authority that make self-knowledge distinctive are precisely the marks of a relation that is *not* a matter of tracking. I do not stand to my own belief as a detector to a detected fact; that is why there is no felt gap to cross, no genuine "let me check whether I believe it," and why my pronouncement carries an authority no observer's could. The authority of "I believe it's raining" is the authority of one who is *answerable for* the belief, not the reliability of one who has *measured* it well. So to satisfy role two — to seat the specialness — a relation must be non-receptive.

The telos demands one relation that is both. That demand cannot be met, and the five objections of §2 are the five ways of failing to meet it.

The **receptive** accounts (a)–(b) satisfy role one and forfeit role two. Inner observation and self-interpretation are paradigmatically receptive, and they purchase warrant at the cost of the specialness: if knowing my mind is tracking it, it is continuous with knowing yours, the asymmetry goes (Ryle; Boghossian), and I am a spectator of my own attitude (Moran's alienation). Empiricist transparency is the most instructive case, because it is a receptive relation *wearing* a first-personal disguise: Byrne's safety is a tracking-credential, and Boyle's objection is exactly that the tracking-credential leaves the relation first-personally opaque — reliable but not the subject's own reasoning. The receptive pole secures knowledge and loses the self.

The **non-receptive** accounts (d)–(e) satisfy role two and cannot, *as such*, discharge role one. Agency and expression capture the immediacy and authority precisely because they are not tracking-relations — but for that very reason they are not *warrants*, and the telos requires a warrant. Hence the agentialist's predicament: charged with not being a genuine epistemic competitor (because control-*over* is not the control-*by* that knowledge wants), she is driven to reattach an epistemic credential, and the reattachment is what makes Moran "too demanding" (O'Brien) — the reflective grasp of one's reasons *as evidence* is a receptive supplement bolted onto a non-receptive base. Hence, symmetrically, the expressivist's predicament: an avowal is the wrong kind of thing to be a *cognitive achievement* exactly because it is groundless and non-receptive, and Campbell's "without expressivism," with its acknowledged unfinished business, is the attempt to supply the missing receptive credential without giving up the groundlessness that was the insight. The non-receptive pole seats the self and cannot, on the telos's terms, count as knowledge.

The **middle** accounts (c) are crushed from both sides, which is why they are the most revealing. The reasons and rationalist theories try to find a warrant that is rational rather than observational — a non-receptive credential — and the objections show the telos forcing a receptive demand back in: Coliva's complaint that the judgment is not *accessible* enough to rationalize the self-ascription is the demand that the rational base be *known*, i.e., received; the rationalist's dilemma is that a constitution strong enough to *guarantee* knowledge is too strong to be *our* psychology. The middle is unstable because it is the place where the two roles are pulled apart most visibly.

So the field's objections are not five independent difficulties. They are the determinate consequences of one demand: that a single relation be both receptive (to be a warrant) and non-receptive (to be first-personally special). Each account satisfies the demand it can and fails the one it cannot, and the pattern of failure — warrant-without-self at one pole, self-without-warrant at the other, instability in the middle — is the signature of the telos. A presupposition that, held fixed, deforms every attempt to theorize a phenomenon into one of two complementary failures is not a neutral statement of the explanandum. It is the problem.

## 5. Why we should abandon it

A presupposition can generate a field's problems and still be true and indispensable; showing that the telos generates the problems is not yet showing that we should give it up. Three considerations together make the case.

First, *the problems dissolve without it, and only without it.* The receptive/non-receptive tension is a tension only because *one* relation is required to be both a warrant and the seat of specialness. Drop the demand that the special relation be a warrant — drop, that is, the insistence that the distinctive first-person relation *be* a relation of knowledge — and the tension evaporates: the non-receptive relation (owning) is allowed to be what it manifestly is, a relation of answerability rather than of tracking, and is no longer charged with a failure (to be a warrant) that was never its job. No rearrangement of the accounts *within* the telos can do this, because the tension is the telos's own shadow. The diagnosis thus carries a recommendation: if a single assumption is what turns every candidate relation into one of two failures, and dropping it turns the best candidate into a success, that is a reason to drop it. This is an inference to the best diagnosis, and it is the same form of reasoning by which we reject a framework that can only generate antinomies.

Second, *the telos is a presupposition, not a datum.* What we actually find, pre-theoretically, is the special relation: the immediacy, the authority, the asymmetry. That this relation *is a relation of knowledge* — rather than a relation from which knowledge may be *derived* — is already a theoretical gloss, and §4 has shown it to be a costly one. The objector will protest that surely the relation *is* knowledge; we *do* come to know our minds. I devote §7 to this, but the headline of the reply belongs here: abandoning the telos does *not* deny that we come to know our own attitudes. It denies only that this knowledge is the *fundamental* first-person fact, the seat of the specialness, or the thing a theory of self-knowledge must put first. The knowledge is preserved, as a product; what is abandoned is its primacy.

Third, *abandoning it explains more than keeping it.* The telos cannot explain why first-person access has the very features — groundlessness, immediacy, non-alienation, an authority unlike a good detector's — that drove the non-receptive accounts; on the telos these features are an embarrassment, the marks of something that looks too unlike knowledge to be it. Released from the telos, the same features are exactly what one expects of a non-receptive, agential relation. And the *profile of error* fits: we go wrong about our attitudes not by random mis-measurement, as a faulty detector would, but through self-deception, evasion, and disavowal — failures of *owning*, not of *tracking*. A view on which the fundamental relation is owning predicts the shape of our fallibility; a view on which it is detection must treat that shape as a coincidence.

The three considerations are an argument to abandon the telos: it is dispensable (the data survive its loss), it is culpable (it generates the problems), and its rival is more explanatory (it predicts the features and the errors). What survives its abandonment is the subject of the next section.

## 6. The owning thesis

(T) holds that first-person access to one's avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes is constituted by a stance of *owning* the attitude — a first-personal answerability for it, in which the subject stands behind the attitude as hers to defend, revise, and act on — and not by any relation of detection; and that the distinctiveness of self-knowledge is in the first instance *practical*, its epistemic features following from the owning rather than constituting it.

**Three marks** distinguish owning from detection, and each was foreshadowed by an objection that the telos turned against the non-receptive accounts but that becomes, here, a positive datum.

*(i) The failure-signature is self-deception, not misperception.* When first-person access to an attitude goes wrong, its characteristic shape is not the misreading of an inner item but the refusal or inability to own what is one's own: the disavowed belief, the unacknowledged motive, the bad-faith denial. A relation whose failures are failures of honesty is not a relation of detection. (This is the scope-and-recalcitrance phenomenon that embarrassed agentialism, now correctly placed: the recalcitrant cases are not counterexamples to owning but its breakdown mode — see below.)

*(ii) It is groundless.* The subject who avows her belief offers no evidence and passes through no intervening judgment, because owning a commitment is not the conclusion of an inquiry into whether one has it. (This is the expressivist's insight, now unembarrassed: groundlessness is not a deficiency in a would-be cognitive achievement but the positive mark of a non-receptive relation.)

*(iii) It is ineliminably first-personal.* Another can *attribute* an attitude to me on evidence; only I can *own* mine. The asymmetry is one of authorship, not of epistemic access — Moran's insight, freed from the requirement that the asymmetry be epistemic.

**Preservation of the explananda.** The data that made self-knowledge a distinctive topic are preserved under (T) — and, I have argued, better placed than under the telos. *Asymmetry* is the answerability/authorship asymmetry of mark (iii). *First-person authority* is the standing of the one who owns: to meet "I believe it's raining" with "no, you don't" is not to correct a measurement but to refuse the subject her authorship. *Moore's paradox* — the incoherence of "it's raining but I don't believe it" — is the incoherence of disavowing what one is in the act of asserting, since to assert *p* is to own the commitment to *p*; the irrationality lies at the level of owning, not of evidence. *Immediacy* is the absence of a gap: there is nothing to cross because one is not detecting anything.

**Placement.** (T) is the convergence point of the two non-receptive families, and it is best understood by what it removes from each. From agentialism it takes *owning/answerability* and removes the burden — imposed by the telos, not by the insight — of converting owning into a warrant; this dissolves the "epistemic burden" objection (owning need not be a knowledge-relation, because the knowledge is downstream) and O'Brien's "too demanding" (no reflective grasp of one's reasons *as evidence* is required, since one is not assembling a warrant). From expressivism it takes *groundless avowal* and removes the burden of making a groundless expression into a cognitive achievement; this answers the standing worry against expressivism and supplies precisely the non-epistemic analysis of first-person privilege that Campbell (2022) leaves open. The nearest kin is Coliva's (2012) constitutive view, on which judging "I believe that *p*" *undertakes* the commitment it ascribes; (T) subsumes the performativity — owning is something one does — while dropping Coliva's residual aim of thereby securing a *true self-ascription*, which keeps her inside the telos. The difference is the whole point: for Coliva the doing is in the service of a knowledge-output; for (T) the doing is the answerability, and the knowledge is a by-product.

**Recalcitrance and the breakdown mode.** The standing objection to any agential view — that detached self-observation does not preclude ownership and can even be *enlightening* (Reed 2010; Borgoni 2015; Doyle 2019) — is granted in full and located. The cases in which one must treat an attitude as an object of inquiry are the cases in which one *cannot presently own it*: the recalcitrant, the self-deceived, the akratic. That is the breakdown profile predicted by mark (i). Detached self-observation is the remedial supplement one reaches for when owning has failed; its usefulness in those cases is no evidence that owning is not the constitutive mode in the cases in good order. A theory of vision is not refuted by spectacles.

**Scope.** (T) is restricted to the avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes. I do not extend it to sensations, where the acquaintance theorists are plausibly right that one's relation to a present pain is not a matter of *owning* it — there is "no appearance/reality distinction in the case of sensations" (Hill 1991), and answerability is a poor model for an itch. A hybrid is therefore both available and correct: acquaintance for the sensory, owning for the attitudinal. This is a sharpening, not a retreat. The attitudes are the ground on which the whole dispute of §§2–4 is actually fought, and it is there that abandoning the telos pays.

## 7. Objections

**Objection 1: this is a relabeling of agentialism or expressivism.** The content of (T) lies near Moran and Bar-On, and I have granted as much. But the contribution is not the content; it is the diagnosis (§§3–4) and the consequence drawn from it (§5). Agentialism and expressivism appear in the literature as two *accounts among the eight*, each defended on the telos's own terms and each defeated, on those terms, by the objections of §2. (T) is what they become when the telos is removed: their insights stop being failures *because* they are no longer asked to be warrants. No one reaches this by reading agentialism or expressivism as one more candidate; one reaches it only by the diagnosis that the candidates' shared presupposition is the disease. The relabeling charge mistakes the conclusion for the argument.

**Objection 2: but we *do* come to know what we believe and feel, and that was the explanandum.** This is the strongest objection, and it must be met in full, for it appears to convict (T) of explaining the phenomenon away. Regiment it: (O1) our self-ascriptions are true and non-accidentally warranted — knowledge; (O2) a theory must explain this; (O3) (T) says the fundamental relation is owning, "not a relation of knowledge"; (O4) so (T) either denies the datum or fails to explain it. I deny (O4).

*(a) The datum is preserved.* (T) grants that we come to have true, authoritative second-order beliefs about our attitudes; that is knowledge, and (T) keeps it. (T) denies only that this knowledge is the *fundamental* or *constitutive* first-person fact.

*(b) The debt is discharged.* Owning *p* makes "I believe that *p*" true — the standing-behind is the truth-maker, so the self-ascription does not track an independent fact and risk missing it — and authoritative, warranted by the standing of the one who owns rather than by evidence. So the second-order knowledge is real and *explained*: its truth-maker and its warrant are the same act of owning. To know what one believes is to make explicit, in a true and authoritative second-order belief, what one already owns.

*(c) The disagreement is located.* (O1)'s phrase "that was the explanandum" packs in the telos: it assumes the *distinctive* relation is itself epistemic. (T) holds that the distinctive relation is practical — answerability — from which the epistemic features follow. So (T) explains *more*: it explains both the knowledge and its otherwise-puzzling character — the groundless, gapless, yet authoritative quality that is an embarrassment to any view on which self-knowledge is a kind of tracking.

*(d) The error cases confirm rather than refute.* (T) is not committed to infallibility. Owning makes the self-ascription true *only when owning succeeds*; when it fails — self-deception, disavowal, the wishful belief one will not claim — the second-order ascription comes apart from the attitude and is false. These are exactly the actual error cases. So (T) predicts the *shape* of our fallibility — clustered around motivated failure-to-own, not random mis-measurement — which the detection model must treat as mysterious.

This last point also threads the needle the reply might seem to snag on. To say owning "makes the self-ascription true" is not to assert an *infallible* constitution (which would falsely predict we cannot err), nor to smuggle a *detection* relation back in (the self-ascription is not credited by tracking). It is constitution-*when-successful*: a truth-making that can fail, and whose failure is self-deception. That is neither infallibilism nor receptivity, and it is exactly the profile the data show.

**Objection 3: the receptive/non-receptive distinction is too crude; some warrants are not receptive (a priori knowledge, knowledge of one's own decisions).** I can grant non-perceptual warrant without granting what the objection needs. The claim of §4 is not that all knowledge is receptive but that *warrant*, the relation that makes a second-order belief count as knowledge *of a state it is about*, is a relation of sensitivity to that state — and that the first-person specialness is, by contrast, a non-sensitivity (an authority that does not consist in tracking). Cases of self-knowledge of one's own *decisions* are, if anything, evidence *for* (T): one knows what one has decided not by detecting a decision but by having *made* it — which is owning, not receptivity. The distinction is doing exactly the work it should.

**Objection 4: "owning" is too freighted a notion for the belief that it will rain.** Distinguish the *form* from its *intensity*. The form — answerability, standing-behind — is present in every case, however trivial, which is why even the flattest Moorean conjunction registers as a failure of *owning* rather than of *evidence*. The dramatic cases of self-deception and avowal make the form vivid; they are not the only cases in which it operates.

## 8. Conclusion

The theory of self-knowledge has divided over which relation a subject bears to her own mind, and has not converged because each candidate relation is asked, by an assumption the candidates share, to be two incompatible things at once: a warrant, which is a relation of sensitivity to an independent fact, and the seat of first-person specialness, which is a relation of authorship that consists in *not* tracking. This assumption — the knowledge-telos — is not the innocent observation that self-knowledge is knowledge; it is the substantive and, I have argued, false claim that the *fundamental* first-person relation is itself a relation of knowledge. Its abandonment costs nothing we should keep: the knowledge it promised survives as the downstream product of a more basic relation, and the very features it could not explain — groundlessness, immediacy, non-alienation, and a fallibility shaped like self-deception rather than mis-measurement — become exactly what one should expect. What is left standing, once the telos is gone, is the owning thesis: first-person access to one's avowable attitudes is a stance of answerability, and the distinctiveness of self-knowledge is in the first instance practical. We do, in the end, come to know our own minds. But the knowing is the registering of an owning already in place; it is less like reading a gauge and more like standing behind one's word.
