# What the Objections Assume: A Joint Defense of Agentialism and Expressivism about Self-Knowledge

*Draft v5 — working paper. A middle-ambition *defense*: it does not propose a new theory of self-knowledge, nor argue that self-knowledge is non-receptive; it argues that the objections widely taken to refute agentialism and expressivism share one premise — a *receptive conception of knowledge* — which those views are entitled to reject, and which is in any case explanatorily idle for the features that make self-knowledge distinctive. Citations are to primary texts; a few items reached only through Gertler's SEP survey are flagged.*

## Abstract

Agentialism (self-knowledge is grounded in rational agency) and expressivism (avowals are groundless expressions, not detections) are usually treated as embattled minority views, each sunk by a decisive objection: agentialism by the charge that it "changes the topic" from how we *know* our attitudes to how we *shape* them (agency is control *over* the attitude, not the belief's being controlled *by* it), and expressivism by the charge that a groundless avowal is no epistemic achievement and so no knowledge. I argue, first, that these are *one* objection seen twice: each deploys, as its load-bearing premise, a **receptive conception of knowledge** — that a second-order self-ascription is knowledge only by being controlled-by or answerable-to the attitude as an antecedent, owning-independent fact. Since agentialism and expressivism are precisely the views that deny this, the objections beg the question, and the two views stand or fall together. Second, I answer the empiricist's natural rejoinder ("the receptive conception is correct; you owe an alternative") with an **idle-wheel test**: granting that reliability explains why self-ascriptions are *true*, I argue that for each feature that makes self-knowledge *distinctive* — first-person authority, the asymmetry of Moore's paradox — the receptive component does no explanatory work, and a non-receptive element does it instead. The burden therefore reverses. I do not show that self-knowledge is non-receptive; I show that the standard objections do not show it is, and that the question they assume settled is open.

## 1. Introduction

Two families of view about self-knowledge define themselves against the picture on which knowing one's own mind is a matter of *detecting* an inner state. **Agentialism** holds that first-person access to one's reasons-sensitive attitudes is grounded in rational agency — in one's responsibility for, and authorship of, what one believes and intends (Moran 2001; Boyle 2009; McGeer 1996; Coliva 2012). **Expressivism** holds that an avowal — "I believe it's raining," "I'm in pain" — is a groundless, evidence-free *expression* of the state it ascribes, authoritative because it is an expression, not because it is a good measurement (Bar-On 2004; Finkelstein 2003). The two are usually filed apart, and each is usually thought to face a decisive objection of its own.

Agentialism's reputed refutation is the **change-the-topic** objection: "Knowledge ordinarily involves the knower… being appropriately *controlled by* what is known… our agency… concerns a direction of control that is the reverse… The agentialist emphasis on agency thus seems to change the topic, from how we *know* our own attitudes to how we *shape* those attitudes" (Gertler 2018). Agency explains *responsibility*, not *warrant*; so agentialism is "not a genuine competitor… unless it explains how agency… provides for self-knowledge" (Gertler 2024, §3.7). Expressivism's reputed refutation is the **cognitive-achievement** objection: knowledge is a status a belief must *earn* through some epistemic work; an avowal, being groundless by definition, earns nothing; so however reliably true, it is not *knowledge* (the worry the whole sophisticated expressivist literature exists to answer — Gertler 2024, §3.8; Brueckner 2006).

This paper makes two claims, one defensive and one offensive, and is careful about what it does *not* claim.

The defensive claim (§3): these two objections are **one objection**. Each rests on a single load-bearing premise — a *receptive conception of knowledge*, on which a second-order self-ascription counts as knowledge only by being controlled-by or answerable-to the attitude as an antecedent, owning-independent fact. Agentialism and expressivism are, at bottom, the denial that self-knowledge of one's attitudes is receptive in this sense. So the objections do not *refute* these views; they *presuppose their falsity*. They beg the question — and, since they beg it in the same way, agentialism and expressivism stand or fall together.

The offensive claim (§4): the empiricist will reply that the receptive conception is not a question-begging prejudice but the *correct* conception of knowledge, so that the burden is simply returned — the agentialist and expressivist owe an alternative. I answer with an **idle-wheel test**. Grant the empiricist that reliability explains why our self-ascriptions are, by and large, *true* (I do not deny that self-knowledge is knowledge). The test asks, of each feature that makes self-knowledge a *distinctive topic* — first-person authority, the peculiar asymmetry of Moore's paradox — whether the *receptive* component of the empiricist's account does the explaining, or whether a *non-receptive* element must be smuggled in to do it. I argue the latter, case by case. If that is right, the receptive conception is not merely *one option among two*; it is *explanatorily idle for the very features at issue*, and the burden reverses: it is the empiricist who owes an account of why, if self-knowledge is receptive, receptivity explains none of its distinctiveness.

What I do **not** claim: I do not propose a new theory of self-knowledge, and I do not argue that self-knowledge *is* non-receptive. Whether the attitudes are antecedent facts one detects or commitments one sustains — the question on which the receptivity of self-knowledge ultimately turns — is left open. The thesis is narrower and, I hope, more secure: the objections that are supposed to settle that question instead *assume* it, and assume it on the side of a conception of knowledge that does no work where it would have to.

A note on scope and provenance. I restrict the discussion to *avowable, reasons-sensitive attitudes* (belief, intention); sensations, for which an acquaintance model and a receptive conception may well be correct, are set aside (§5). And I should say at once what is owed to others: the local observation that reliability is idle for first-person *authority* is Campbell's (2022); my contribution is to generalize it into a test, to run it together with the cross-view unification, and to keep the whole within a defense that incurs no constitutivist debt.

## 2. The views and the objections that are taken to sink them

**Agentialism, charitably.** The agentialist begins from the datum that I do not relate to my own beliefs as to found objects. Asked whether I believe it will rain, I do not investigate myself; I make up or stand behind my mind, and I am *answerable* for the belief in a way no observer is. Moran's version: a rational subject has an "epistemic right" to assume her attitudes answer to her reasons, and this entitlement licenses the transparency method (answer "do I believe p?" by considering p). McGeer's: self-ascription is "commissive," a commitment one undertakes and lives up to, more like the knowledge of a driver than a passenger (McGeer 1996). Coliva's: to judge "I believe that p" is to *undertake* the commitment it ascribes. What unites them is that the distinctive first-person relation is one of *agency/answerability*, not detection.

**The objections to agentialism.** (i) *Change-the-topic / not a genuine competitor* (Gertler 2018): agency is control *over* the attitude, the reverse of the control-*by* that knowledge requires; so agentialism, as stated, supplies no epistemology and is at best a theory of doxastic responsibility sitting atop whatever the real epistemology is. (ii) *Too demanding* (O'Brien 2003): to get from the first-order question (whether p) to second-order knowledge (that I believe p), Moran needs me to treat my first-order reasoning *as evidence that I hold the belief* — a reflective sophistication ordinary self-ascribers lack.[^obrien]

**Expressivism, charitably.** The expressivist begins from a different datum: the *immediacy* and *groundlessness* of avowal. "I'm in pain" is not the conclusion of an inquiry; it issues from the state as a wince does, with no evidence and no intervening judgment, and yet it is authoritative. Bar-On's neo-expressivism keeps avowals truth-evaluable (so as to honor semantic continuity) while explaining their security by their expressive, rather than evidential, relation to the state.

**The objections to expressivism.** (i) *Cognitive achievement* (Brueckner 2006; Gertler 2024 §3.8): a groundless voicing, however reliable, is not the *achievement* knowledge requires — a parrot trained to say "I'm in pain" when pricked would meet the expressivist's conditions. (ii) *The expression–knowledge gap* (Boyle 2010): from the avower's own standpoint, either she makes a comprehending, truth-evaluable assertion about herself — in which case, grasping that she is committed to a state of affairs, how is she *entitled* to assert it with no evidence? — or she does not, in which case we collapse into a classical expressivism on which one learns one's mind by overhearing oneself. "Bar-On has not yet explained how it can be reasonable for us to make avowals without evidence."

These are the objections the literature treats as primary. I now argue they are one.

## 3. The shared premise, and the joint defense

**Two things "the knowledge-telos" might mean.** It is tempting to say the field shares a "knowledge-telos." But one must distinguish (i) the bare claim that *self-knowledge is knowledge* — true, and shared, but nearly trivial, since it merely names the topic — from (ii) the substantive **receptive conception**: that a second-order self-ascription is knowledge *by being controlled-by or answerable-to* the attitude as an antecedent, owning-independent fact. It is (ii), not (i), that does the work in the objections, and it is (ii), not (i), that agentialism and expressivism deny. They keep (i): each insists the upshot is genuine self-*knowledge*. What they reject is (ii): that knowing one's attitude is a matter of one's belief being made to conform to a fact that obtains independently of one's owning it.

**The objections deploy (ii).** The change-the-topic objection says in so many words that knowledge requires the knowing-state to be "controlled by what is known," and faults agency for being the reverse direction of control. That *is* the receptive conception, stated as a premise. The cognitive-achievement objection says a groundless state cannot be knowledge because it has not earned its status by the right kind of epistemic work — which presupposes that knowledge of one's attitude must be *answerable to* the attitude in a way evidence-gathering or reliable tracking would secure and groundless avowal does not. Again: the receptive conception, as a premise.

**So the objections beg the question, and the two views are one target.** Each objection refutes its target only by assuming the very thesis the target was advanced to deny. That is not a decisive refutation; it is a statement of the opposing position. And the point generalizes across the two literatures: the "primary burden" on agentialism and the "price of admission" for expressivism are the same demand — that a non-receptive relation deliver a receptive prize. It follows that agentialism and expressivism are not two separately embattled views but **two faces of one position** — the denial that self-knowledge of the attitudes is receptive — facing **one premise**, which is just the empiricist's conception of knowledge. They stand or fall together. A single concession (that self-knowledge of one's attitudes need not be receptive) answers the primary objection to both at once; a single premise (that it must be) sinks both at once. The debate is, at this level, *one* question, and the standard objections do not advance it — they presuppose its answer.

This is the defensive result. It shows the objections are not decisive. It does not yet show they are *wrong*, because the empiricist can accept all of it and dig in.

## 4. The burden re-asserted, and the idle-wheel test

**The rejoinder.** "Grant that my objection deploys the receptive conception, and that your views deny it. The receptive conception is not a prejudice; it is what knowledge *is* — a belief is knowledge by being suitably sensitive to what makes it true. So pointing out that I assume it does not relieve you of anything; it states precisely your burden, which is to provide an alternative notion of knowledge on which control-*over* yields knowledge. Until you pay, my objection stands."

This rejoinder is the real threat to a defense, and it cannot be met by repeating that the conception is "assumed." It must be met by showing that the conception, however correct elsewhere, *does no work here*. That is the idle-wheel test.

**Two explananda-classes.** Distinguish (A) the *reliability/truth* of self-ascriptions — that they are by and large true — from (B) the features that make self-knowledge a *distinctive topic*: first-person **authority**, **immediacy**, and the peculiar asymmetry of **Moore's paradox**. I grant the empiricist (A) without reservation: reliability may well explain why self-ascriptions are usually true, and I have not denied that self-knowledge is knowledge. The question is whether the receptive conception earns (B). I argue it does not — that for each feature in (B), the explanatory work is done by a *non-receptive* element, and the receptive component is an idle wheel.

**(B1) Authority — the wrong kind of deference.** First-person authority is the defeasible impropriety of another's overriding one's sincere, present-tense self-ascription. If self-ascription were receptive, the proper ground of others' deference would be *evidential*: the avowal reliably indicates the fact, so one defers as to a trusted instrument. But the deference owed an avowal is not evidential in character. We may *properly* override even a highly reliable instrument when we have countervailing evidence, and in doing so we do not *wrong* it; whereas to override a person's sincere avowal of her own mind, absent special reason, is not merely to risk error — it is to fail to accord her a *standing*, to wrong *her*. The deference is second-personal, owed *to her*, not merely owed to the truth. So authority is a normative standing, not an evidential reliability; and reliability — which could at most underwrite deference-to-an-instrument — is idle for it. The empiricist may grant that authority is a normative or conventional matter while insisting self-knowledge is still receptive *knowledge*; but that concession is the point — it removes authority, a Class-B feature, from the receptive conception's explanatory reach, leaving it to explain (at most) Class-A truth.[^wright]

**(B2) The Moore-asymmetry — a triangulation.** "p, but I don't believe p" is incoherent in the present first person. But "p, but I didn't believe p" (past, first person) and "p, but S doesn't believe p" (present, third person) are not incoherent at all — merely possibly false. Now, a *receptive* relation is symmetric across time and person: detecting my present belief is the same *kind* of act as detecting my past belief or another's, so a detection-model predicts no special present-first-person incoherence, only a conjunction that might happen to be false, exactly as in the other two cells. The incoherence is therefore not a feature a detection-relation can explain; it is the incoherence of *commitment* — to assert p just is to undertake the commitment whose disavowal the second conjunct attempts. One might note that the empiricist is not silent here: Byrne (2005) explains Moore's paradox through his schema, "p ∴ I believe p." But that is exactly the point — the schema is a *rule one follows in committing oneself*, a non-receptive element; the empiricist's own account of this Class-B feature runs on a committal wheel, not a tracking one.

**(B3) Security — held in reserve.** A third consideration points the same way but cannot be pressed here without incurring the debt this paper refuses. Receptive knowledge is Gettierizable, because belief and fact are independent and can be linked by luck; yet there are no genuine Gettier cases for present-tense self-ascription of one's avowable attitudes. The natural explanation — that the fact is not independent of the ascription — *is* the constitutivist claim I am leaving open. So I record the datum and its suggestiveness, and decline to lean on it: it marks the seam at which this defense would give way to a positive, and heavier, account.[^gettier]

**The result.** Across the distinctiveness-features (B1)–(B2), the receptive component of the empiricist's account spins idle; what explains authority and the Moore-asymmetry is in each case a non-receptive element — a standing, a commitment, a rule one follows. So the rejoinder fails. The receptive conception is not merely *one option among two*; it is explanatorily idle for the features that make self-knowledge distinctive. The burden does not return symmetrically to the agentialist and expressivist; it reverses: it is the empiricist who must explain why, if self-knowledge is receptive, receptivity does none of the work where the action is. Reliability has its place — it explains why we are usually right — but being usually right is not what makes self-knowledge *special*, and it was the special features the objections claimed these views could not account for.

## 5. The objections that do *not* assume the receptive conception

Honesty requires conceding that not every objection to these views deploys the receptive conception, and that the joint defense therefore does not dispatch all of them. Three deserve direct treatment; in each case the defense's work is positive rather than diagnostic, and in two of the three the objection turns out to be congenial.

**Levy: observation is not alienation.** Levy (2016) argues that Moran's privileging of the deliberative over the observational has the normative valence backwards: for addicts, the rationalist prescription ("look to your reasons") is counterproductive, while *impersonal*, third-personal self-knowledge — "my name is Joe and I'm an alcoholic," treating one's addiction as an external fact — is what restores agency. This is not a *receptive-conception* objection; it cannot be deflected, and it must be met. Three points meet it. First, the defense need not inherit Moran's strong evaluative thesis that observational self-knowledge is *alienating*; the core agentialist claim is descriptive (the distinctive relation to *ownable* attitudes is answerability), and is fully compatible with conceding that detached self-knowledge is genuine and sometimes agency-restoring. Second, "I am an alcoholic" is knowledge of a standing *condition*, not first-person access to an occurrent *ownable* attitude; it belongs to a category (substantial self-knowledge of one's dispositions) the agentialist never claimed for agency. Third, and most telling, Levy's *positive* moral is itself agentialist: agency is restored through a *regulative, commissive* self-relation (the recovering addict reclaims agency by *taking on* "I am an alcoholic" — an avowal, an act of owning a condition), which is McGeer's commissive agentialism, not Moran's deliberative purism. Levy thus does not refute agentialism; he adjudicates an *intramural* dispute in favor of the commissive version this defense already prefers. We concede Moran's anti-observation overlay, gladly, and keep the core.

**Wright: authority is grammar, not knowledge.** The deepest objection, and the one this paper does *not* claim to defeat. Wright (1998) holds that first-person authority is a constitutive feature of the self-ascription language-game — a by-product of its rules — and not a cognitive accomplishment at all; so an account that explains authority has illuminated our *practice* and *changed the subject* away from *knowledge*. Two things must be said. First, the present paper is in one respect on Wright's side: §4 (B1) argues precisely that authority is a normative standing rather than an evidential achievement, which is congenial to Wright's anti-evidential point. Second, the disagreement that remains — whether a non-evidential, standing-based first-person relation can still be a mode of *knowing*, or only of *avowing* — is exactly the open question this paper declines to settle. I claim only that Wright's objection, too, does not vindicate the *receptive* conception; it is a rival *deflationary* account, not a defense of detection. Adjudicating it requires the positive theory I have set aside, and I flag it as the residual front.

**Scope, and the empirical worries.** Expressivism is pressed by the complaint that the wince-model fits sensations but strains for standing attitudes one is not currently expressing (Boyle 2009), and both views by the empirical unreliability of introspection (Schwitzgebel 2008; Nisbett & Wilson 1977). These are real, and they bear on the *positive* theory rather than the defense. The scope worry is in fact accommodated by the restriction announced in §1: sensations are plausibly receptive (acquaintance), and the defense is offered for the avowable attitudes, where the wince-model is anyway the wrong model and *owning*, not venting, is the operative notion. The empirical worries target the reliability and fine-grained accuracy of introspective report — Class-A matters — and so do not touch a defense that has *granted* Class A and located the action in Class B.

## 6. Allies, placement, and what is and is not new

The view this defense protects has close kin, and candor about them fixes the contribution. **Campbell** (2022) is the most important: a critic of expressivism whose own "expression without expressivism" holds that self-knowledge is groundless yet *epistemic* (a matter of a rational ability to respond to the fact, not of evidence). Her Parallelist Dilemma against Bar-On — that an evidence-free self-knowledge makes the non-epistemic account of authority *redundant* — is the local original of the idle-wheel test (reliability idle for authority). Two consequences. First, the dispute between Bar-On and Campbell is, on the present analysis, largely terminological: both accept groundless privileged self-knowledge; they differ over whether to call it "epistemic." Second, Campbell's acknowledged open problem — that to *explain* rather than presuppose privilege, her "rational response" must be analyzed non-epistemically — is exactly the place where the non-receptive agentialist notion of *answerability* could be put to work. **Rödl** (2007) is the nearest precedent for the bare thesis that self-knowledge is not receptive ("knowledge from spontaneity"), reached by a different (Kantian) route and without abandoning the category of knowledge.

So what is new here is neither the idea that self-ascription is more like owning than detecting (agentialist and expressivist tradition), nor the bare rejection of the achievement conception (Campbell, Rödl). It is three things: the **cross-view unification** (that the primary objection to agentialism and the primary objection to expressivism are one receptive-conception objection, so the views stand or fall together); the generalization of Campbell's local point into a **systematic idle-wheel test** that answers the burden-re-assertion; and the **bounded conclusion** that follows — that the objections do not advance the debate, while the question they assume is left open. The contribution is modest by design: a clarification of the dialectic and a reversal of a burden, not a theory of self-knowledge.

## 7. Conclusion

Agentialism and expressivism look like two beleaguered views, each with its own fatal objection. They are one view with one objection. The view is that self-knowledge of one's own attitudes is not a matter of detecting an antecedent inner fact; the objection is that knowledge requires exactly such detection. Stated that way, the objection plainly begs the question — and, worse for the objector, the conception of knowledge it deploys turns out to be idle precisely where self-knowledge is distinctive: first-person authority is a standing one is accorded, not the reliability of an instrument; the incoherence of Moore's paradox is a failure of commitment, not of measurement. Reliability explains that we are usually right about our own minds; it does not explain what is special about how we are right, and it was the special features that were at issue. None of this shows that self-knowledge is non-receptive — that question, which turns on whether our attitudes are facts we find or commitments we sustain, is left where it should be, open. What it shows is that the objections meant to close it do not; they presuppose an answer, and presuppose it on the side of a conception of knowledge that, here, does no work. The agentialist and the expressivist may proceed.

## Notes

[^obrien]: The "too demanding" objection is reconstructed from Gertler's gloss of O'Brien (2003: 379–81) and from Moran's (2003) reply; I have not consulted the original article directly, and flag the citation accordingly. Boyle (2011) supplies the standard agentialist answer (the transition is not an inference, so no demanding meta-cognition is required); I do not rely on the objection.

[^wright]: This concession aligns the present argument with Wright's (1998) claim that authority is a matter of the grammar of the practice rather than a cognitive achievement — see §5. The point here is only that, whatever authority is, reliability does not explain it; the further question whether a standing-based authority is compatible with *knowledge* is the one §5 leaves open.

[^gettier]: The cleanest way to press the point would be: receptive knowledge is Gettierizable because belief and fact are independent; present-tense self-ascription of one's attitudes is not Gettierizable; so the relation is not the contingent belief–fact link receptive knowledge requires. The empiricist replies that *safety* explains the immunity; the rejoinder — that the ascription is safe *because* the fact is not independent of it — is the constitutivist claim this paper declines to defend. Hence "in reserve."
