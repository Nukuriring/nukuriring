## 0. Scope & framing

- **Definition.** In philosophy, "self-knowledge" standardly = **knowledge of one's own mental states** — what one is feeling/thinking/believing/desiring. (A *separate* topic — knowledge of a persisting self — is handled in a different SEP supplement and is **not** this entry's subject.)
- **The Cartesian baseline.** Since Descartes, most philosophers hold that self-knowledge **differs markedly** from knowledge of the external world (incl. knowledge of others' minds). But there is **little agreement on what exactly distinguishes it**, and hence competing accounts of *how* we get it and *what epistemic status* it has.
- **Stakes.** These accounts bear on epistemology, philosophy of mind, and moral psychology.
- **Map of the entry:** §1 the *distinctiveness* of self-knowledge (four marks); §2 *doubts* about distinctiveness (general + empirical); §3 *eight accounts*, ordered **empiricist → a priori/rationalist → agency/expression**.

## 1. THE DISTINCTIVENESS OF SELF-KNOWLEDGE — four candidate marks

Self-knowledge is thought to differ from other knowledge in one or more of these ways (the **four statements**):
1. **Epistemic security** — self-knowledge is especially secure, epistemically.
2. **Special method** — it is (sometimes) acquired by an *exclusively first-personal* method.
3. **Agency** — our capacity for it reflects our *cognitive agency*.
4. **First-person authority** — one's pronouncements about one's own states carry special authority / presumption of truth.

**How they differ:** (1) picks out the distinctiveness as the **epistemic status** of a class of beliefs; (2) by the **method** of forming them; (3) emphasizes the subject's **agency** (esp. for reason-sensitive attitudes like beliefs/intentions); (4) focuses on how self-ascriptions ("I'm in pain") are **treated by others**. Statements (1)+(2) cash out **"privileged access."** Only (1)+(2) make the distinctive feature *plainly epistemic*; most who hold (3) also claim the agential relation *grounds* a special epistemic relation. A **minority deny self-knowledge is special at all** (§2).

### 1.1 Epistemic security
- **Two strongest claims:**
  - **Infallibility** — one *cannot* have a false belief that one is in a certain mental state.
  - **Omniscience** — being in a mental state *suffices* for knowing one is in it. (a.k.a. states are **self-intimating / self-presenting**.)
- **Few/no contemporary philosophers** assert full infallibility/omniscience for *all* states.
- **Counterexample (against both) — Kate:** she trusts her therapist, who wrongly tells her she distrusts her mother; she believes it, but in fact she *trusts* her mother. So she is **fallible** (false self-ascription) and **non-omniscient** (fails to know she trusts her mother). Note: Kate's belief was based on **another's testimony**.
- **Restricted infallibility** limits the domain to self-ascriptions via an *exclusively first-personal method* (e.g., introspection). **Descartes** thought in-principle infallibility is achievable here, but **only with meticulous care** (1644/1984: I.66): clearly perceive sensations/emotions/appetites by judging "no more than what is strictly contained in our perception" — "a very difficult rule to observe."
- **Constitutive-connection route to infallibility/omniscience** (about beliefs/attitudes): a constitutive link between the first-order attitude and the belief that one has it. Variants: believing p *constitutes* believing one believes p, given rationality + concepts (**Shoemaker 1994**); or the reverse — *taking oneself* to believe p constitutes believing p (**Coliva 2012a; Bauman 2017**). [→ 3.6, 3.7]
- **Wittgenstein/Wright objection:** "where one cannot be wrong, one cannot be right either." Error (even second-order) must be possible for genuine **concept application**, which substantial self-knowledge requires (**Wright 1989: 634**).
- **Omniscience is worse off than infallibility** [fn. 1]. **Regress worry:** omniscience seems to require some states *comprehend themselves.* **James (1884: 2):** "No subjective state, whilst present, is its own object; its object is always something else." 
- **Weakened omniscience:** for some states, anyone in such a state is *justified* in believing she is, even if she doesn't form the belief (**Peacocke 1999; Siewert 1998; Silins 2012; Smithies 2012**); **Horgan & Kriegel (2007)** defend a modified omniscience (sensations are by definition conscious).
- **The cogito ≠ these general theses.** Descartes' **cogito** (1641) concerns the certainty of a *particular* belief: while carefully attending to your thoughts you can know *that you're thinking* (hence that you exist) — certain even given an evil genius.
- **The widely held modest view:** self-knowledge, even if not *absolutely* certain, is **more secure than other empirical knowledge** (esp. perceptual). Some ground this in a **causal gap** between a perceptual state and its object — a gap that introduces error sources *absent* in **direct introspective apprehension** of a sensation (**Russell 1917; Chalmers 2003; Gertler 2012; Horgan 2012; Siewert 2012**).

### 1.2 Special method — two opposite pictures
- **Introspection (the traditional, "inward" picture):** literally "looking within"; a spatial metaphor (inner vs external world). Most common usage = **inner observation / "inwardly directed attention"** (Goldman 2006: 246) yielding awareness of a state. **Central to Acquaintance (3.1) and Inner Sense (3.2) accounts.**
- **The transparency method (the "outward" picture):** we ascertain our own thoughts by **looking outward, to the states of the world they represent.** Self-knowledge by "**looking through**" the (transparent) mental state to the world it represents.
  - **Evans (1982: 225)** [canonical passage]: "in making a self-ascription of belief, one's eyes are, so to speak, or occasionally literally, directed **outward — upon the world.** If someone asks me 'Do you think there is going to be a third world war?', I must attend, in answering him, to precisely the same outward phenomena as I would attend to if I were answering the question 'Will there be a third world war?'"
  - **Central to Empiricist Transparency accounts (3.4) and some Agentialist accounts (3.7).**
  - ⚠ **Terminology note:** this "transparency"/"looking through" is the *self-knowledge* sense (Evans). It is a *different* technical use from Walton's "transparent pictures" (seeing the world *through a photograph*) — recorded here as a factual parallel only; synthesis deferred per project scope.

### 1.3 Agency
- Some mental states we merely **undergo** (itches, tickles); others are **active.** "Our rational beliefs and intentions are not mere mental attitudes, but **active states of normative commitment**" (**Korsgaard 2009: 39**).
- This inspires **agentialism (3.7):** the truly distinctive self-knowledge is knowledge of these *active states of normative commitment*; distinctive *because believing and intending are things we do* → not known simply by **observation** (the means for other empirical phenomena, including sensations).

### 1.4 First-person authority
- A competing approach (often attributed to **Wittgenstein**; cf. Wright 1989): the special authority of self-ascriptions is primarily a matter of **social-linguistic practices** that dictate we *treat* subjects as authoritative about their own states. Responding to "I believe it's raining" with "no, you don't" (ordinarily) shows a **misunderstanding of social-linguistic norms.**
- This diagnoses authority as deriving from **social norms**, not the subject's privileged epistemic position → **strictly speaking, not about self-knowledge.** But some **Expressivist accounts (3.8)** treat first-person authority as central to understanding self-knowledge.

## 2. DOUBTS ABOUT DISTINCTIVENESS

### 2.1 General doubts
- **Ryle (1949) / behaviorism:** the self/other difference is **at most a matter of degree**, stemming from the mundane fact that each of us is always present to observe our own behavior.
- **Theory-free observation doubts:** Dennett (1991) — no theory-free observation; **Hill (1991)** — the observational process **alters** its target [fn. 2].
- **Schwitzgebel (2008: 268):** "our judgments about the world to a large extent **drive** our judgments about our experience. Properly so, since the former are the more secure." (Self-attributions may express self-knowledge but aren't epistemically *superior*.)
- **Stich (1983) / folk-psychology-as-theory:** psychological concepts are *theoretical*; so both self- and other-ascriptions proceed by **inference from observed behavior** → self-knowledge not special.
- **Boghossian's puzzle / TRILEMMA (1989):** attitudes (beliefs, desires) are **relationally individuated** (partly by relations to other states/environment) [fn. 3: content externalism]. E.g., desiring lemonade partly = being disposed to go to the fridge when you believe lemonade is there, etc. How can we have *privileged access* to relationally-individuated states? Three options:
  - (a) **inner observation** — won't reveal relational properties;
  - (b) **inference** — not *truly privileged*;
  - (c) **on the basis of nothing** — not truly privileged.
  → a **trilemma.** Responses: deny that recognizing a relational state requires identifying its relational properties (**Burge 1988; Heil 1988**); allow privileged-yet-**inferential** access (**Dretske 1994; Byrne 2005; Lawlor 2009**); know attitudes by **introspective observation**, weakening relational construals (**Pitt 2004**).

### 2.2 Doubts based on empirical results
- **Nisbett & Wilson (1977):** subjects routinely **misidentify the factors** influencing their reasoning. (E.g., subjects justified a preference by "quality," though the items were identical — the real driver was **spatial position**.) "The accuracy of subject reports is so poor as to suggest that any introspective access … is not sufficient to produce generally correct or reliable reports" (1977: 33).
- **Wilson's own limit (2002: 106):** the argument applies only to **unconscious sources** of decisions; "to the extent that people's responses are caused by the conscious self, they have privileged access … the Nisbett and Wilson argument was wrong about such cases."
- **Schwitzgebel (2002)** marshals further evidence that **introspective reports are unreliable**; also that our *attitudes about introspection are obstinate.* The **Hurlburt & Schwitzgebel (2007)** "Experience Sampling" collaboration **failed to resolve** their disagreement — Hurlburt "optimistic," Schwitzgebel "a skeptic."
- **The "problem of calibration" (Goldman 2004: 14):** some targets (e.g., sensations) are identifiable *only* through introspection itself; third-personal methods (e.g., pain via brain scan) **depend on prior correlations** with introspective data. So we can fix introspective reliability only by *using* introspection and checking internal coherence/consistency — and there's no consensus on how. **Spener (2015):** calibrate via abilities we **couldn't have** unless introspection were reliable (e.g., focusing binoculars shows introspection reliably tracks when things "look blurry").

## 3. ACCOUNTS OF SELF-KNOWLEDGE (eight)

**Dimensions of variation:** how self-knowledge is *achieved*; *kind/degree of epistemic security*; nature of *privilege* (epistemic) vs *authority* (social-linguistic); and the role of *observational / rational / agential / expressive* powers. **Scope** varies: **Acquaintance** → mainly *occurrent* states (current thinking/feeling); **Reasons & Agentialist** → *exclusively* commitment-attitudes (beliefs, intentions); **Inner Sense, Self-Interpretation, Empiricist Transparency, Rationalist, Expressivist** → at least one proponent applies each to *all* states. **Hybrids possible** (e.g., Acquaintance for sensations + Agentialism for beliefs). Ordering: **empiricist → a priori reasoning → agency/self-expression.**

### 3.1 ACQUAINTANCE accounts
- **Lineage:** Russell (1917); back to Descartes. Core: awareness of one's mental states is sometimes **peculiarly direct in two senses:**
  - **Epistemically direct** — I needn't rely on awareness of *something else* to be aware of my state.
  - **Metaphysically direct** — *no event/process mediates* between my awareness and the state. (Contrast: knowing last night's rain via wet pavement = epistemically *and* metaphysically indirect.)
- **Appeal:** foundationalism (beliefs justified but not by other beliefs).
- **Most plausible for sensations (e.g., pain):** because how a sensation **appears** = how it **is** — "there is **no appearance/reality distinction** in the case of sensations" (Hill 1991: 127; cf. Kripke 1980: 152–3).
- **Scope dispute:** which states are individuated by phenomenology? Renewed interest in a **distinctive phenomenology of thought** (**Pitt 2004** uses our direct, secure knowledge of what we're thinking to argue thoughts have proprietary phenomenology with which we're acquainted); some say conscious **judgments** have phenomenology too.
- Directness ≠ infallibility/omniscience (open whether all states are introspectible). But proper use may yield high certainty; some argue intense sensations are **"luminous"** (being in the state ensures one *can* know one is — **Weatherson 2004; Duncan 2018**), responding to **Williamson's (2000) anti-luminosity** argument (no states are luminous).
- **The James challenge (the big one):** self-knowledge requires more than direct *contact* — it requires **properly conceptualizing** the state (as *pain*, *coldness*). The hardest task for acquaintance: show that direct/immediate awareness can also be an **epistemically substantial grasp** of the state *as a state of a certain kind*.
  - **Demonstrative-reference response (Gertler 2001; Chalmers 2003):** as pointing fixes "that desk," **attending** to an experience fixes "this quality." "**Selective attention is the index finger of the mind**" (Sosa 2003: 279). Attending to the itchiness lets one grasp it *as* itchiness → a **"direct phenomenal concept"** "wholly based on the attention to the quality, 'taking up' the quality into the concept" (Chalmers 2003: 235).
- **Objections:**
  - We sometimes **err** about experiences → reply: the theory only implies that *under certain conditions* the quality constitutes the concept (then self-ascription is true) (Horgan & Kriegel 2007; Gertler 2012).
  - **Too-close objection** (Wittgenstein 1953; Stalnaker 2008): introspective beliefs are *too close* to their objects to be genuine **knowledge** — denies that direct attention provides an epistemically substantial grasp.

### 3.2 INNER SENSE accounts
- **Opposite of Acquaintance:** construes introspection as **similar to perception** (not metaphysically/epistemically direct).
- **Locke** (early inner-sense): the faculty "might properly enough be call'd **internal Sense**" (1689/1975 II.1.iv).
- **Mechanism:** a **monitoring mechanism / "self-scanning process"** (Armstrong 1993: 324) taking states as **input**, yielding **representations** as **output**; reliable because it **"redeploys" the content** of the input into the output (Nichols & Stich 2003; Goldman 2006). The input→output connection is **causal and contingent** (vs Acquaintance).
- **Differences from outer perception:** no literal **organ**; no **sensory experience** of the states ("No one thinks that one is aware of beliefs … by having sensations of them," Shoemaker 1994: 255).
- **Epistemology:** suits **reliabilism / externalism**; "a mere flow of information or beliefs" (Armstrong 1993: 326); **non-inferential**; first-personal only because scanners are aimed at one's own states — **but this asymmetry is merely contingent:** in principle one's inner-sense could be wired to *another's* states → "direct [non-inferential] awareness of the mental states of others," a kind of **telepathic scanning** (Armstrong 1993: 124).
- **Chief benefit — naturalism:** assimilating introspection to perception makes the mental **epistemically continuous** with the nonmental → a single epistemology for self- and world-knowledge; undercuts dualist arguments (which rely on epistemic divergence) [fn. 4: inner-sense theorists often accept the **higher-order perception (HOP)** theory of consciousness].
- **Shoemaker's (1994) critique — self-blindness:** assimilating self-knowledge to vision implies a rational person could **lack** it — be **"self-blind"** (unable to recognize own states). But self-blindness is **impossible in a rational creature** (with ordinary states + mental-state concepts). [His positive moral — self-knowledge tied to **rationality** — drives 3.6/3.7.]
  - **Kind (2003):** self-blindness's impossibility doesn't say *how* awareness occurs → doesn't rule out inner sense.
  - **Gertler (2011a, ch.5):** inner-sense theorist can stipulate "no creature counts as rational if self-blind" → "**No rational creature is self-blind**" becomes a **de dicto** truth, compatible with inner sense; Shoemaker needs the stronger **de re** thesis (capacity for self-knowledge a *necessary characteristic* of rational beings).
- **Further objection:** inner sense's **externalism** won't satisfy those who want **internal/accessible reasons** for belief (Peacocke 1999: 224).

### 3.3 SELF-INTERPRETATION accounts
- **Claim:** grasping our beliefs/desires (perhaps emotions) requires a process of **self-interpretation** (Lawlor 2009; Carruthers 2011; Cassam 2015). A response to the **causal-role** point (2.1): whether a state plays the relevant causal role isn't knowable by mere introspective observation (echoes Boghossian's first horn). We know it only by **interpreting** current thoughts/feelings, explaining them as manifestations of an attitude. Relies on **inference** → sometimes called **"inferentialism"** (broader category, also includes some transparency accounts, 3.4).
- **Lawlor's example — Katherine** at her son's crib thinks "have another." Inner speech alone doesn't tell her if she *wants* another child; she must **interpret**: genuine longing or idle daydream? Best interpretation = best *explanation* of the thought. Often involves **imaginative exercises** (imagine a newborn in her arms; notice it makes her happy → infer desire). Supported by research that people detect nonconscious dispositions by **vividly imagining a future situation and attending to feelings** (Wilson & Dunn 2004, re: Schultheiss & Brunstein 1999).
- **Carruthers' (2011) Interpretive-Sensory Access (ISA):** rejects a dedicated **"inner sense"**; a single **"mindreading" system** detects *both* one's own and others' states; it "**evolved for outward-looking social purposes**," only later co-opted for self-knowledge (2011: 69). Input = sensory data; interpreted via situational facts + background info; output = state-representations. Output qualifies as knowledge on **externalist** (reliability) grounds.
- **Lawlor (vs Carruthers):** friendly to **internalism** — interpretation based on internal, accessible evidence (thoughts, feelings, own behavior).
- **Echoes Ryle (1949):** self-knowledge broadly similar to knowing others — inference from what one says; **inner speech = maximally effective "eavesdropping."** "We eavesdrop on our own voiced utterances and our own silent monologues … I cannot overhear your silent colloquies with yourself" (Ryle 1949: 184). (Indebted to Byrne 2012a.)
- **Compatible with some non-inferential knowledge:** Carruthers — no interpretation for states recognizable on **raw sensory data** (the system's inputs); **Cassam** more circumspect (even knowing a **pain** sometimes takes cognitive effort, 2015: 164).
- **Regress worry:** is even introspective evidence inferential? Cassam — even identifying your *feeling* requires interpretation ("When you identify your feeling as the yearning for another child what you are doing is **interpreting** it," 2015: 163); the process is **holistic** (drawing on recent mental life/behavior).
- **Differences from other-knowledge retained:** in our own case we have **introspective evidence** (about experiences); for others only **behavior** (incl. verbal).
- **Critics:** it neglects the **profound asymmetry** — rationality guarantees **non-inferential** access to our attitudes (Shoemaker 1994); or self-interpretation implies we are **"alienated"** from our attitudes (**Moran 2001**). [→ 3.6, 3.7]

### 3.4 EMPIRICIST TRANSPARENCY accounts
- **Root idea (Evans, see 1.2):** answer "Do you believe p?" by **considering whether p** — "look through" the transparent state to what it represents. **This subsection = versions taking Evans' procedure to generate *empirical* justification/warrant** (Dretske 1994; Fernández 2003, 2013; Byrne 2005, 2018). (Rationalist/agentialist uses of transparency → 3.6/3.7.)
- **Dretske (1994: 264):** self-knowledge is "a form of **perceptual knowledge** … obtained … by awareness of **non-mental objects**." I know I'm thinking it's snowing only by **inference from awareness of the snow.** Secures **first-person privilege** (my awareness of snow supports no inference about *another's* mind). Requires **background beliefs** to license the inference. **Worry (Aydede 2003):** unclear those background beliefs are available/justified.
- **Fernández — the "bypass model" (2013):** **non-inferential.** A *single state* grounds both the belief that p and the belief that one believes p. Seeing snow → belief it's snowing; that visual experience *also* grounds "I believe it's snowing" — **bypassing the first-order belief itself.** "**Partly internalist**" (I have access to the basis — seeming to see snow) + "**partly externalist**" (warranted because that perceptual state correlates with the belief). First-personal (only I can base a belief on *my* perceptual state); more secure than others' knowledge of my states; **non-inferential.**
- **Byrne — the "doxastic schema" (2005, 2018; schema from Gallois 1996):** **explicitly inferentialist.**
  - Schema: **p ∴ I believe that p.**
  - Argument: to follow the schema is to infer "I believe p" from premise **p**; you don't infer from a premise unless you **believe** it; so such reasoning yields **true** self-ascriptions → the schema is **self-verifying** [fn. 5: earlier Byrne — rule "If p, believe that you believe that p"]. Self-verifying *regardless* of whether p is justified.
  - Two supporting claims: (1) it's independently plausible we **do** use the schema (Evans); (2) the hypothesis **best explains** self-knowledge's security + asymmetry. **Security = externalist warrant:** self-ascriptions are **"safe"** (couldn't easily have been false) [fn. 6: safety blocks the Gettier-style objection that inference on a false premise can't yield knowledge]. **Asymmetry:** the schema won't yield knowledge of *others* (p ∴ *they* believe p is unreliable).
- **Objections to transparency accounts:**
  - **Shah & Velleman (2005):** answering "Do you believe p?" by considering p may **produce a new belief** rather than reveal the pre-existing one (considering geopolitics may *create* a war-belief; cf. Wilson & Kraft 1993). Can't fix by stipulating "draw no new conclusion" (that would require already knowing your beliefs). → Exposes a **limitation:** these accounts can't distinguish **newly-formed judgments** from **prior dispositional beliefs**, so can't explain knowing what one believes *at the time the question is posed* (Gertler 2011b).
  - **Boyle (2011) vs Byrne:** even if the p → "I believe p" inference is reliable/safe, it **won't seem reasonable to the thinker** (in general p doesn't imply *I believe p*). Fernández replies bypass-beliefs *do* seem reasonable. Others: the transition **isn't a genuine inference** — **Valaris (2011)** (can't be used in **hypothetical** reasoning from un-believed premises); **Barnett (2016)** (violates evidentialist restrictions on inference).
  - **Scope skepticism:** Fernández limits to beliefs & desires; Byrne extends to **all** states. Critics doubt extension to desires (Ashwell 2013a,b), thoughts/intentions (Samoilova 2016), or beliefs held with **<100% confidence** (Tang 2017).

### 3.5 REASONS accounts
- **Motivation:** if attitudes are constituted by dispositions (linked to counterfactuals knowable only by inference), self-knowledge of attitudes looks **inferential.** The reasons account (first: **Peacocke 1999**) says we can sometimes know beliefs **without inference.**
- **Core mechanism:** beliefs are **dispositional**, but **judgments are occurrent** (a judgment that p is an *event*). **Judging** it's snowing gives you a **reason** to think you have the dispositions of the belief (e.g., to put on boots) → a reason to believe *that you believe* it's snowing.
- **Crucial subtlety:** the judgment can serve as a reason **so long as it is conscious** — you **need not be aware of** the judgment. **Analogy:** an **itch** can be a reason for scratching without any *further* conscious state constituting awareness of the itch. So a conscious judgment that p directly rationalizes self-ascribing the belief that p — **without introspecting the judgment** (cf. Silins 2012).
- **Why the transition is rational:** judging is **conceptually linked** to believing — making a judgment "is the **fundamental way to form a belief**" (Peacocke 1999: 238). Similarly conscious **memory** that p (remembering↔believing) can justify believing one believes p. Anyone able to self-ascribe a belief **possesses the concept of belief** → grasps these conceptual truths [fn. 7: the account also captures the agentialist intuition that self-ascription = **endorsement/commitment**, not mere report].
- **Extensions/arguments:**
  - **Paul (2012):** reasons account for **intentions** — deciding (or remembering one decided) → believing one **intends**, since *deciding normally suffices for intending* (a conceptual truth).
  - **Silins (2020):** **Moore-paradoxical** judgments ("it's snowing but I don't believe it is") are plainly **irrational**; they'd be rational if judging p gave no justification for self-ascribing belief that p; so judging p **does** provide (propositional) justification → explains the irrationality ("you flout the justification given to you by your judgment that p," 2020: 334).
- **Objection (Coliva 2008):** judgments aren't suitably **accessible** to rationalize self-ascriptions; the **phenomenology** of a conscious thought ("things will look up") doesn't reveal whether it's a **judgment** or a **wish.** **McHugh (2012)** replies there is "a **phenomenology characteristic of judging that p**" present where ordinary self-knowledge is possible. → Prospects may depend on whether attitudes have **"proprietary phenomenology"** (Pitt 2004).

### 3.6 RATIONALIST accounts
- **Thesis:** our status as **rational thinkers guarantees** our capacity for self-knowledge. ("Rationalist" reserved for accounts grounded in **rationality**, *independent of agency claims* — contrast 3.7.)
- **Shoemaker (1994) — no rational thinker is self-blind.** A self-blind person would (i) make irrational **Moore-paradoxical** judgments; (ii) be **unable to communicate** beliefs / cooperate; (iii) regard themselves **"as a stranger"** (observe themselves taking aspirin without grasping they're in pain). A rational thinker wouldn't → rational thinkers **cannot** be self-blind.
  - **Constitutivism** [fn. 8: a different sense than "self-attribution constitutes being in the state"]: for rational thinkers with the right concepts, **being in certain states constitutes knowing one is in them.** For a rational creature, feeling pain *partly consists in* believing it's in pain; believing p *partly consists in* believing it believes p; etc. Cashed out via a **functionalist** analysis — in rational creatures, a state's causal role also suffices for the belief that one is in it (1994: 287–9).
- **Stoljar (2018, 2019):** an "**introspective principle**" partly constitutive of rationality: "If one is in a conscious state C, … one will believe that one is in C" (2019: 405). If rationality means being guided by rationality's rules incl. this, **no rational creature is self-blind.** Also explains **Evans:** a belief is "conscious" when one **attends to its content**; so a rational person who believes p and attends to p will believe she believes p.
- **Smithies (2019):** **Moore-paradoxical beliefs are always irrational** → a rational thinker **knows all** their beliefs ("Rationality requires knowing what you believe, since otherwise you're liable to fall into an irrational Moorean predicament," 174). Needs a **nonstandard belief**-conception: "to believe that p is to be **disposed to judge that p when you consciously entertain whether p**" (175–6) → beliefs necessarily accessible.
- **Two challenges to rationalism:** (1) the conception of rationality must be **demanding** enough to exclude self-blindness — but then may **not apply to flawed creatures like us** (Kornblith 2012; Cassam 2015); (2) to show we *meet* the standard, rationalists invoke **nonstandard "conscious"/"belief"** — must show the process is **psychologically realistic** for the states we actually have. (The shared intuition "no rational thinker is self-blind" is widely held; success hinges on finding conceptions robust yet realistic.)

### 3.7 AGENTIALIST accounts
- **Turn from the epistemic:** denies the special character of self-ascriptions is *primarily epistemic.* Charge: standard accounts portray the introspective thinker as **passive** — "a mere **spectator** (or detector) of a cognitive show." **Agentialism:** the special character stems from our **agency over** our mental states (we're **responsible** for them).
  - "The privilege of first-person knowledge is … more like the knowledge of a person **driving a car** as opposed to … her **passenger**" (**McGeer 1996: 505**).
  - "asymmetries of **responsibility and commitment**" more than capacities/access (**Moran 2001: 64**).
- **Kantian core (Burge 1996; Moran 2001; Bilgrami 2006; Boyle 2009):** the basic self-conception (as an "I") is **agential** — *authors* of our beliefs/intentions, not an inert thing in which attitudes occur. This authorial agency is **rational** (believing/intending on the basis of reasons). Differs from rationalism: capacity derives from **agency**, not (just) rationality.
- **Variants (precise link between agency and self-knowledge):**
  - **Burge — entitlement (transcendental, "ought implies can"):** rational agency obliges us to satisfy rational norms (beliefs fit evidence; sets be consistent). To do this we must rely on **judgments about which beliefs we have** → responsibility **epistemically entitles** us to those judgments. "[I]f one lacked entitlement to judgments about one's attitudes, one could not be subject to rational norms governing how one ought to alter those attitudes …" (1996: 101) [fn. 9: Burgean **entitlements** are warrants that need not be accessible to the subject + carry reliability].
  - **Moran — transparency via responsibility (transcendental):** we couldn't think rationally without the **"epistemic right"** to assume our attitudes are **shaped by our reasons** ("something like a Transcendental assumption of Rational Thought," 2003: 406). That right licenses the **transparency method** (answer "do you believe p?" by considering reasons bearing on p) → its self-ascriptions are **knowledge.**
    - **Key contrast w/ 3.4:** for **empiricists**, transparency works because it's **reliable / safe**; for **agentialists (Moran)**, our entitlement to it is rooted in a **normative fact** — we are **responsible** for our attitudes.
    - **O'Brien objection:** Moran is too demanding — I'm justified in self-attributing the war-belief only if I **recognize** that my reasons for expecting war are *evidence that I believe it*, requiring a reflective grasp of deliberation **unnecessary** for ordinary self-attribution (2003: 379–81).
    - **Supplements:** **Keeling (2018)** — confabulation (Nisbett & Wilson 1977) shows we take ourselves *obligated* to know our attitudes' rational basis; **Marcus & Schwenkler (2019)** — the **honesty norm** for assertion requires non-empirical knowledge of what we believe (transparency-type).
  - **Bilgrami (2006) — commitments:** we **couldn't exercise the agency in believing** unless we **actually knew** our beliefs. "We cannot … have commitments without believing that we have them … the very condition for having a commitment presupposes … a second-order belief that one has that commitment" (2006: 287).
  - **Boyle (2009, 2019) — in-built (non-higher-order) awareness:** deliberating whether p requires awareness of *how* p is represented (settled vs hypothesis) → a rational thinker who consciously believes p has a **tacit** awareness of that belief — **not** a separate higher-order state: "being aware of her own belief and **seeing the world from the perspective of a p-believer are two aspects of the same awareness**" (2019: 1018).
  - **McGeer (1996) — empiricist agentialism (no rational-agency faculty):** self-ascriptions are **"commissive"** — in self-ascribing, we **commit** to behaving accordingly. "[W]e do not just wait to see if our actions make sense … but rather **make them make sense**" (507). Normativity from **ordinary practical obligations**, not rationality → **scope extends beyond** reasons-sensitive attitudes (e.g., "I want to go to the beach"). *(McGeer is the recurring exception to agentialism's limits.)*
  - **Coliva (2012a) — performative/constitutive:** for a rational thinker with the concepts, self-ascriptions of **commitments** (beliefs, intentions, *and* rationally-held desires) are true because they **create** the attitude ascribed — like a **performative** ("I promise…"). Judging "I believe/desire/… that P" = an alternative way of undertaking the same commitment as judging P. Agency = power to **undertake commitments through self-ascription** (vs Kantian agency *in having* the attitude); retains a **rationalist element** (committing to there being good reasons).
- **Prospects / challenges for agentialism:**
  - **The epistemic burden:** most knowledge involves the belief being **controlled by what is known** (my dog's-location belief tracks his location); agentialism focuses on our **control over what is known** → not a genuine competitor **unless** it explains how agency *provides* self-knowledge. (Parrott 2015's non-epistemic version is *explicitly not* a competitor — it explains why deferring to others' self-ascriptions is reasonable.)
  - **Limited application:** most versions cover only **reasons-sensitive** attitudes (McGeer excepted). But **non-instrumental desires** and some attitudes are **reasons-insensitive** (entrenched superstitions, wishful/comforting beliefs, **akratic/self-sabotaging intentions** that survive deliberation) — part of the motivation for self-interpretation (3.3).
  - **The alienation claim:** agentialists say knowing a recalcitrant attitude only by **self-observation** makes me **"alienated"** (Moran 2001) — relation "brute, contingent, non-rational" (Burge 1996), "third-person perspective" (Bilgrami 2006), not "as my own" (Boyle 2009) [fn. 10: agentialism splits attitudes into **avowable/reasons-sensitive** vs **reasons-insensitive**; opposes both purely-epistemic accounts and **Humeanism** about attitudes]. **Critics (Reed 2010; Borgoni 2015):** observational knowledge needn't preclude ownership; a **detached** perspective can be **enlightening** — help combat recalcitrant attitudes (McGeer 2008; Levy 2016) and be necessary for genuine self-understanding (Doyle 2019).

### 3.8 EXPRESSIVIST accounts
- **Core:** utterances like "I'm in pain" resemble **direct expressions** of states (wincing, "ouch!") (Wittgenstein 1953; Finkelstein 2003; Bar-On 2004; Campbell 2020). Such utterances = **avowals**, which **directly express** the states they ascribe.
- **Simple Expressivism** (Bar-On's label; ~Wittgenstein): avowals, like wincing, are **neither true nor false** → express **no knowledge.** *(Most contemporary expressivists reject this; the entry focuses on those who take avowals to express self-knowledge.)*
- **Two senses of directness** (avowal↔state): (1) **no intervening judgment** ("I'm in pain") between the state and the avowal (as a wince flows from pain); (2) the knowledge is **epistemically "groundless"** — not based on **evidence** that one is in the state. (One can directly express only **one's own** states.)
- **Division of labor:**
  - **Finkelstein & Bar-On** focus on **first-person authority** (social-linguistic: we defer; avowals presumed true).
  - **Campbell** focuses on **first-person privilege** (the epistemic position).
- **Finkelstein (2003):** my avowal "I'm so happy!" may be **caused** by my happiness, but **not by brute causation** (vs a hammer-tap making a leg kick); happiness + avowal "have … a particular kind of **intelligibility**" (126) — they make sense together in "**the logical space of animate life**" (like a dog's pain and its moaning). Whether this is **knowledge** depends on whether "knowledge" requires **evidential grounding** (if so, avowals don't express it).
- **Bar-On (2004) — Neo-Expressivism:** avowals enjoy a **presumption of truth** (it'd be inappropriate to question whether *I'm* the one happy, or whether what I feel is happiness), explained by the avowal flowing **directly** from the feeling (no evidence, no mediating judgment). Avowals can be "**genuine and privileged self-knowledge**" (405) though the subject hasn't "formed the active judgment … on some basis" and can't offer evidence. One compatible approach: a conscious state warrants a self-ascription **directly** — "their pronouncements, when true, are **epistemically grounded in the very states they ascribe** … which states also serve as the **reasons** for their acts of avowing" (405). ("Epistemically grounded" = *warranted*, not evidence-based.)
- **Critic worry:** the **spontaneous, reflexive** quality (no evidence/reasoning) means avowals **can't be the kind of epistemic achievement knowledge requires.**
- **Campbell (2020):** uses the expressivist core (only the subject can directly express her states) to ground **first-person privilege** — and takes the **epistemic** dimension to be the **basis** for the **social-linguistic** one (reversing Finkelstein/Bar-On). An avowal "I'm in pain" expresses **knowledge** that I'm in pain (15), via her **Rational Response** account of knowledge: knowing p = "being able to **rationally respond to, or operate with, the fact that p**" (12). To know I want water = to have rational capacities to act on / express / reason with the desire (pour water; reroute to a fountain; say "I want water"). The utterance expresses self-knowledge iff it's a **direct, rational response** to the desire (direct = no intermediate judgment / no evidence). **Rationality** distinguishes it from a **brute** reaction (a dog going to its water bowl). **Open problem:** to *explain* rather than *presuppose* first-person privilege, "rational response" must **not** be analyzed in **epistemic** terms — flagged as fruitful future work.

## 4. COMPARISON MATRIX (the eight accounts at a glance)

| Account | Achieved by | Inferential? | Internalist / Externalist | Primary scope | Security source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Acquaintance** | direct acquaintance / attention (demonstrative phenomenal concepts) | No | Internalist | occurrent/phenomenal states (esp. sensations) | metaphysical+epistemic directness; possible luminosity |
| **Inner Sense** | internal monitoring/scanning mechanism | Non-inferential but causal | Externalist (reliabilist) | potentially all states | reliability of the mechanism |
| **Self-Interpretation** | interpreting one's thoughts/feelings/behavior | **Yes** (inferential) | Carruthers externalist / Lawlor internalist | beliefs, desires, emotions | best-explanation + (Carruthers) reliability |
| **Empiricist Transparency** | look outward at the world (p → "I believe p") | Dretske/Byrne yes; Fernández no (bypass) | mixed ("partly" both) | beliefs (Byrne: all) | reliability / **safety** (externalist warrant) |
| **Reasons** | a conscious **judgment** rationalizes the self-ascription | Non-inferential (rational transition) | Internalist-leaning | beliefs (Paul: intentions) | conceptual link judging↔believing |
| **Rationalist** | rationality constitutively guarantees it | — (constitutive) | Internalist-leaning | all states (for rational thinkers) | constitution (being in state ⇒ believing one is) |
| **Agentialist** | **agency/responsibility** over attitudes | Moran: transparency (non-empirical) | normative (non-reliabilist) | reasons-sensitive attitudes (McGeer: broader) | normative entitlement / commitment |
| **Expressivist** | avowal directly **expresses** the state | No | groundless/warrant-based | sensations + attitudes (avowals) | directness; (Campbell) rational-response |

**Two orthogonal "transparency"-using camps to keep distinct:** **Empiricist transparency (3.4)** = transparency works because *reliable/safe*; **Agentialist transparency (Moran, 3.7)** = transparency licensed by *normative responsibility*. Both descend from the **same Evans procedure** (1.2).

## 5. CORE DISTINCTIONS & TERMS (glossary)

- **Privileged access** = the (1)+(2) idea: special epistemic security and/or an exclusively first-personal method.
- **Infallibility** (can't be wrong) vs **Omniscience / self-intimation** (being in a state ⇒ knowing it).
- **First-person privilege** (epistemic) vs **first-person authority** (social-linguistic, presumption of truth).
- **Epistemic vs metaphysical directness** (Acquaintance's two senses).
- **Introspection (inward) vs transparency method (outward / "looking through")** — the two pictures of the "special method."
- **Relationally-individuated attitudes** → **Boghossian's trilemma** (inner observation / inference / nothing).
- **Self-blindness** (Shoemaker) — the (alleged) impossibility a rational creature can't know its own states; **de dicto vs de re** versions of "no rational creature is self-blind."
- **Doxastic schema** (p ∴ I believe p; self-verifying) — Byrne; **bypass model** — Fernández; **safety** — couldn't easily be false.
- **Moore-paradoxical judgment/belief** ("p, but I don't believe p") — engine of Reasons/Rationalist arguments.
- **Avowal** — a spontaneous first-personal expression of a state (verbal or in thought) that directly expresses it.
- **Constitutivism** (Shoemaker sense) — being in a state (for rational thinkers) constitutes knowing one is in it; [fn. 8] *other* sense = self-attribution constitutes the state.
- **Commissive / performative self-ascription** (McGeer; Coliva) — self-ascription that *commits to* or *creates* the attitude.
- **Alienation** (Moran et al.) — the agentialist charge against purely observational self-knowledge.
- **The problem of calibration** (Goldman) — fixing introspection's reliability without circularity.

## 7. THE TEN FOOTNOTES (digest)

1. **Locke** ("impossible … to perceive, without perceiving that he does perceive") more likely expresses the **higher-order theory of consciousness** than a universal self-knowledge thesis.
2. One reaction to the **observation-alters-target** worry: construe privilege as purely about *epistemic status* of self-attributions (they can be secure even if the process changes the object). **Fridge-light analogy:** opening the door lets me know the light is on *now*, even if opening it *turns it on*. This **denies** that the special thing is **access** (access implies a way of grasping a fact *prior* to grasping it). [Discussed in 3.5.]
3. **Content externalism:** attitude contents individuated by relations to environment; implications for self-knowledge are disputed (see SEP entry on externalism and self-knowledge). Largely set aside here.
4. **Inner-sense theorists** often accept the **higher-order perception (HOP)** theory of consciousness (conscious states = those one has higher-order quasi-perceptual awareness of); combining the two addresses the problem of consciousness.
5. Earlier **Byrne**: the doxastic schema as the rule "**If p, believe that you believe that p**."
6. **Safety** blocks the objection that the doxastic schema can't yield knowledge when premise **p is false** (cf. Gettier diagnosis that inference on a false premise can't yield knowledge); safety gives an alternative Gettier diagnosis.
7. **Reasons account ↔ agentialist intuition:** self-attributing a belief is not "a mere report" but "involves the same kind of **endorsement and commitment** as would be made in entering the first-order … state itself" (Peacocke 1999: 235).
8. **"Constitutivism"** is sometimes used differently — for the thesis that **self-attribution of a state constitutes being in that state.**
9. **Burgean entitlement:** "epistemic rights or warrants that need not be understood by or even accessible to the subject" (Burge 1993: 458) + a **reliability** component (entitled judgments are "well-positioned to achieve … truth in normal circumstances," 507).
10. **Agentialism** rests on a basic division: **avowable, reasons-sensitive** vs **reasons-insensitive** attitudes — opposing both purely epistemic accounts and **Humeanism** about the attitudes.
