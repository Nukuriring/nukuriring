# Lecture 3 — *The Conversation of Justice* (pp. 101–126): Reader's Map

> Rawls and the Drama of Consent. The book's culmination — where perfectionism, skepticism, and democracy meet. A spine to read *against* the text.

## The one sentence

Democratic life rests on **consent**, but there is a moment in moral and political life where justification *runs out* — where the violated raise a cry of outrage that no rule or argument can settle. At that point you cannot say "I am right"; you can only **show** something — your consent, your being compromised by it, your commitment to change. **Moral Perfectionism is the dimension of moral life that lives at exactly this point**, and far from being undemocratic (Rawls's worry), it is *the condition of democratic morality.*

## The fight that frames everything

Back to **Rawls** — but deeper than Lecture 1's §50 quarrel. Cavell finds threaded through *A Theory of Justice* something he calls **"the conversation of justice"**: under just institutions we "can say to one another" that we cooperate on fair terms (p. 13). That mutual address presupposes **consent** (the social-contract tradition). Cavell's pressure: Rawls's full utopia **must give a place to Perfectionism** that Rawls seems to leave no room for — specifically at the point where the conversation of justice *stalls.*

## The spine, in eight moves

1. **Consent can't be proportioned (p. 107–108).** Rawls suggests we consent to the *principles*, not society as such, knowing any real society falls short of ideal justice. Cavell's intuition: **consent is not modifiable or proportionable.** "I cannot keep consent focused on the successes or graces of society; it reaches into every corner of society's failure or ugliness." You can't 80%-consent to an 80%-just society. *"Psychological exile is not exile."* Consent is "neither unrestricted nor restricted" — its content **is** the ongoing conversation of justice.

2. **The cry that has no hearing (p. 108).** Rawls: "those who express resentment must be prepared to show why certain institutions are unjust." But the **least advantaged** may be silent (demoralized), and "it may be part of the resentment that there is no satisfactory hearing for it." So the conversation of justice can break down precisely where it's most needed.

3. **Marriage as conversation — the load-bearing analogy (p. 103–105).** Cavell brings in his film work: **comedies of remarriage** (*It Happened One Night, Bringing Up Baby, The Philadelphia Story, The Lady Eve, Adam's Rib*…) and **melodramas of the unknown woman** (*Letter from an Unknown Woman, Gaslight, Stella Dallas*). From **Milton's** divorce tract: marriage = **"a meet and happy conversation."** What constitutes marriage isn't outside it (church, children) but the **willingness for it**, the readiness to repeat the acknowledgment — "all genuine marriage is **remarriage**." And Milton makes the **marriage contract an analogy of the social contract** — so marriage becomes an *emblem of society*, and the willingness-to-converse becomes the model of *consent to democracy.*

4. **Nora as the central case (p. 108–113).** *A Doll's House.* Nora is the "least advantaged" who finds her voice — her disadvantage isn't material but that she has "no inclinations of her own" (Mill's conformist), has lived "**without a voice in her history**." She is **violated** ("Every word they say chagrins us" — Emerson). When she tries to "show why the institution is unjust," Torvald says she's "talking like a child," "out of her senses." She has **no acceptable reasons** — she knows all the reasons that can be given her. *The point:* here justification runs out; right can't be asserted; **something must be shown.** Her leaving is a showing — the "miracle of change" ("we shall all be changed," 1 Cor. 15). She is **representative beyond herself**, beyond women.

5. **The asymmetry vs. the scene of instruction — the bridge to Lecture 2 (p. 111–112).** In the scene of instruction the one *out of authority* is the **pupil/newcomer**, and the teacher says "This is simply what I do" and waits. In **moral encounter** the exhaustion of justification is reached **first by the victim** (Nora). So the alternative to "I am right" can't be "this is what I do, wait" — that just *reiterates* right. The real alternative: to find myself **dissatisfied with what I do, with what I consent to.** As society's representative, when reasons run out, I'm in **impersonal shame** — and if I keep consenting (as is likely), what must be **shown** is that *my consent compromises me*, that I know change is called for "beginning with myself," yet I consent without reason, on the bare intuition that our distance from justice is "still habitable, even necessary as a stage for continued change."

6. **Against "above reproach" — morality isn't a rulebook (p. 113–115).** Rawls: "A rational individual is always to act so that he need never blame himself." Cavell reads this as *the rejection of perfectionism.* Reprising his old critique of Rawls's "Two Concepts of Rules": in **games** a rule (three strikes) settles things; in **morality** finality is carried not by a rule but by a **judgment** — "one that may be competently opposed." Promising is **not an institution** like baseball ("no more an institution than warning or cursing or beseeching or forgiving"). Torvald treated marriage as a rulebook — and "the whole of a society… looks like a **doll house, or marionette theater**." Moral life is judgment, responsiveness, conversation — not rule-application.

7. **The Marquise of O—— — the limit case (p. 115–124).** Kleist's tale / Rohmer's film. A woman, raped in her sleep by the Count who "rescued" her, is mysteriously pregnant, publishes an **open newspaper appeal** to the unknown father, rejects then finally **forgives and marries** him (twice). A *real, vile* wrong — yet she forgives. **How?** The forgiveness rests on nothing in the man himself; it is "a forgiveness for **being human**, a forgiveness of the human race." Each is the other's **rejected self.** Punchline: **"Moral Perfectionism [is] the rescue from a *false* perfectionism"** — false autonomy, the aspiration to *transcend* the human (purity, the angel). Her worst moments came from perfectionism-as-transcendence (wanting the angel, purity); her best from accepting its **domestication** as "the mark of the human." *Pascal*: "Man is neither angel nor beast, and the mischief is that he who would play the angel plays the beast."

8. **Recovery from skepticism through writing (p. 119–124).** *The Winter's Tale* = skepticism as inability to count, denial of one's child (the man doubts). In *Marquise* the **woman** doubts — the father's identity. **Kleist** himself was "shattered" by Kant (the 1801 letter: we can't decide whether truth is truth or only appears so → "despair of the truth"). His story allegorizes **recovering from skepticism through *public writing*** — not a private whisper (when the Count tries to whisper, she recoils as from fresh violation: "a man's omniscience is at the price of stealing the woman's separate existence"). Recovery = the world returned as **acknowledgment**, not knowledge — but the return is **uncanny, disappointing**, not the restoration of a former presence.

## The synthesis — what unifies all three lectures (p. 123–125)

**Disappointment** is the hinge. It appeared in *both* registers: our relation to the **criteria** of ordinary words (Lecture 2 — skepticism) *and* our relation to **democratic existence** (Lecture 3 — conformity). The deep parallel: in both we "give over the little crossroads of perspective and freedom at our disposal" —

> in **morality** through **conformity** (stripping ourselves of our *partiality*);
> in **epistemology** through perverseness (stripping ourselves of our shared *criteria*, opting for false totalities/theories).

So **conformity : morality :: skepticism : epistemology** — both are flights from finitude and partiality (the angel/beast temptation). That is the unity of the book.

**The landing — "consent from above" (p. 124–126).** The favored find their lives "without justification" (they'd be ashamed to tell the less advantaged that society deserves consent *from below*). So what they **show** is **consent from above** — an acknowledgment of being *compromised* by democracy's failures, persisting in consent to a "shameful condition" by living as **an example of human partiality**, "open to the further self," "holding oneself in knowledge of the need for change," "living in promise, as a sign, a representative human." This is **"not a particular moral demand, but the condition of democratic morality."** Conformity is "not a mere lack of community, but its **parody**." *"The price of liberty is our subjection to eternal vigilance."* The scholar cheers us to withstand it; "books are for the going and coming of the dark times." Closing: Emerson, accused of being "impervious to the evidence of evil" — "Could Emerson have made his ambition clearer? To whom?"

## The whole arc of the book

- **Lecture 1** — *what* aversive thinking / perfectionism is (attained → unattained self); defended vs. Rawls §50.
- **Lecture 2** — its **epistemological** face: skepticism, criteria, the ordinary; vs. Kripke.
- **Lecture 3** — its **political-moral** face: consent, democracy, the conversation of justice; vs. Rawls again — via marriage, film, Nora, the Marquise.

One thread: the **human as the partial, finite, mixed being** who must find a voice and give consent — refusing both **conformity** (the beast, ceding voice) and **skepticism/transcendence** (the angel, despising finitude) — and keep **turning toward the unattained self**, which is also a turning toward others and toward the democratic community.

## Glossary — terms doing special work

| Term | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **conversation of justice** | Cavell's name for the mutual address/consent threaded through Rawls — and the point where it *stalls* (justification exhausted) |
| **consent (from above / from below)** | acceptance of society; *from below* = the disadvantaged's; *from above* = the favored's acknowledgment of being compromised, shown not argued |
| **conversation (marriage as)** | from Milton: marriage = "a meet and happy conversation"; the willingness to repeat acknowledgment; emblem of the social contract |
| **remarriage** | the genre's claim that what constitutes a bond is the *renewed willingness* for it — "all genuine marriage is remarriage" |
| **showing (vs. saying / proving)** | when justification runs out, the moral response is to *show* (one's consent, compromise, change), not assert right |
| **false perfectionism** | the aspiration to *transcend* the human (purity, autonomy, the angel) — what true perfectionism *rescues us from* |
| **disappointment** | the shared root of skepticism (with criteria) and conformity (with democracy) — the flight from partiality/finitude |
| **partiality** | (from Lecture 1) being *not whole* and *biased/invested* — the mark of the human individual democracy must preserve |

## Which citations carry the argument vs. resonate

- **Load-bearing:** Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (pp. 3, 13, 246, 250, 422, 533 — consent, least-advantaged, "above reproach") and "Two Concepts of Rules" (the games/morality analogy); Milton's divorce tract (marriage = conversation); Ibsen, *A Doll's House* (Nora); Kleist/Rohmer, *The Marquise of O——* (the limit case); Cavell's own film books (*Pursuits of Happiness*, the remarriage/melodrama genres) and *The Claim of Reason*.
- **Structural / thematic:** Shakespeare, *The Winter's Tale* (skepticism); Pascal (angel/beast); Emerson, "The American Scholar" / "Self-Reliance" (the through-line); Marx (criticism of religion); Freud / Lévi-Strauss (incest & the social contract).
- **Resonance / aside (don't get stuck):** Bloom & Updike (the "fascism" charge — answered in Appendix A "Hope against Hope"); Shaftesbury/Rousseau on sympathy; Annette Baier on promising.

## Reading tips

- The big shock here is **genre**: the lecture is largely **film and drama criticism** (Hollywood comedies, Ibsen, Kleist), not Wittgenstein. It *is* the same argument — hold the **Milton analogy** (marriage = conversation = social contract = consent) and the set-pieces stop looking like digressions.
- **Nora** is the cleanest entry. If you grasp why her leaving is a *showing* (not a winning argument), the whole "conversation of justice runs out" thesis lands.
- The **Marquise** is the hardest stretch and the OCR is degraded around pp. 106–122; read it from the PDF. Its job: to push "forgiveness when justification is impossible" to its limit and reveal perfectionism as *rescue from false (transcendent) perfectionism.*
- Read the **disappointment** synthesis (pp. 123–125) slowly — it's where Cavell finally ties skepticism (Lecture 2) and conformity (Lecture 3) into one knot.
