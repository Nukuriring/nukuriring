# Lecture 1 — *Aversive Thinking* (pp. 33–63): Reader's Map

> A spine to read *against* the text. Cavell never states his thesis once; this does.

## The one sentence

For Emerson, the moral life does not begin with rules (*what is right? what maximizes good?*) but with a prior relation: your dissatisfaction with the self you've become (**attained self**) and your aspiration toward a further, better self that is still *yours* (**unattained but attainable self**). To turn from the first toward the second is "thinking." This is **perfectionism** — not "being perfect," but endless self-perfecting — and Cavell's whole lecture is a rescue of it from Rawls's dismissal.

## The fight that frames everything

Cavell admires Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, the central moral philosophy of the era) **and** Emerson. But Rawls, in **§50**, dismissed "perfectionism" as elitist and undemocratic: a doctrine that society should be arranged to maximize the excellence of a few great men, the rest living for them. If that reading stands, Emerson is thrown out of serious philosophy. Cavell cannot accept that, so the lecture is one long demonstration that **Rawls misread what perfectionism is.** The Introduction set this up; Lecture 1 answers it.

## The spine, in six moves

1. **What "thinking" means for Emerson (p. 35–37).** Not reasoning / problem-solving (analytic philosophy's picture) but **transfiguration = conversion = aversion** — three names for one process of *turning the self*. Thinking is moral from the start, not a separate faculty.

2. **Why "aversive" (p. 37).** "Self-reliance is [conformity's] aversion." Society constantly demands your *consent / conformity* ("every word they say chagrins us"); thinking is the perpetual **turning-away-from and re-figuring of** that demand — which also makes Emerson's own writing something society recoils from.

3. **The unhandsome condition (p. 38–39).** Objects "slip through our fingers when we clutch hardest." Our **grasping** (conceptualizing, pinning the world down) is the *un-hand-some* part of our condition — Western thought as sublimated violence (Heidegger's *greifen* = clutching). The cure is **reception** — receiving rather than seizing (thinking as *thanking*, taking a gift).

4. **The hinge — why the two halves are one lecture (p. 46).** "A certain relation to words... is inseparable from a certain moral-like relation to thinking." Caring this heavily about *each word* (Austin, Wittgenstein, ordinary language) is an **allegory of caring about your life** — making yourself intelligible, owning it. So the *thinking* question and the *moral* question are the same question.

5. **Perfectionism rescued (p. 48–56) — three moves:**
   - *Not maximizing.* Emerson and Nietzsche **despise** official high culture (universities, institutions). You can't be asking to "maximize the distribution" of a culture you scorn — the culture perfectionism wants **doesn't exist yet**, so there is nothing to maximize. Not teleological.
   - *The crux — Exemplare ≠ specimens (p. 50–51).* Rawls reads Nietzsche's **Exemplare** (exemplars) as **"specimens"** — biological samples of a class, valued independent of you. Wrong. An **exemplar is something you delegate to: it stands for your own further self.** "For each self there is a genius," NOT "there is a genius all selves live for." Nietzsche writing on Schopenhauer isn't living *for* Schopenhauer (who is barely in the book) — he uses him as a **sign pointing to his own unattained self.** So perfectionism = self-transformation, not service to an elite.
   - *So it's democratic — even essential (p. 56).* The disdain for mediocre official culture is itself an expression of commitment to democracy ("only within the possibility of democracy is one committed to living with, or against, such culture"). Perfectionism supplies democracy's **internal measure** — the standard by which we register its failures and keep its hope alive. **Training to withstand democracy's failures, not its rigors.**

6. **The mechanism — how a text transforms you (p. 57–61).** "In every work of genius we recognize our own rejected thoughts; they come back to us with a certain **alienated majesty**." Emerson's text occupies the position of your *rejected / further* self, your "beyond." Reading it, you recognize what you'd repressed (Freud's **uncanny** — the familiar invaded by another familiar). To credit those thoughts to *Emerson* instead of reclaiming them as *yours* = **idolatry**. The exemplar is also your **enemy** (it contests your present self) — hence the role of the **friend** ("another myself"). This is why the writing *must* be hard and dare you to read it: it can't hand you conclusions (yours you don't need; not-yours won't help) — it must **provoke** you to find your own.

## The landing (pp. 57–63, where your OCR is worst)

Why does Emerson keep using Kant's and Descartes' terms instead of just ditching philosophy? Because the "**domestication of culture**" he calls for — finding a *home for humanity in the everyday / ordinary* — is itself a philosophical task. The deepest tie: our dispossession, loss of world, loss of ground, is what modern philosophy calls **skepticism**. Aversive thinking is a way to *live with* skepticism rather than refute it — turning it into the "**argument of the ordinary**," shared with **Wittgenstein** (the everyday) and **Heidegger**. The **Mill** ending makes it respectable to analytic ears: even Mill asks whether you actually *desire* the conformist, censored condition of mankind. That is perfectionism's question — and until each person answers *in their own voice*, we don't know what we're consenting to.

## Glossary — words Cavell bends out of their normal sense

| Word | Ordinary sense | Cavell/Emerson's sense |
|---|---|---|
| **thinking** | reasoning, problem-solving | turning the self; transfiguration/conversion/aversion |
| **aversion** | mere dislike | active, continual turning-away-from conformity |
| **partial** | incomplete | both "not whole" *and* "biased toward / favoring" — thinking is always invested |
| **condition** | circumstance | (from Kant) the *terms* on which we speak together; what must be derived |
| **unhandsome** | ugly | *un-hand-some*: the grasping/clutching in our nature |
| **representative** | typical sample | one who stands *for* your further self; delegation |
| **illustrious / illustrate** | famous / give an example | making the a priori (necessary, universal) *visible* in a life |
| **constitution** | a body / a charter | the make-up of his prose; (re)writing the nation |
| **standing** | posture | standing *for* humanity / standing *as* a standard/measure |
| **perfectionism** | striving to be flawless | the attained→unattained self relation; a dimension of *all* moral thinking |

## Which citations carry the argument vs. resonate

- **Load-bearing:** Rawls §50 (the antagonist); Nietzsche, *Schopenhauer as Educator* (the *Exemplare* crux); Kant (the terms being transfigured — condition, realm of ends, heteronomy); Mill, *On Liberty* (the respectable landing).
- **Structural parallel:** Heidegger, *What Is Called Thinking?* ("we are still not thinking" ‖ "the American Scholar does not exist").
- **Resonance / aside (don't get stuck):** Jaspers, Dewey, Marx, Plato, Aristotle, Freud, and the many "argued elsewhere in [my other book]" parentheticals.
