# Lecture 2 — *The Argument of the Ordinary* (pp. 64–100): Reader's Map

> Scenes of Instruction in Wittgenstein and in Kripke. A spine to read *against* the text.

## The one sentence

The whole lecture is a defense of Cavell's own reading of Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* against **Kripke's** famous reading. Where Kripke says the *Investigations* turns on a **skeptical paradox about rule-following** solved by **communal agreement**, Cavell answers: the book turns on **criteria** and **the ordinary**, skepticism is a permanent *voice* in it rather than a paradox to be solved, and Kripke's "solution" is *more skeptical than the problem it claims to solve.*

## The fight that frames everything

Kripke's *Wittgenstein: On Rules and Private Language* ("Kripkenstein") claims:
- The **rule** is fundamental to Wittgenstein (§201: "no course of action could be determined by a rule").
- There is **no fact about me** that fixes whether I mean *plus* or *quus* (a deviant function that agrees with my past answers but diverges now). So meaning faces a **skeptical paradox**.
- Wittgenstein's **"skeptical solution"**: the **community**. A teacher judges the child correct when the child does what the teacher *is inclined to do*; the community licenses or excludes the individual by matching inclinations.

Cavell admires this — it's the only reading besides his own that takes Wittgenstein to keep skepticism *internal* rather than refuting it. But "if Kripke is right, I am wrong," so the lecture must show, slowly, why it isn't right (not *false* — "nothing internal is wrong with it" — but *not true to the Investigations*).

## The spine, in seven moves

1. **Rules vs. criteria (p. 65–68).** Kripke makes **rules** fundamental and **subordinates criteria** — and *that* is exactly how he evades "the ordinary." For Cavell **criteria** are fundamental: rule, agreement, inclination, the ordinary are "more various and entangled" than Kripke allows. Wittgenstein himself (§201, next sentence) deflates the paradox: "there would be neither accord nor conflict here" — i.e. the paradox is named and *immediately tamed*; it isn't a serious "solution"-demanding problem.

2. **The recast — "inclined to say" ≠ "licensed" (p. 69–73).** Wittgenstein's **scene of instruction**, §217: "If I have exhausted the justifications I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned. Then I am **inclined to say**: 'This is simply what I do.'" Kripke effectively rewrites this as "I am **licensed** to say 'this is what I am **inclined to do**.'" Cavell's point: *what I am inclined to say is precisely not what I necessarily go on to say* — there's hesitation, consideration, patience. The good teacher saying "This is what I do" is **not** "do it my way or leave my sight"; it's a **readiness to keep presenting himself as example**, inviting the child in. Kripke's version turns the scene into licensing/exclusion.

3. **The solution is more skeptical than the problem (p. 74–77).** If all Wittgenstein leaves us with is *matching inclinations* ("My inclination agrees with yours"), that's no solution — "What kind of solution is that?" Cavell accepts Kripke's striking truth that **"there is no fact about me that constituted my meaning"** — but refuses the **skeptical** conclusion. Kripke treats the *absence of the fact* as a **discovery** (itself a quasi-fact, giving an eerie vanishing of meaning). Cavell treats it as **the skeptic's requirement** — a craving for purity/foundation (§107) that was misplaced to begin with. The lack of ground isn't a *lack*; it's the **finite condition of a creature that talks.**

4. **The "all the whirl of organism" passage (p. 80–81).** Cavell's own early formulation (from "The Availability of Wittgenstein's Later Philosophy"): we project words into new contexts, and **nothing insures** the projections agree — what holds is our **sharing routes of interest and feeling, senses of humor, of what is outrageous… "all the whirl of organism Wittgenstein calls forms of life."** "Human speech and activity, sanity and community, rest upon nothing more, but nothing less, than this." Terrifying *and* ordinary. Crucially: "no fact" ≠ "nothing insures" — the former implies an *oversight in our evolution*; the latter names a *finite* creature.

5. **The walking allegory (p. 85–86).** Imagine someone who suddenly hops, whirls, goose-steps instead of walking, insisting "I'm doing the same as always." We look for a **fact about him** explaining the deviance. But walking is **groundless** (grounded only "on the human and the ground"), and deviance there does *not* generate a skeptical paradox. So why does rule-following? Because **philosophy demands a foundation where there is none** — the skeptic's possibility dresses itself up as a *fact (or absent fact) of the human condition.*

6. **The "tabair" skeptic & math vs. ordinary (p. 87–95).** Kripke's skeptic: maybe by "table" you meant **"tabair"** (a table *not* at the Eiffel Tower base, or a chair *there*). Cavell: this **builds criteria up at will**, the *opposite* of real skepticism, which **strips criteria from us**. On math: ordinary and mathematical concepts are *both* forms of **counting**, but math rules settle first instance / interval / order **in advance** ("I always meant this" — eternal, no history); ordinary concepts have **histories**, don't form a series, aren't "bounded" — yet are **no less precise**. The feeling that ordinary concepts "lack something" is the **philosopher's sublimizing**, not a real lack. Math isn't a special case of the ordinary's problem; **without** the mathematical-as-sublime-norm the problem of the ordinary wouldn't even arise.

7. **The scene of instruction & the moral (p. 96–100).** Augustine's child learning language (the *Investigations'* opening) can be read two ways: **ordinary/unremarkable** OR **metaphysical/isolating**. "Nothing is wrong; everything is wrong. It is the philosophical moment." The metaphysical is "the ordinary **hyperbolicized/sublimized**" — Freud's **uncanny**, the familiar as unfamiliar. The child appears **isolated**, *stealing* an inheritance. Final moral (§ via Wittgenstein): **"I am not to give myself explanations that divide me from myself, that take sides against myself, that would exact my consent, not attract it. That would cede my voice to my isolation. Then I might never be found."**

## The bridge back to Lecture 1

"This is simply what I do" is not "I am right" but something like **"Follow me, I am the way"** — which "recalls the problematic of the **perfectionist** relationship… Emerson's **illustriousness**." The question — *how do I take my representativeness of what is said and done as the source of my authority?* — is the **same** question as Lecture 1's. The scene of instruction is a **crisis of consent**: whether the newcomer is accepted into the community (= Emerson's **conformity**). Skepticism / the ordinary is the *philosophical face* of perfectionism's drama of voice, consent, and conformity. (And **Pascal**: the human between grandeur and debasement = Wittgenstein between dissatisfaction-with and satisfaction-in the ordinary, two voices oscillating between vanity and humility.)

## Glossary — words and terms doing special work

| Term | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **criteria** | (Cavell's key term, from *The Claim of Reason*) the grammatical ways we **count** something as falling under a concept — rooted in shared *natural reactions* and *forms of life*, **not** a contract or convention |
| **the ordinary** | ordinary language/life as the site where meaning lives; the thing skepticism wishes to escape |
| **argument of the ordinary** | the standing two-sided tension — **attunement in** criteria (we agree) vs. **disappointment with / repudiation of** criteria (the wish to escape to purity); "an argument neither side should win" |
| **skeptical paradox** (Kripke) | no fact fixes what rule I follow → I can't know I mean *plus* not *quus* |
| **skeptical solution** (Kripke) | community licensing by matching **inclinations** — what Cavell rejects |
| **inclination** | Wittgenstein's "inclined to say" = *not necessarily* what I go on to say (hesitation, room for the self); Kripke flattens it to a *licensing basis* |
| **"This is simply what I do"** (§217) | bedrock, **spade turned** — for Cavell an expression of readiness/patience, not "I'm right, obey" |
| **forms of life / "whirl of organism"** | the shared, unchosen background on which agreement rests — "nothing more, but nothing less" |
| **attunement** (Übereinstimmung) | agreement-in-language deeper than contract; we *come to walk*, we don't *achieve agreement to walk* |
| **counting / tallying** | both math and ordinary concepts rest on counting; ordinary counting = *what counts for us, what matters* |
| **uncanny / sublimized** | the ordinary hyperbolized into the metaphysical; the familiar made unfamiliar (Freud) |

## Which citations carry the argument vs. resonate

- **Load-bearing:** Kripke, *On Rules and Private Language* (the antagonist — §§7, 21, 87–91, 105); Wittgenstein, *Investigations* §201, §217, §185, §107, §25, §304 (the disputed text); Cavell's own *The Claim of Reason* (criteria) and "Availability of Wittgenstein's Later Philosophy" (the "whirl of organism" passage).
- **Structural / thematic:** Augustine (the opening scene of instruction); Pascal (grandeur/debasement ‖ the two voices); Emerson's "Self-Reliance" / "Experience" (the bridge to perfectionism).
- **Resonance / aside (don't get stuck):** Descartes (the first Meditation's madness fear), Hume/Kant (experience), Chekhov/Beckett/Thoreau (nothing-ordinary), the many *Claim of Reason* / "Declining Decline" / "Finding as Founding" self-citations.

## Reading tips

- This lecture is a **rebuttal of one book**. Hold Kripke's three claims (rule fundamental / no-fact paradox / community-matching solution) in mind and the moves stop floating.
- Cavell quotes *Investigations* section numbers as if you have it open. Keep a copy of §§185, 201, 217 to hand.
- **OCR is badly degraded pp. 70–80** (the heart of the Kripke rebuttal). Use the PDF for that stretch; the spine above reconstructs the argument.
