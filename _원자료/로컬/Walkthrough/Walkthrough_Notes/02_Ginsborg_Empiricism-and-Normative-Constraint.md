# Ginsborg, "Empiricism and Normative Constraint" — Walkthrough

*In the Light of Experience*, Essay 4, pp. 101–38.

Ginsborg is the *source* of the reason₁/reason₂ taxonomy that Gersel later extended. Here she uses it to do the opposite of what Gersel does: Gersel pushes the ladder *up* (reason₃/₄) to defend McDowell; Ginsborg works the same base to dismantle minimal empiricism *from below*, replacing it with a sub-rational "primitive normativity."

## I. The target — McDowell's two steps

McDowell's *Mind and World* starts from a general demand: the world must exercise a *normative* (not merely causal) constraint on thinking, or thought has no intentional content. Two steps. **Step 1**: answerability to the world is, in the first instance, answerability to the *empirical* world. **Step 2**: that answerability is, specifically, to *experience* — Quine's "tribunal of experience" — and this answerability is *rational* (Sellars's space of reasons). So experiences must *justify* beliefs without *being* beliefs. McDowell's solution: experience is conceptually structured yet *passive* ("saddled with content," 1996: 10), so it can be "openness" to reality and answerability-to-experience becomes answerability-to-the-world. This threads between Davidson (sense-impressions merely causal → coherentism) and the Myth of the Given (non-conceptual yet rationally constraining → incoherent).

**Ginsborg's wedge** (p. 104): she *accepts* Step 1, *rejects* Step 2. The "broad empiricist requirement" — that the empirical world normatively constrain thought — can be met *without* the constraint being *rational*, and so without McDowell's passive-yet-reason-giving experience. Two difficulties motivate this; then her own construal.

## II. First difficulty — passivity defeats rationalization

Built on Davidson's dictum ("nothing can count as a reason for a belief except another belief"). The **reason₁/reason₂ distinction** (pp. 108–10):

- **reason₁**: a consideration you cite *in support* of a belief, first-personally ("Why believe it rained? — Because the streets are wet"). The *fact* is the reason₁.
- **reason₂**: the *attitude* in light of which, from a *third-person* standpoint, your belief is rationally intelligible — your *believing* the streets are wet.

Davidson's dictum concerns reason₂. McDowell wants to preserve its "substantial point," which means he needs experience to be a reason₂ — to play the role another belief plays — not merely a reason₁. But the ordinary reason-giving he cites ("Because I see that it's green") shows only that experience is a reason₁: it sits alongside "Because the box says so," "Because the detector reads green."

The deep bind (pp. 111–13): to be a reason₂ under McDowell's *internalism* (the subject recognizes her reason as the reason it is), the subject needs a corresponding reason₁ *in view*. What is it? Not *p* itself — McDowell denies the fact is the reason (the hummingbird passage, 2006a: 134). So reason₁ = reason₂ = "my *seeing* that p" — which forces seeing-that-p to be *self-conscious* (I recognize that I see that p). But that collides with the *passive, nondoxastic* character McDowell needs (the brown-sweater case: I do not recognize I am seeing it as brown). McDowell's 2011 escape — restrict "seeing that p" to cases that would be *knowledge* — disqualifies the green-tie/brown-sweater examples, which (a) removes the intuitive handle on nondoxastic seeing, and (b) **trivializes** the claim (p. 116): if a nondoxastic state counts as "seeing that it's green" *only if* it entitles belief, then "experiences rationally warrant beliefs" collapses into a restatement of "we're entitled." The 2008 non-propositional ("intuitional") revision makes it *worse* (pp. 117–18): a *non-discursive* state is even harder to construe as a recognized consideration favouring belief.

## III. Second difficulty — concept-acquisition

Complementary; targets the *conceptual* side. McDowell's experience draws on *antecedently possessed* concepts, so he answers only how a *concept-possessor* forms beliefs, never how empirical concepts are *possible* — the *acquisition* question (pp. 118–19). He would call this an illegitimate "sideways-on" question (his standard move against Kripke/Wittgenstein rule-following).

Ginsborg gives it teeth via **Sellars** (Woodbridge lectures, pp. 119–24). Sellars: conceptual representations must be "guided from without" by *extra-conceptual* sense-impressions (else idealism). McDowell: no — the guidance is supplied by the *objects themselves* becoming present in intuition (the green cube "says": "See me as I am"). **Ginsborg's hinge**: objects guide us *only because we already have the concepts* ("objects speak to us only because we have learned a human language," 1998b: 43) — so they *cannot* do the job Sellars wanted sense-impressions to do, namely make the *learning of language* possible. Sellars's real question is *acquisition*. McDowell misses this, so he cannot claim to out-answer Sellars; and his unanswered acquisition question leaves him "helping himself" to concept-possessors just as he accuses Davidson of helping himself to a body of contentful beliefs (p. 123). The acquisition question is *transcendental*, not merely psychological: without room for answerability to mind-independent objects *in acquiring* concepts, we cannot make sense of there being empirical concepts at all. (Rule-following cousin, p. 124: how does the child trained on green cones/spheres know to say "green," not "grue," at the cube?)

## IV. The positive proposal — primitive normativity

Look at *pre-linguistic* children. **Spontaneous sorting** (pp. 124–27): nine-to-eighteen-month-olds, unprompted, sort an array into kinds. Take the eighteen-month-old adding the fourth pillbox to the pillbox pile. *Not* McDowellian rationality (responsiveness to reasons *as such* — she cannot reason from facts to what-to-do or assess warrant). *Not* mere animal blindness either (rabbit/fox, squirrel/nuts) — her sorting is flexible, general, "reflects an intelligent grasp."

The **middle ground**: what she grasps is *not a reason* but "the sheer appropriateness of her responding that way." She sees the situation as *normatively calling for* her response. Drop the assumption that recognizing a situation as calling for a response *requires* recognizing a *reason*. That yields **primitive normativity** — a normative constraint *more primitive than* rational constraint, available to creatures who cannot yet reason (p. 127).

Applied to Sellars's language game: the child uttering "green" at the new cube is "putting it in a pile." Crucial clarification (pp. 127–28): primitive normativity does *not* explain *why she says "green" rather than "blue"* — that is explained exactly as a pigeon's trained preference (Quine's innate "quality space"). What it supplies is "a different way of understanding *what her saying 'green' amounts to*" — making it *concept-acquisition* rather than mere discrimination (flatworm/rat behavior). Her response is informed by a *consciousness of its own appropriateness to the object*. She "counts as knowing what to say… because, in responding as she does, she is in a position to see her response as called for by the situation" (p. 129).

The Kantian objection and replies (pp. 129–31): doesn't possessing *green* require combining concepts in judgments/inferences (Kant: concepts as "predicates of possible judgments")? Two replies — (a) raise the bar gradually ("Cow!"/"Moo!"/"Cows moo": the awareness of normativity carries over, and combination/inference become plausible); (b) the deeper one — keep concepts as predicates of possible judgments but reject that judgment must be *categorial* combination; use the *third Critique*'s "thinking the particular as contained under the universal," so simple demonstratives ("This is green") are *genuine* judgments. What the child lacks before relating concepts is *not* the capacity to judge or to be intentionally directed at objects, but the capacity to recognize *rational relations*. (The load-bearing heresy: intentionality/objectivity come *before* rationality, not from the categories — fn 38.)

"Does it survive into adulthood?" (pp. 131–33): McDowell *stipulates* conceptual capacities are tied to rationality and might grant primitive normativity only as a *developmental stage* superseded once rationality arrives. Ginsborg's two replies: (1) the child/adult case is *not* parallel to the animal/human case — animals are literally a different species; children are not, so the capacities that make the transformation possible can persist after it. (2) We *continue to need* the sorting capacity as adults — McDowell's own "standing obligation to reflect on the credentials" of our concepts, "ensuring our empirical concepts pass muster." Deciding whether a fossil skull sorts with mammals, whether deckchairs and beanbags are "chairs," exercises the *same* capacity to see objects as normatively constraining sorting — a condition of being a concept-possessor at all.

## V. Resolution and the olive branch

Her picture (pp. 133–35): the world constrains thought by constraining our *sorting behaviour* (paradigmatically verbal responses), thereby constraining the activity by which we *acquire and maintain* empirical concepts. So empirical thought is answerable to the world not only in that judgments depend for *correctness* on the facts, but, more fundamentally, in that our *concepts* depend for their *adequacy* on which objects are presented.

How does she handle the *first* difficulty (passivity)? By giving it up: experience is **active/doxastic** — *seeing that the cube is green already involves taking it to be green* (siding with Davidson). So experiences *can* rationalize belief because they are committive. She dodges coherentism not by McDowell's passivity but by denying the cube→seeing relation is *merely causal*: seeing the cube *as green* depends on the capacity to *sort* it with green things, i.e., to see the cube as normatively constraining the sorting. The episode counts as a *seeing-that* (not bare discrimination) precisely because it incorporates recognition of itself as normatively constrained by the cube. And this is *not* the Myth's guidance: objects do not hand us advance knowledge of how to respond; they "put us in a position to *see* how to respond," as the array does for the sorting child. Objects do not "speak to us" without language — but they can *show* us how to respond (and hence how to represent them) without language (p. 135).

She closes on **agreement** (pp. 135–36): she keeps McDowell's Herder-against-Condillac thesis — "no making sense of naked thought lying behind linguistic clothing"; perception and speech are *already informed by thought*. But there is a *more basic* way they are so informed — *by the consciousness of their own appropriateness to the objects that cause them*. That consciousness of normativity constitutes responses as *expressive of thinking*, and furnishes, one level down, the account McDowell merely *assumes* of how experience and speech are intrinsically contentful.

## Where it plugs into the rest of Part I

1. **The taxonomy's two directions.** Ginsborg builds reason₁/reason₂ to argue experience is *only* reason₁ → minimal empiricism fails. Gersel takes the same pair, adds reason₃/₄ ("responding to reasons *as such*"), and uses the upward extension to *rescue* McDowell. Same lever, opposite directions: Ginsborg below rationality, Gersel above it.

2. **The third stance on rule-following/skepticism.** McDowell *denies* the Kripke/Wittgenstein skeptical problem is genuine; Travis *dissolves* it; **Ginsborg concedes it and answers it** with primitive normativity — fn 40: "appeal to primitive normativity is needed in order to avert the kind of sceptical threat to meaning… posed by Kripke's Wittgenstein… but since McDowell does not consider the sceptical problem to be genuine he would not accept this as a response." Hers is the one position that grants the skeptic standing and then meets it, with a normativity that is genuine but sub-rational.

3. **The sideways-on symmetry.** McDowell forbids both Travis's "how is instancing possible?" and Ginsborg's "how are empirical concepts acquired?" as illegitimate sideways-on questions. Travis and Ginsborg both defy the prohibition — Travis to *deflate* (no Myth, no gap), Ginsborg to *build* (the acquisition question is real and needs the primitive-normativity answer). "No sideways-on view" is the single wall both climb over, from opposite sides.
