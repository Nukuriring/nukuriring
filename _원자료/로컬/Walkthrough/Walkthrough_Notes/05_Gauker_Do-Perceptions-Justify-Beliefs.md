# Gauker, "Do Perceptions Justify Beliefs? The Argument from 'Looks' Talk" — Walkthrough

*In the Light of Experience*, Essay 5, pp. 141–60. Opener of **Part II — The Epistemology of Empirical Belief.**

The temperature drops sharply after Part I: Gauker does cool analytic epistemology + philosophy of language. He lands on the *anti*-conceptualist side (with Travis, against McDowell) but reaches it by an entirely different road — the semantics of "looks."

## 0. Thesis and strategy

Target: **"perceptions justify beliefs"** — the orthodoxy that gained steam with *Mind and World* (1994). Gauker's own view (Gauker 2012) is that perceptions have content that can be accurate/inaccurate but is *not propositional or conceptual*, hence not expressible as "a looks F." He **brackets** that and argues on the opponent's turf: *even granting* "a looks F" reports perceptual content, the one explicit argument for the orthodoxy — the **argument from "looks" talk** — fails. Payoff: remove a standard argument that perceptions must have *conceptual* content ("if perceptions justify beliefs, they share conceptual content with beliefs" — deny the antecedent, and perceptual content is free to be nonconceptual).

## 1–2. Why it is not obvious that perceptions justify beliefs

Alternative: the **entry-level justifiers are non-perceptual beliefs** — beliefs the world imposes through a sensory process, justified by reliable provenance, not *by* perceptions serving as premises. Cases: seeing a table, my belief isn't justified *by* the perception; what justifies "I brushed my teeth" is *my belief that I can still taste toothpaste*. (Historically the orthodoxy is a newcomer; Russell, C. I. Lewis, Chisholm made entry-level justifiers *beliefs* apprehending the given.)

Against the presumption that *unless* perceptions justify beliefs they lack rational grounding (the *MW* p. 10 "last step"/receptivity/friction/openness passage):

- **Beliefs can be "tied down" too.** Seeing a horse, I'm not "free" not to believe there's a horse; the world imposes the belief. McDowell's privileging of passive experience over spontaneous belief is unpersuasive on its own.
- **The expressibility constraint.** A justification is something one can *give* — express in words, persuasively. So a perception's content would need verbal expression. **Dilemma**: words are either *generic* (cube-shaped) → indeterminate choice among determinables; or *completely determinate* (an exact shape) → but perceptions don't represent completely determinate properties.

Also the standing **conflation** (against Pryor's dogmatism): the perceptual experience vs. the *conceptual response* to it (a propositionally-contentful *thought-not-yet-belief*, "that's a table"). Thoughts-not-yet-beliefs giving prima facie justification ≠ *perceptions per se* justifying.

## 3. The meta-argument from "looks" talk

```
The "looks" argument           The meta-argument from "looks" talk
   Premise:    a looks F.          P1: The "looks" argument is good.
   Conclusion: a is F.  (defeas.)  P2: The premise reports perceptual content.
                                   P3: The conclusion reports belief content.
                                   ∴   Perceptions defeasibly justify beliefs.
```

He records **Ginsborg's** diagnosis of McDowell (conflating reason₁ — justify by a *state of affairs* — with reason₂ — justify by reasoning from the *content of one's mental states*) but **declines to rest on it**: these authors plausibly *do* mean justification by something contentful. His master move instead: **P1 is false *if* P2 may be taken for granted** — on the reading of "a looks F" needed to make P2 true (reports perceptual content), the argument is *not* good.

## 4. Pinning down "looks"

Clear away impostor uses ("looks like it was built for Mickey Mouse"; comparative "looks like an ivory-billed woodpecker"; source-flagging "looks like the river flooded") and two confounds:

- **Confound 1 — "looks" doing no work:** "looks like a package → the books arrived" — the load may be carried by the *belief* that it's a package, not the perception. **Fix:** restrict to cases where content *apart from "looks"* equals the conclusion ("looks like a package → is a package").
- **Confound 2 — "looks" flagging the source:** "it looks like construction is finished" beats "I'm told" because we trust observation-based testimony — the pull is from *testimony*, not perception.

Block two semantic theories the meta-arguer can't use: **Glüer's** "phenomenal" reading (*looking-F* as a respectable property → "a looks F" becomes a non-perceptual *proposition*, killing P2); and the reduction to "I am having a visual perception that a is F" (the **argument from a fact about perception** — its goodness wouldn't show perceptions justify beliefs and just *assumes* propositional perceptual content).

What remains: "looks" must express a **special modality** yielding a truth *only if* the embedded proposition is the content of a perception. Coin the operator **"visually appears"**:

```
The "looks" argument (revised)
   Premise: a visually appears F.   Conclusion: a is F.
```

Semantics: "visually appears" is **assertion-undermining** (family of "apparently / allegedly / possibly," not "surprisingly / certainly"), so it does *not* assert that a is F (else the premise inherits every doubt about the conclusion). "a visually appears F at i" is true only if *the proposition that a is F is the content of some visual perception at i* — but it is **not identical to, and not implied by,** "someone has a visual perception with the content that a is F." That **modal-fact ≠ existential-fact** asymmetry powers §5.

## 5. The critique — five modes of single-premise support, all failing

- **#1 Logical implication** — out at once: a non-factive modality doesn't entail "a is F."
- **#2 Fulfillment of a necessary step** — even if the biologically-normal process passes through a perceptual representing-as-F stage, there is **no immediate inference from "biologically well-formed" to "epistemically justified."**
- **#3 Inference to the best explanation** — "a is F" can explain the *existential* fact ("someone has a visual perception that a is F" = the argument from a fact about perception), but **not** the *modal* fact "a visually appears F." (Parallel: "a is F" may explain "*someone alleges* a is F" but not "a is *allegedly* F.")
- **#4 Probability raising** — does "a visually appears F" even take a probability? If its only available probability is Pr(a is F), then Pr(a is F | a visually appears F) = Pr(a is F | a is F) = 1 — **proves too much** (we didn't want certainty).
- **#5 Ruling out alternatives** — no doubt about the conclusion the premise answers. "Visually appears rectangular" rules out "visually appears *non*-rectangular," but that's no alternative to *being* rectangular; ruling out "tilted trapezoid" would help, but it's no easier to see how the premise does *that* than how it justifies the conclusion.

**Verdict:** **P1 is false if P2 is true.** A *sui generis* support peculiar to perception-to-belief inferences would be ad hoc and collapse under the §2 doubts.

## 6. Positive account — "looks" as a Sellarsian hedge (and the closing irony)

Building on **Sellars's** "looks"-as-hedge (the necktie-shop story: under electric lighting John's tie reads green indoors / blue outdoors, so he learns to say "it *looks* green," withholding the all-in assertion — this is the *ancestor* of McDowell's green-tie/brown-sweater cases and Ginsborg's discussion). Gauker keeps two elements (withhold the all-in assertion; flag vision as source) and strips the propositional-content ascription:

> "a looks F" is true in context c iff the agent **would be disposed to assert that a is F if their assertions about a were guided only by immediate visual experience of a** (without checking against further requirements like normal lighting).

**The irony:** on *this* analysis the "looks" argument *is* good — what you'd say going on vision alone is a (defeasible) reason that a is F (the orange T-shirt that "looked a beautiful red in there" excuses the prior belief). **But** then **P2 is false**: the premise reports *a counterfactual fact about what the agent would assert*, not the content of a perception.

Pincer closed: **P1 false if P2 true (§5); the one reading on which P1 is true makes P2 false (§6).** Either way the meta-argument collapses. "Looks" talk is fine reasoning; it never licenses "perceptions justify beliefs."

## Where it sits in the volume

First chapter to argue McDowell's conclusion is wrong *from epistemology + philosophy of language* rather than transcendentally. Gauker is a **content-nonconceptualist** ally of Travis and the particularists, but never needs "instancing," "the historical," or the Myth. Two links to Part I: (1) he engages **Ginsborg's reason₁/reason₂** and pointedly declines to rest on it; (2) his Sellarsian "hedge" account sits atop the **same necktie/green-tie/brown-sweater example** that recurs across the volume — here given a deflationary linguistic treatment.

**One-line takeaway:** "a looks F" can justify "a is F" — but only ever by routing through *belief* (or a counterfactual about what one would assert), never by the *perception itself* doing the justifying; the most-cited quick argument for "perceptions justify beliefs" equivocates between a perceptual reading of "looks" (on which the inference isn't good) and a doxastic one (on which it is).
