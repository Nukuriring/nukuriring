# Gersel, "What is the Myth of the Given?" — Walkthrough

*In the Light of Experience*, Essay 3, pp. 77–99.

This is a meta-interpretive paper. Gersel does not defend a first-order view of perception so much as **reconstruct McDowell's "Myth of the Given" so that it can play the dialectical role McDowell needs it to play** in the debate with Travis, Brewer, Kalderon, and Crane.

## 0. The task — why the paper exists

McDowell wields the Myth in two roles at once:

- **Incontestability**: a theory that falls into the Myth is *simply incoherent* (McDowell 2009a: 256).
- **Substantiality**: the *only* way to avoid the Myth is **conceptualism**.

McDowell's own formulations swing between these, so critics peel off one side at a time. Brewer, Crane, Kalderon, Travis (2013) *accept incontestability but thin out the substantiality* ("my non-conceptualist theory also passes the constraint"); Travis (2018) *accepts substantiality but denies incontestability* ("there is nothing mythical about that Given"). Gersel's goal: reconstruct one reading on which the constraint is **both incontestable and forces conceptualism**.

## 1–2. Locating the Myth — the dilemma of the two quotations

Givenness = something given *to the subject* that can rationally constrain his thinking; it must be accessible at the **personal level**, and the subject must be describable as *responding* to it. Founding premise: **Minimal Empiricism** — experience must provide reasons for thought.

Two *Mind and World* quotes stage a dilemma. One (xvii) is incontestable but too thin (a mere logical requirement: don't conceive experience so it cannot give reasons, then say it gives reasons). The other (p. 7, "the space of justifications does not extend wider than the conceptual sphere") is substantial but not incontestable. So a good reading must present *some less controversial constraint as incontestable, and then show it forces conceptualism*. The only firm anchor: the Myth functions inside an argument from Minimal Empiricism to conceptualism.

## 3. The four-rung ladder of "reasons" (the paper's key apparatus)

Extending Ginsborg (2007):

- **reason₁**: what objectively counts in favour of a judgment — what grounds/ensures its truth (a truth-maker).
- **reason₂**: the psychological state/event (experience, belief, memory) that makes a reason₁ *psychologically relevant* to the subject. Third-personal rationalizing.
- **Very-Minimal Empiricism**: "experience must be able to be a reason₂ by providing a reason₁." → Gersel's diagnosis: *most critics mistake the Myth for this level.*
- **reason₃**: a reason the subject *himself* can appreciate as counting in favour — responding to it *in light of its status* as a reason. Analogy: *reacting to something green* vs *reacting to something in light of its being green* (Kantian autonomy: stepping back from an inclination and asking whether the apparent reason really licenses the response).
- **reason₄**: the psychological state/event that makes reason₃ relevant, enabling self-conscious, autonomous rationality.

**Full Minimal Empiricism**: "experience must be able to be reason₄ for thought by providing the self-conscious subject with reason₃." (You may keep only Very-Minimal Empiricism — but then you have left the conversation with McDowell.)

## 4. Conceptualism — and disposing of Travis's first objection

Since McDowell abandoned propositionalism (content is now conceptual but *non-propositional*), the issue is **unity** — not the unity of synthesizing sense-data into objects (the Kalderon/Johnston misreading), but **Kantian** unity: a judgment just *is* representing something as falling under a generality. So conceptualism = "what is given in experience must include the presentation of objects *as falling under a generality*."

**Travis's objection**: if experience is conceptual, what we meet is a *proposition*; propositions are general and abstract, so they cannot be the *particular truth-maker* (the tree blossoming *right now, only on the south side* = "the historical"). Hence conceptualism violates Minimal Empiricism.

**Gersel's two-point reply**:

1. Travis assumes "conceptual ⇒ propositions are given," but for McDowell what is given are *objects*, encountered *by being seen to be the ways they are* — ways that can *also* be judged. We are presented with non-conceptual items (objects) *as falling under concepts*.
2. **The role of Sense**: the Sense of a predicate concept is *not* a descriptivist general condition but *a particular way the concept is presented as fulfilled* — a maximally specific way. So we encounter the specific way an object satisfies a general predicate, but encounter it *as the Sense of a general concept*. Particularity and generality meet at one point.

## 5. The constraint and the skeleton of the argument

Load-bearing premise:

> **Non-Myth**: Unless generalities are given in experience, we cannot step back and evaluate whether a judgment is appropriate in light of what is given.
> (Rehearsed: reasons₃ cannot be given in experience unless generalities are given.)

Complete argument:

```
P1 (Minimal Empiricism):  Experience must be a reason₄ for judgment.
P2 (Def. of reason₄):     A reason₄ is a psychological state/event in which a reason₃
                          is given to a subject.
─ Interim conclusion:     Reasons₃ must be given in experience.
P3 (Non-Myth):            Reasons₃ cannot be given unless generalities are given.
─ Conclusion (Conceptualism): Generalities are given in experience.
```

P2 is true by definition; P1 is granted (with reservations). So **all weight falls on P3 = Non-Myth.** §§6–8 are the "somewhat incontestable" defense of this one premise.

## 6. Particularist vs generalist

Both agree experience puts us in *direct contact with concrete particulars* (even, à la Kalderon, with structured unities of particulars). The single fork: the **generalist** says particulars are given *as falling under generalities*; the **particularist** denies it — greenness is never given *as greenness*, the beetle never *as a beetle*. Gersel's diagnosis: particularists think they have dodged the Myth only because they are picturing *Very-Minimal Empiricism* (reason₁ = truth-maker).

## 7. Three forms of evaluating an experiential reason "as such"

1. **First form (experience → judgment direction)**: ask whether *the way the world is given* ensures it is as you take it to be. (Is seeing the car in the garage enough to judge Fred is home? Is encountering the beetle's greenness enough to judge it green?) Suasive force flows *from the given to the judgment*.
2. **Second form**: the judgment contradicts something you know (Fred is in India) → by modus tollens, "the experience could not have been a reason₁."
3. **Third form**: question whether you have an adequate reason₂ (strange lighting, a hallucinogen, a non-perceptible subject matter).

Forms 2 and 3 run the *reverse* direction (start from beliefs, conclude about what must/can't have been given). McDowell's decisive move: **forms 2 and 3 cannot be the sole basis.** If they were, the only self-consciously evaluable constraint would move *from how we already take the world to be* — i.e., **Davidsonian coherentism, a frictionless spinning in a void**. So Minimal Empiricism *requires* that the first form be possible.

## 8. The first form and the particularist's obstacle (the climax)

A subject-predicate judgment commits you to "this particular instantiates this generality." The **generalist** has it easy — just experience the beetle's way of being coloured *as a way of being green*. The **particularist** cannot: experience is *mute* on whether particulars meet the requirements for generalities, so a **gap** opens between what is given (bare particulars) and what is judged (a particular-under-a-generality).

The natural patch: *perceptual concepts bridge the gap.* But that only solves a *metaphysical* problem (concepts fix the requirements); the *epistemic* question remains — how is the subject *sensitive* to those rules (otherwise judgments are only accidentally true)? Appeal to a **recognitional skill** (possessing "green" = being inclined to take things as green just when their given ways are in fact ways of being green)? That explains reason₂ (third-personal), *not* reason₃. **There is a world of difference between properly bridging a gap and appreciating that you have bridged it.**

Now **Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations** bite: we cannot credit thinkers with knowledge of the *substantial* rules governing concepts. McDowell's reading (1984) permits only *modest* rule-knowledge: "for it to be proper to judge something green, it must be green." The generalist needs only this trivial rule (the step from *seeing-as-green* to *judging green* is trivial). The particularist needs knowledge of a *substantial gap-bridging rule* (from sheer particularity up to generality) — exactly what Wittgenstein says is impossible.

Terminal dilemma: either the rule specifies the requirements *via further generalities* → regress (no generalities are given); or knowledge is a *list of particular ways of being* → infinite, ungraspable by a finite mind. Either way, impossible.

### 8 (cont.). Why McDowell cares about this "trivial" self-scrutiny

McDowell's theory must preserve two things at once: (1) judgment is constrained by a *passive* element, and (2) there are *no bounds* on a rational thinker's self-scrutiny. If preserving both forces self-scrutiny to be trivial, so be it. A *substantial* gap immediately demands an appreciation of how it is bridged → regress and untenable knowledge-attributions. **McDowell's solution: the gap is already closed in experience** — we are *passively given particulars as falling under generalities in specific ways.* That lets the passive element supply reasons for the active element (judgment) such that we can self-consciously scrutinize. Travis and Brewer ground the rationality of perceptual judgment in the *proper exercise of recognitional capacities* (active) — but Minimal Empiricism demands those very active responses be *passively constrained*; you cannot get self-scrutiny if active exercises are only evaluable against further active exercises.

## 9. Conclusion

A recap of the argument's form. Gersel grants that *many controversial premises* were used, each an exit point for the particularist. His aim is modest: reconstruct an argument that fills the dialectical role McDowell assigns the Myth, and show how the constraint *could appear incontestable* to someone moved by McDowell's ambitious picture of rational self-consciousness.

---

**One-line takeaway**: the real edge of the Myth is not the *truth-maker* (reason₁) but **self-scrutiny** — for the *first form* of evaluation (assessing an active judgment against what is passively given) to be possible, you must close the gap without running into Wittgenstein's rule-following problem, and the only way to do that is for *generalities to already be given in experience* (conceptualism).
