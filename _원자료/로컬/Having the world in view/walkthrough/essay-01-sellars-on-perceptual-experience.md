# Essay 1 — Sellars on Perceptual Experience
### A dialectical walkthrough

*Source: McDowell, *Having the World in View* (Harvard UP, 2009), Essay 1, pp. 3–22.*

---

## Orientation: what the essay is doing, and how to read it

This is the first of three linked essays (1–3) under the heading "Sellars, Kant, and Intentionality." Its method is **diagnostic**. McDowell reconstructs Sellars's account of perceptual experience as sympathetically and strongly as he can — and he does this precisely in order to locate the *single* place where, in his view, Sellars takes a wrong turn. That one wrong turn is then supposed to illuminate, by contrast, both what Kant really thought and what *we* ought to think about intentionality (the directedness of thought and language toward the world).

The argument almost never proceeds by flat assertion. Its characteristic motion has four beats, and it is worth watching for them at every stage:

1. **set up** a position (usually Sellars's);
2. **raise** the tension or objection that position naturally invites;
3. **find** the resource inside the position that answers the tension;
4. **split** that resource — keep the part McDowell endorses, reject the part he does not.

So the dialectic works by *dissolving* several oppositions that McDowell regards as false dichotomies, while *preserving* exactly one. The preserved opposition — the fault line the whole essay is built to expose — is this: **transcendental philosophy carried out from *within* the conceptual order, versus transcendental philosophy carried out "from sideways on," from a standpoint *outside* it.** Everything else is scaffolding for that contrast.

---

## §1 — The backhanded compliment

McDowell opens by stating a shared conviction: no one has come closer than Kant to making intentionality unproblematic, and the best route *to* Kant runs through Sellars's sustained attempt to be a Kantian. Praising Sellars as the way into Kant is the compliment.

The "backhanded" part is the thesis that organizes all three essays: a *fully* Kantian vision of intentionality is, McDowell claims, **inaccessible to Sellars "because of a deep structural feature of his philosophical outlook."** The strategy follows directly from this: McDowell proposes to bring Kant (and the right view) into focus by attending to **the gap between what Sellars knows Kant *wrote* and what Sellars thinks Kant *should have written*.** Where a great reader feels forced to "correct" his master, the correction is diagnostic of the reader's own commitments.

Read dialectically, §1 plants the target before the argument begins. We are told a fault line exists and that it is structural — not a local slip but something built into Sellars's outlook. The rest of the essay is the slow approach to naming it.

---

## §2 — The master thought: drawing the line

Sellars's "master thought" in *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (EPM) is that some characterizations place a state or episode **"in the logical space of reasons"** (§36), and that the conceptual apparatus for doing so is **irreducible** to any apparatus that does not. The master thought therefore **"draws a line"**: *above* it, placings in the space of reasons; *below* it, characterizations that do something else.

McDowell immediately notes that this is so far only a *negative* demarcation, and that Sellars's real worry is a specific **pitfall**: the temptation to suppose that certain below-the-line characterizations can discharge tasks that only above-the-line characterizations can. The positive gloss is the contrast between **epistemic facts and natural facts**: we must not think we can understand epistemic states as the actualization of *merely natural* capacities (capacities had at birth or acquired through animal maturation).

Here McDowell makes his first interpretive move, one he will lean on repeatedly: **"epistemic" amounts to something like "concept-involving."** That reading lets him bolt Sellars directly onto Kant. The logical space of reasons becomes the space in which we place episodes *described as actualizations of conceptual capacities*; and its Kantian counterpart is **the realm of freedom**. The bridge is the thought that conceptual capacities are paradigmatically exercised in **judging** — and judging is a free, responsible act, answerable to criticism in the light of reasons. Hence: space of reasons = realm of (the freedom of) judging. The distinctively twentieth-century addition is that this capacity arrives *with initiation into a language*.

Dialectically, §2 supplies the grid. "Above/below the line" and "the pitfall of confusing the two" are the coordinates in which every later question will be posed.

---

## §3 — Anti-foundationalism, and why this is about intentionality

Does empirical knowledge have foundations? Sellars's answer is nuanced, and McDowell uses it to dissolve a dichotomy. Traditional empiricist foundationalism is not just the claim that knowledge rests on perception; it adds an **atomistic independence** — each basic observation report is supposedly available on its own, independent of the world-view it grounds. *That* independence is what Sellars rejects.

Sellars's picture has **two logical dimensions** at once: in one, observation reports are basic and other propositions rest on them; in the other, observation reports themselves **depend on** the world-view they help ground. The relation is one of **mutual dependence** rather than one-way support. So the view remains *empiricist* (it keeps the first dimension) while breaking with *traditional* empiricism (it insists on the second). This is a classic McDowellian resolution: not foundationalism, not its flat denial, but a reconceiving that keeps what each side was right about.

The deeper point McDowell extracts is that **this is not narrowly epistemology at all — it is a theory of intentionality.** Using Sellars's remark about "thinking of a celestial city," he shows Sellars equating "epistemic character" with **intentionality** and applying it where no knowing is in question. So "epistemic" really does mean "concept-involving," and the dependence on a wider world-view is a claim about *objective purport* in general: the intentionality of perceptual experience depends, in that second logical dimension, on **"having the world in view"** — on a view that reaches beyond the here and now. This is what licenses reading Kant (Sellars's model) as a philosopher of intentionality. Borrowing Heidegger's provocation that the first *Critique* "has nothing to do with a theory of knowledge," McDowell defuses it: epistemology is no more *the* concern of the *Critique* than it is of EPM — both are really about directedness toward the world.

---

## §4 — The "mongrel," the programme, and experiences that "contain" claims

Now the substantive analysis. Sellars (§7) diagnoses the classical **sense datum** as a **"mongrel"** bred from two ideas: (1) *non-concept-involving sensory episodes* (sensations of red) and (2) *non-inferential knowings that such-and-such is the case*. The defect is a **conflation across the line**: (1) belongs below it, (2) above it. Sellars's **programme** (§10) is to take the two apart, keep "what survives criticism" in each, and recombine them properly — i.e., to show how **sensibility and understanding** jointly furnish the intentionality of perceptual experience.

McDowell follows the *above-the-line* strand first. The non-inferential-knowing idea is refined into **seeings**, then widened to the class of **ostensible seeings** (experiences in which it merely *looks* to one as if things are thus-and-so, whether veridical or not). The pivotal image: an experience, "so to speak, makes an assertion or claim," or **"contains" a claim** (§16). Visual experiences are **conceptual episodes** — actualizations of conceptual capacities — to be understood on the model of linguistic acts in which claims are literally made.

McDowell unpacks "contains a claim" through judging. Judging that there is a red cube exercises (at least) two conceptual capacities — one shared with "red pyramid," one shared with "blue cube" — and exercises them with a **specific mode of togetherness** answering to the semantic togetherness of "red" and "cube." Then comes the careful **split**: experiences are *not* judgings. Belief in perception is usually acquired "by a sort of default," with none of the freedom judging involves — there is a real **"disconnection between perceptual experience and judging."** Yet the same conceptual capacities are **actualized** with the same togetherness — only *involuntarily*. (Hence McDowell's deliberate word: **"actualization," not "exercise."**) And the actualization has a distinctive character: the claim is "contained" as **ostensibly necessitated by the object seen** — "evoked or wrung from the perceiver by the object perceived" (§16 bis).

The dialectical payoff: McDowell has captured *everything experience seems to deliver* — full conceptual, claim-bearing, world-directed content — using only **above-the-line** resources. This sets up the essay's central pressure.

---

## §5 — The hinge: "Why sensations at all?" and the transcendental turn

If the above-the-line story already makes experience fully conceptual *and* recognizably visual — for the conceptual episodes are themselves **"shapings of visual consciousness,"** not phenomenologically colorless items needing to be tinted by added sensations — then **why does Sellars insist a complete account must *also* include non-conceptual sensations (sensations of red)?** Sellars simply says this is "clear." McDowell refuses to grant it, and presses the strongest objection in the essay.

On EPM's own stated motivation, the sensations are posited for an **explanatory** purpose "in scientific style": to explain why the *same* claim is "contained" across a trio — a genuine seeing, an illusion, and a hallucination. But McDowell objects: why must the required sameness lie at the level of **sensations (items in consciousness)** rather than at the level of **proximate causes** — retinal images, patterns of light? Sellars himself speaks of the proximate causes of sensations. If similar impacts on sensory equipment can explain the similar conceptual episodes, the inner sensations do no extra work. **"The sensations look like idle wheels."**

This is the turn. In *Science and Metaphysics* (p. 18) Sellars meets exactly this objection and **changes the picture radically**:

- The question is no longer about the *sameness of contained claims*. It becomes: **how is it that sensory relatedness to the environment takes the form of conceptual episodes — episodes that "contain" claims — at all?**
- The explanatory need is no longer *scientific*. It is **transcendental**.

"Transcendental" is used in a recognizably Kantian sense: the sense-impression inference is meant to **vindicate the legitimacy of the very apparatus** — experiences as conceptual actualizations that "contain" claims — by which we conceive experiences as **ostensibly of objects at all**. Sensations are not an optional scientific extra; they are what supposedly *entitles* us to have spoken of experiences as having objective purport in the first place.

---

## The crux: transcendental philosophy, yes — *from sideways on*, no

Here McDowell finally names the fault line promised in §1, and performs his decisive **split**.

A **temptation** attaches to transcendental philosophy: to suppose it must be conducted from a standpoint **external** to the conceptual goings-on whose objective purport is to be vindicated — a place from which one could survey the relation between thought and its objects **"from sideways on."** Sellars's move **fits this temptation exactly**: he undertakes to vindicate the objective purport of conceptual occurrences **"from outside the conceptual order."**

McDowell's verdict is precise, and the precision is the whole point:

> "I shall be taking issue with this conception of transcendental philosophy. It is important to see that this is not to take issue with the very idea of transcendental philosophy."

So the preserved opposition is finally explicit. McDowell **keeps** the Kantian, transcendental project (we *do* owe an account of our entitlement to objective purport) and **rejects** the sideways-on execution of it (the account must *not* stand outside the conceptual order and reach for it from without). This is the "deep structural feature" of Sellars's outlook flagged at the start — his scientism expresses itself as a willingness to ground the conceptual from a vantage point beyond the conceptual. The diagnosis is now on the table; Essays 2 and 3 will press it.

---

## §6 — Apperception: the sensation as *that through which*, not a veil

Before moving on, McDowell strengthens Sellars's position against an objection McDowell himself once pressed (in *Mind and World*) — a fine example of the four-beat method turned to a charitable end.

At the culmination of EPM, Sellars vindicates inner episodes in **two phases** (conceptual, then non-conceptual), each in **two stages**: first the concepts are introduced theoretically (episodes attributable only inferentially), then a **non-inferential, self-reporting** use is secured. By EPM's end, conceptual episodes and sensations are "on a level" with respect to such self-attribution. But in *Science and Metaphysics* Sellars revises this: visual impressions are **states** of consciousness yet **not objects** of consciousness — they are **not apperceived** ("apperception" = non-inferential self-knowledge).

The puzzle: how could there be items in consciousness *constitutionally* incapable of being apperceived? McDowell's charitable reconstruction: they are not apperceived **while they are playing their transcendental role** — which is not to say they are unapperceiv*able*. If one turns attention onto the manifold of "sheer receptivity," it **ceases** to perform its enabling function. One can attend either *to the environment* (through the sensations) or *to the sensations*, **but not both**.

This dissolves McDowell's old worry that below-the-line items would be an **opaque veil** — something we infer *from* rather than see *through*. On the revised picture the manifold figures **"as that through which"** attention reaches the environment without hindrance, not as an object blocking the way. Notably, McDowell can repair this *without* yet touching the sideways-on commitment — which is why the essay's real objection is held in reserve.

---

## §7 — The handoff to Essay 2

Sellars holds that this is essentially the picture **Kant was aiming at**, though not adequately stated on Kant's pages. Two strains show: (1) Kant tends to **restrict "consciousness" to the apperceived**, making it hard to locate non-apperceived impressions in him; and, more seriously, (2) Sellars must convict Kant of **"serious confusion" in the thesis that space is the form of outer sense** (Sellars: that idea "is incoherent").

That second strain is the wedge. It forces the question McDowell carries forward: **is Sellars getting Kant wrong — and is that bound up with Sellars's being wrong that sound philosophy *requires* sensations in the role he assigns them?** Because Sellars's reading of Kant is shaped by his own philosophical convictions, the exegetical question and the substantive question are **inextricable**. "These are my questions for the next of these three essays."

---

## The dialectic in one view

- **Dichotomies McDowell dissolves:** foundationalism vs. its denial (→ two logical dimensions, mutual dependence); epistemology vs. theory of intentionality (→ they are the same inquiry once "epistemic" = "concept-involving"); conceptual content vs. visual character (→ conceptual episodes *are* shapings of visual consciousness); sensation-as-veil vs. no-sensation (→ sensation as *that through which*).
- **The one opposition he preserves:** transcendental vindication **from within** the conceptual order vs. **from sideways on**, outside it. McDowell endorses the project, indicts the external standpoint.
- **The structural diagnosis:** Sellars's scientism makes the sideways-on standpoint feel obligatory — which is why a *fully* Kantian view of intentionality stays out of his reach.

**Carry into Essay 2:** the status of *space as the form of outer sense*, and the test of whether Sellars's transcendentally posited sensations really are demanded by sound philosophy — or only by the external standpoint McDowell has marked for attack.
