## Essay 1 — "The Meaning of a Word" (1940)

*Read to the Moral Sciences Club (Cambridge) and the Jowett Society (Oxford), 1940. Three parts; we are in Part I.*

### Frame
Part I's thesis: the phrase **"the meaning of a word" is "in general, if not always, a dangerous *nonsense-phrase*."** Tone (self-deprecating — "the most trite… the most muddled… all too long") enacts the anti-grand temperament: ordinary standards of truth/accuracy for philosophy. Note **"dangerous"** — not merely empty, but a *trap*.

### Preliminary remark — sentence-primacy (load-bearing, not really "preliminary")
"What alone has meaning is a **sentence**." Word/phrase meaning is *derivative*: a word "has a meaning" = there are sentences containing it that have meanings; a dictionary only "suggest[s] aids to the understanding of **sentences** in which it occurs." [= Frege's **context principle**.] Austin says he'll "overlook" it for now — but it already pulls the floor: "the meaning of *a word*," a word ripped from all sentences, is looking in the wrong place.

### Meaning is something you *do* — the *racy* example
Asked "what is the meaning of *racy*?", I do two things: (i) **explain the "syntactics"** — describe what raciness is/isn't + specimen sentences where "racy" fits and where it doesn't; (ii) **demonstrate the "semantics"** — get you to imagine, or actually experience, situations we'd *correctly* describe with "racy," and others where we wouldn't. → Explaining a meaning = teaching a **capacity** to deploy the word across the right situations and withhold it across the wrong ones. No third item, "the meaning of *racy*," is handed over alongside. [The engine of "linguistic phenomenology": words pinned against the world.]

### The spurious leap — the **"Nothing-in-particular" fallacy**
From "meaning of *rat*/*cat*/*mat*?" we — "being philosophers" — leap to "**What is the meaning of *a word*?**" — "something spurious." It is *not* the harmless "meaning of the word 'word'?" (a fine particular question), but "meaning of **a-word-in-general**… no particular word at all." That is "a perfectly absurd question": "I can only answer 'What is the meaning of "x"?' if "x" is some particular word." Name of the disease: **"the fallacy of asking about 'Nothing-in-particular' — decried by the plain man, but by the philosopher called 'generalizing' and regarded with some complacency."**

### Master-key
This fallacy is the engine behind the dichotomy-dissolving of the whole SEP arc: "meaning" (the entity), "reality," "Being" are manufactured by sliding from sensible *particular* questions to a counterfeit *general* one, then hunting an object to answer it. Austin's own next example is **"reality"** — "How would you distinguish a **real** rat from…" — the very "real ___?" / trouser-word move that dissolves the sense-datum dichotomy in *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962). The 1940 essay already holds the weapon used against Ayer twenty years later.

### Part I (cont.) — the reification of "meaning"
Faced with the nonsense question, we don't drop it — we **re-hyphenate**: from "What-is-the-meaning-of (the word) 'rat'?" (genuine) to "What is **the-meaning-of-a-word**?" — and "a crowd of reassuring answers present themselves: 'a concept', 'an idea', 'an image', 'a class of similar sensa'," all "spurious answers to a pseudo-question." Stress-tested three ways: the **plain man** ("meaning of *muggy*?" → "the concept of mugginess" earns "a stare as at an imbecile"); the **parody** ("the point of doing *anything*?" → a new "universe of entities called 'points'"; for meaning, the effects are mild — "the writing of books"); the **square-root contrast** — "the square root of *x*" *is* a definite description of a number *y*, but **"the meaning of *p* is *not* a definite description of any entity."** Sophisticates relapse anyway: **Hampshire** ("a class of similar particular ideas") and **C. W. Morris** ("the designatum… a kind of object or class of object").

**Two reasons we relapse:** (a) **"the curious belief that all words are names"** — every word, like a proper name, *stands for / designates* a thing (the naming / "Fido"-Fido theory — ancestor of the descriptive fallacy); (b) the **analysis-residue malady** — having analysed a sentence containing "x", we still ask "but what, *in* the analysis, *is* x?" ("The State owns this land" → "but what *is* the State?"; "trees exist unperceived" → "sensing sensa" → unease → **theories of 'sensibilia'**). **Loop:** Austin (1940) already diagnoses the **sense-datum theory** as an instance of this fallacy — the very theory he demolishes in *S&S* (1962). Upshot: "**no simple and handy appendage of a word called 'the meaning of "x"'.**"

### Part II — "meanings" have no "parts" → the analytic/synthetic dichotomy collapses
"Is *y* part of the meaning of *x*?" (= "is 'x is y' analytic or synthetic?") presupposes meanings are *things containing parts*. They aren't — so "part of the meaning of x" is "completely undefined… left hanging in the air," and the analytic/synthetic dichotomy rests on "a **working-model which fails to fit the facts.**" [**This is where the compositionality / part-whole instinct lands** — Austin denies *meanings have parts*, not "compositionality" by name. And the date: a **1940 lecture**, a decade before Quine's "Two Dogmas" (1951), by a different route — parallel insight, not influence (printed only 1961).] The model also crushes the **syntactics/semantics** distinction (treating every "can't-say" as *syntactic* self-contradiction, ignoring *semantic* convention).

### Moore's paradox — the speech-act seed (1940)
"The cat is on the mat, and I do not believe it" is absurd; but "the cat is on the mat" does **not** *imply* "I believe it" (the speaker may be lying). The truth: **asserting *p* implies "I believe *p*"** — in a *special* sense of "imply" (as **asking a question implies I don't know the answer**): "**By asserting *p* I give it to be understood that I believe *p*.**" What blocks the absurd conjunction is not syntax but "**some semantic convention (implicit) about the way we use words in situations.**" [= the **sincerity condition** of assertion (the "abuse" of §2.3) + proto-**Grice** ("give to be understood" = implicature) — well before *HTDTW* and before Grice.]

### Part II (cont.) — the cases that break the dichotomy
**"Might-not-Q" test:** a *genuine* "p implies r" makes *both* "not-Q" and "might-not-Q" absurd ("triangles are figures and triangles might have had no shape" is as absurd as the flat conjunction); the *special* implication (assert→believe) makes "not-Q" absurd but **"might-not-Q" fine** ("p and I might not have believed it"). The asymmetry catches a "can't-say" that is *not* a logical contradiction.

Cases that **cannot be classed analytic or synthetic** (neither verbal contradiction nor empirical):
- **"This noise exists"** (subject = a sensum). A: "does not exist" absurd → "exists" is *part of the meaning* of "this" (analytic). B: "might not have existed" fine → *not* part of the meaning (synthetic). Both right in *argument*, wrong in *conclusion*: truth = **using "this" gives it to be understood that the sensum exists** — a fact of *use* (presupposition; cf. "Caesar is bald"), not analytic/synthetic content.
- **"I think x good but I do not approve of it"** — not *verbally* self-contradictory; maybe **neither** self-contradictory **nor** "perfectly good sense." [meta-ethics.]
- **"Pink is more like red than black," "what is good ought to exist,"** Berkeley's extended/coloured/shaped, "*est sed non percipitur*" — you get **"landed with 'synthetic a priori knowledge'"** if you force the dichotomy.
- **"x is extended but has no shape"** — "extended"/"shaped" only ever learned *together*; the sentence is **not prohibited** yet has **no convention covering it**: "we cannot see what this could mean." "**Ordinary language blinkers the already feeble imagination.**"
- **The philippic cat** (→ "real" in *S&S*, *in utero*): live with a cat four years, then it delivers a philippic — **neither** "it is a real cat" **nor** "it is not a real cat" "fits the facts semantically… each is designed for other situations." So **"every proposition has a contradictory" is false.** "**Ordinary language breaks down in extraordinary cases. (The cause of the breakdown is semantical.)**"

**What it proves:** the analytic/synthetic dichotomy is **not exhaustive** — a third "can't-say," grounded in **"semantic convention… about the way we use words in situations,"** falls through the net. **"Synthetic a priori knowledge" is the symptom** you're "landed with," not a real category; the culprit is the **ideal-language "working-model."** Actual language has "**few, if any, explicit conventions, no sharp limits… no rigid separation of syntactic and semantic**" [= **open texture** (Waismann); a shot at Carnap/early-Wittgenstein].

**"First word, not the last word" — already in 1940.** It won't do to force language to a model, *but equally* it won't do to "rest content" with ordinary usage "as though there were nothing more to be discovered… There may be extraordinary facts… which would need new and better language to describe."

### Part III — "why do we call different things by the same name?" (the typology)
Austin rejects *both* the metaphysician's **universals** *and* the nominalist's "**because they're similar**." Against the nominalist: "similar in a certain *respect*" just reopens the door to universals; and worse, it's "not in the least true that all the things… called by the same name are… 'similar' in any ordinary sense." Typology of *good reasons* (none reducible to plain similarity):
1. **Paronymy** (Aristotle, *pros hen*): "healthy" — body (**primary nuclear sense**), complexion ("productive of health"), exercise ("conducive to health"); the nuclear sense is "contained as a part" in the others. [Disputes over whether "exist" has "two senses" (objects / sensa) dissolve once seen as *paronymous*.]
2. **Analogy**: A:B::X:Y → foot of a mountain / of a person. Not "similar"; the *relations* correspond. (Severe: "change" across categories.)
3. **Family-resemblance chains**: B~A, C~B, D~C… but **A and D "do not resemble each other in any recognizable sense at all."** [= Wittgenstein's **family resemblance**, 1940 — 13 years before *PI* — with the sharper anti-universals moral.]
4. **Incomplete senses**: "fascist" (orig. x,y,z; later any *one*) → instances "need not be similar at all"; "cynicism," "cupboard love."
5. **Determinable/determinates**: colour : red/green/blue. ("pleasure" — pleasures "differ precisely in the *way* they are pleasant"; the hedonist's error is treating pleasure as "a single similar feeling.")
6. **Substance / quality / relation**: "youth," "love," and — pointedly — "**truth**," whose theories "turned on whether they interpreted it as a name of substance, of a quality, or of a relation." [The reification fallacy, aimed at his own next topic.]
7. **Activity-part** (his "specially interesting" one): cricket **bat/ball/umpire** — each named "cricket" by playing its **own special part in the activity** "cricketing"; not reducible to "used in cricket" (circular). **Aristotle's suggestion: "good" works this way** — so hunting a simple "definition" of "good," or the "similarity" among good things, goes far astray (likewise "golfing," "**happiness**").

**Two payoffs:**
- **The classification/meaning split is not universal.** "The apparently common-sense distinction between 'What is the meaning of the word x' and 'What particular things are x?' is not of universal application… The questions **cannot be distinguished**" for cricket/golfing/happiness. [= the user's (a) "is this particular an X?" vs (b) "how far does X reach?" — Austin raises *exactly* this and shows it **collapses** for activity-words.]
- **"head" in a good dictionary**: its senses "related to each other in **all sorts of different ways at once**." No single relation; traditional logic's "univocal / equivocal / also analogous" lumps everything non-uniform together uselessly.

**Essay summary (Austin's own):** (1) "the meaning of a word" is a **spurious phrase**; (2) consequently, re-examine "**being part of the meaning of**" and "**having the same meaning**." "Dogmatists require prodding — although history indeed suggests that it may sometimes be better to **let sleeping dogmatists lie**." → **Essay 1 complete.**

### Argument reconstruction (Essay 1)

**Master thesis.** "The meaning of a word" is a *spurious phrase*; meaning is *use*, studied case-by-case — and the meta-linguistic dichotomies built on the reified picture (analytic/synthetic; same/different meaning; universal/similar) are artifacts to be dismantled.

**Method (runs throughout).** Don't assault the abstract question; descend to particular uses — "what we should say when, and why."

**A — there is no entity "the meaning" (Part I).**
- P1. Explaining a particular word's meaning = teaching its *use* (correct/incorrect sentences = "syntactics"; situations it does/doesn't fit = "semantics").
- P2. Nothing in that practice is, or needs, a further object "the meaning" handed over alongside.
- P3. "Meaning of *a word in general*?" has the *form* of a meaning-question but no particular word as subject → asks about "nothing-in-particular" → pseudo-question.
- P4. The entity-answers ("concept," "class of similar sensa," "designatum") arise only from two fallacies: *all-words-are-names* (denotation) and *analysis-residue* ("but what, *in* the analysis, *is* x?").
- **∴ A.** No entity is "the meaning of a word"; meaning is use; the general question is spurious.

**B — the analytic/synthetic dichotomy is not exhaustive (Part II).**
- P1 [from A]. "Meanings" are not entities with *parts*.
- P2. "Is 'x is y' analytic or synthetic?" presupposes y is/isn't *part of* the meaning of x → presupposes part-containing meaning-entities.
- P3 [P1,P2]. So "analytic" is, in general, undefined — content only relative to an idealized "working-model" of language.
- P4. Some sentences we *can't (felicitously) say* are neither verbal contradictions nor empirical falsehoods (this-noise-exists; extended-but-shapeless; "real cat" of the philippic cat); their impossibility is *semantic* (convention of use), per the "might-not-Q" test.
- **∴ B.** The analytic/synthetic dichotomy is not exhaustive; "synthetic a priori" is the residue squeezed out by the working-model; real language has *open texture*. (Corollary: ordinary usage = first word, not last.)

**C — there is no single ground for shared naming (Part III).**
- P1. Why one name for many things? Candidate *single* answers: a shared *universal* (realist) or bare *similarity* (nominalist).
- P2. "Similar" either collapses into "similar *in a respect*" (re-admitting universals) or is plainly false — many co-named things aren't similar in any ordinary sense.
- P3. Inspection finds *several distinct* grounds: paronymy, analogy, resemblance-chains, incomplete senses, determinable/determinates, substance/quality/relation, activity-parts.
- **∴ C.** No single relation grounds shared naming; replace the demand for one definition with a case-by-case *typology* — and for "good"/"happiness"/"cricket," "what it means" and "what falls under it" can't even be separated.

**∴ Master.** Drop "the meaning of a word"; study use, extension, and breakdown case-by-case — and the meta-linguistic dichotomies dissolve.

## Essay 2 — "Other Minds" (1946)

*Reply to John Wisdom's symposium paper. Austin tackles only "**How do we know that another man is angry?**" — sideways.*

### Method: the goldfinch as "stalking-horse"
Austin refuses the frontal "how do we know other minds?" and descends (Essay-1 method) to "**How do you know that's a goldfinch?**" — "a statement of particular, current, empirical fact," "not, on the face of it, unlike 'He is angry'." The goldfinch is a **stalking-horse**: a tame, tractable case whose anatomy of "How do you know?" transfers back to the wild one.

### "Know by analogy" ≠ "argue by analogy"
We don't **know** *by* analogy (analogy as the *source/ground* of knowing, like "by sight"); we only **argue** *by* analogy (analogy as a *piece of reasoning* in support). Asked "how do you know there's a tea-party next door?" you cite a *source* ("I hear the cups"), not "by analogy." Wedge against the **argument-from-analogy** solution to other minds: even if you can *argue* by analogy, it doesn't follow you *know* "by analogy" — and casting other-minds knowledge as an *inference* may already misdescribe knowing (a goldfinch is *recognized*, not inferred).

### The grammar of "How do you know?"
- **Assertion implies knowledge** ("But I took it you knew," reproachfully) — strictly, belief. [Essay 1's assert→believe link.] So asserting exposes you to "Do you know?" / "How do you know?"
- **"How do you know?" vs "Why do you believe?"** — we never say "Why do you *know*?" or "How do you *believe*?" ("be sure"/"be certain" side with *believe*). Pointed: "How do you know?" → *maybe you don't know at all*; "Why do you believe?" → *maybe you oughtn't believe*. **The existence of your *knowledge* is challengeable; of your *belief*, not.**
- **Payoff:** "I believe / I'm sure / I'm certain" may *describe a subjective state* — but **"I know" is not (merely) that; "it functions differently in talking."** [→ the performative "I know" = "I promise," still to come.] The "if I know, I can't be wrong" objection is parked "for due course." [= the capacity-fallibilism vs token-guarantee seam.]
- **Opportunity vs acumen** (bittern answers): (a) "brought up in the fens," (b) "I heard it," (c) "the keeper reported it" = *how you come to be in a position to know*; (d–f) "by its booming" = *how you can tell*. → knowledge needs **opportunity** (past experience + present chance) + **acumen** (learned recognition, exercised now). [= §3.1's "successful exercise of judgmental capacities, given opportunity."] Degrees: a town inside out, a proof backwards, a poem word for word, "a Frenchman when we see one."

### Reasons for knowing / testimony (briefly)
"Reasons for knowing" = stating *how I'm positioned* ("I served on the commission," "I was two yards away"); "reasons for believing" = symptoms/arguments/evidence. **Testimony** is a *source of knowledge* (know "at second hand" by citing an authority who was positioned). **Trust principle:** "we are entitled to **trust others except in so far as there is some concrete reason to distrust** them… accepting testimony is a main point of talking" (cf. games: no game unless the opponent is trying to win).

### The three challenges to "it's a goldfinch" — and the definite-lack *filter*
"How can you tell?" → "By its red head." Three objections *that don't dispute your facts*:
- **(1) "goldfinches don't have red heads"** / **(1a) "that's a goldcrest, by your own description"** — accuse you of *failing to recognize / mis-naming* the type. Reply: "then I was wrong" / "then it's a goldcrest." ("Knowing what a thing is is… knowing the right *name* for it.")
- **(2) "that's not enough"** → here you'd say "then you don't know." Austin's points:
  - **(a) Definite-lack requirement:** "you must have in mind some *more or less definite lack*" ("how do you know it isn't a woodpecker? Woodpeckers have red heads too"). **"If there is no definite lack, which you are at least prepared to specify on being pressed, then it's silly (outrageous) just to go on saying 'That's not enough'."**
  - **(b) "Enough is enough: it doesn't mean everything"** — enough to show, within reason and for present purposes, "it can't be anything else… It does *not* mean enough to show it isn't a *stuffed* goldfinch."
  - **(c) "From/by" vs "because":** "From its red head" is deliberately *vague* — *recognition* of a feature peculiar to goldfinches, often not sayable in words (we recognize the smell of tar, port vintages, "motor-car makes from behind," without saying *how*). "**Because** it has a red head" is "*dangerously definite*" — implies that's *all* you noted and that no other British bird has any red head.
  - **(d) Proving:** "I know" claims I can (appropriately) *prove* = cite features enough to make it so-describable "and not in any other way relevantly variant." "Because" → can prove; "from/by" → "know but can't prove."

### The skeptic proper — the "Reality" worry (≠ objection 2)
The *further*, philosophers' challenge: "**Do you know it's a *real* goldfinch? Aren't you dreaming? Mightn't it be stuffed?**" Either my *experiencing* is phoney (dreaming, delirium, mescal) or the *item* is (stuffed, painted, dummy, trick, mirage). Anti-skeptical core: **"The doubt 'is it a real one?' must have a special basis — some *specific* way it's suggested to be phoney."** Context usually fixes it (goldfinch→stuffed, not mirage; oasis→mirage, not stuffed); else "I am entitled to ask 'What are you suggesting?'" **"The wile of the metaphysician** consists in asking 'Is it a *real* table?'… not specifying what may be wrong with it" (conjurer: "satisfy yourself this is an ordinary hat"). → "real" has no single meaning; **always specify the contrast** [= the *S&S* "real" analysis, here in **1946**]. Riders: **(a)** from "sometimes I can't tell," the skeptic wrongly infers "I never can"; **(b)** if the bird later "explodes or quotes Mrs Woolf," "**words literally fail us** — we don't say we were *wrong*… we **revise our ideas**." **Knowledge-claims aren't *predictions*; the future can't *prove* them wrong, only force revision.** [↔ philippic cat; anti-verificationism.]

→ **The definite-lack demand is *anti*-skeptical.** A "not-enough" challenge *with* a relevant lack ("woodpecker") is **legitimate, answerable inquiry**; the **skeptic** is the one whose doubt supplies **no** relevant lack ("is it *real*?"). *(Corrects the natural slip of filing objection (2) itself as "the skeptic's move.")*

### 2. Sureness & certainty (anti-incorrigibility)
The "are you *sure*?" worry ≠ the reality worry — it's about confidence in *recognition/description*. Against **Wisdom**'s incorrigible "sense-statements": even "it looks red to me now" is **ambiguous** between confident and hesitant (magenta/mauve/heliotrope — I may not be sure what it looks like *to me*). **"Any descriptive word is classificatory, involves recognition and… memory… only when we use such words are we knowing anything… But memory and recognition are often uncertain and unreliable."** [the conceptualism thread; dumb sensa + fallible classification]. So **no incorrigible base** — "knowing I'm in pain" already smuggles in *recognition* ("knowing him to be Jack" = *recognized* him, can be mistaken). Two hesitancies: **(a) "certain"** = confidence in memory/past discernment; **(b) "sure"** = confidence in current perception. ("the original sin — Berkeley's apple, the tree in the quad — by which the philosopher casts himself out from the garden of the world we live in.")

### "I know" = "I promise" — the performative heart (1946)
- **The plunge.** "I promise" doesn't *announce an intention*; "by using this formula (performing this ritual) I have **bound myself to others, and staked my reputation**." "Similarly, saying **'I know' is taking a new plunge.**"
- **Not a super-state.** "It is **not** saying 'I have performed a specially striking feat of cognition, superior… even to being merely quite sure': for **there is nothing in that scale superior to being quite sure**." → **categorically different, not the top of a ladder.**
- **The core.** "**When I say 'I know', I give others my word: I give others my authority for saying that 'S is P'.**"
- **The jump (= the prediction, in his words).** "The very great difference between saying even '**I'm absolutely sure**' and saying '**I know**' is like the difference between '**I firmly and irrevocably intend**' and '**I promise**'."
- **What the word buys:** "I'm sure" is *for my part* (take it or leave it — your responsibility); "I know" → **you are entitled to rely**. "If I have said I know or I promise, you **insult me in a special way by refusing to accept it**." The right to say "I know" is **transmissible** authority (→ knowing "at second hand"; testimony). Immediate challenge: "**Are you in a position to know?**" — show not that you're *sure* but that it's "**within your cognisance**" (∥ "in a position to promise" = within your power).
- **Infallibilism obsession, dissolved** [↔ our seam]: philosophers fasten on "can't predict the future" → "if I know I can't be wrong, so I've no right to say I know." **Doubly mistaken:** (a) justified in saying we know even if it turns out badly; (b) the conditions for "within my cognisance" are about the **present and past, not the future** — "it is not demanded that I do more than *believe* about the future."
- **Apparent disanalogy → infelicities (HTDTW in embryo).** Objection: "proved wrong → you *didn't know*" but "failed → you *did* promise." Austin: *more apparent than real* — you did *say* "I promise"/"I know" (the gravamen of letting us down); but if you never intended / had concrete reason to expect failure, "in another sense you **didn't** promise." The **ritual** analysis: "I do" (already married → **null and void**), "I order" (no authority → "couldn't order"), "I warn" (no bull → "couldn't warn"). **These are *misfires*.** And the name: "**To suppose that 'I know' is a descriptive phrase is only one example of the *descriptive fallacy*, so common in philosophy.**" → the whole *HTDTW* apparatus (performatives, felicity conditions, void, descriptive fallacy) **already here, sixteen years early**.

### Back to other minds — "I know he is angry"
The goldfinch anatomy carries over. We *do* say we know another is angry (vs. believe), but not always — royalty, fakirs, bushmen, Wykehamists, strangers → "**you never can tell**." Depends on **familiarity** (this type of person, this individual, this situation). **New ingredient:** to know what *you* feel, "I must also… be able to **imagine (guess, understand, appreciate) what you're feeling**" — more than discriminating displays; I must (apparently) have **felt it myself**.

### "He is angry" — signs vs display, and the "mumps" pattern
**The grammar of "sign/symptom" (the wedge).** "Symptoms"/"signs" have a *restricted* usage — only "by way of implied contrast with inspection of the item itself," only where the item "is liable to be **hidden**" (future, past, under the skin, in some casket). "When the item is itself before us, we no longer talk of signs and symptoms" (signs of a storm = an *impending* storm, not one on top of us; like *traces*/*clues* — once you know the murderer, no more clues). So Wisdom's "all the signs of bread" (when you see/taste the loaf) misuses "sign": "the taste or feel of bread is **not a sign or symptom of bread at all**." **Punchline:** "**to say that we only get at the 'signs' or 'symptoms' of anything is to imply that we never get at it.**" The skeptic's "we only ever get the symptoms of his anger" **begs the question** through a word that can only be used of the already-hidden.
**Symptoms vs display.** "Symptoms of anger" = *rising/suppressed* anger (twitch, pallor, tremor), contrasted not with the inner feeling but with the **display** (a tirade, a blow — "the acts in which the anger is **vented**"). Only symptoms → *believe*; "given himself away" (display) → *know*.
**The "mumps" analogy (anti-reification, done right).** Feeling↔display is intimate (the felt impulse to vent; "no such thing as 'being angry' apart from any impulse… to vent it"), plus the natural **occasions**. So "'**being angry**' is… like '**having mumps**' — a **description of a whole pattern of events** (occasion, symptoms, feeling, manifestation). It is as silly to ask '**What, really, is the anger itself?**' as to fine down 'the disease' to one item." — *But not behaviorism:* "**That the man himself feels something which we don't… is evident enough**… but there is **no call to say that 'that' (the feeling) IS the anger.**" The feeling is **real**, but *one strand of the pattern*, not the whole. Hence "How do I get from the **scowl** to the **anger**?" is malformed — the scowl is an organ of the pattern, not a clue to a hidden item (parts related far more intimately than "newspapermen scurrying in Brighton… to a fire in Fleet Street").
**Learning the inner from the outer** (proto-Wittgenstein, 1946): we learn to say "I am angry" of ourselves by (a) noting occasion/symptoms/manifestation when *others* say it, and (b) being *told* "You are angry" — so the man "will sometimes accept corrections from outsiders about his own emotions" (you were jealous, not angry).

### The special difficulty — conceded, but local
The dissembler ("might he run the whole display ad infinitum and not be angry?") "**has special features of its own**." **Three genuine doubts:** (1) **deception** (feigning/suppressing — acting abnormally this time); (2) **misunderstanding** (does he "feel like us" at all?); (3) **deliberateness** (is the act deliberate or inadvertent?). "All work together in the feeling of **loneliness** which affects everybody at times." None arises for goldfinches/bread: "the oasis cannot **lie** to us and we cannot **misunderstand** the storm." **The radically differentiating feature: "the goldfinch… is uninscribed and mute: but the man speaks."** His own statement holds "a unique place" — usually accepted (we know "at second-hand"); in conflict cases we may reject it "though we always feel some uneasiness" (the diary case → "we should not know what to say").

But the difficulty is **local, not global**: met by "established procedures" (though "we do not expect always to establish"); "**no suggestion that I never know** what other people's emotions are, nor… that I might be wrong for no special reason." And the anti-skeptical principle persists (a doubt needs a special, intelligible basis) + the readiness to **revise terminology** for new cases (↔ philippic cat).

### "Why believe him?" — believing persons is irreducible
Pushed to "Why believe him *ever*?", the obvious answers (induction on his reliability; his behaviour "most simply explained" by his feeling as we do) are "**dangerous and unhelpful**" — they invite the deeper challenge to "the very possibility of believing another man at all," and tempt us to say "other minds are no more really real than unconscious desires." **"This, however, is distortion.** Believing in other persons, in authority and testimony, is **an essential part of the act of communicating**… **as much an irreducible part of our experience as giving promises, or playing competitive games, or even sensing coloured patches**… But there is **no 'justification'** for our doing them as such." **Final Note:** (1) "of course I don't introspect Tom's feelings"; (2) "of course I do sometimes know Tom is angry"; ∴ to read "How do I know Tom is angry?" as "How do I introspect Tom's feelings?" is "**barking our way up the wrong gum tree**." Knowing ≠ first-person access.

### → Cavell picks up here
Austin stops at: *believing other persons is irreducible and needs no justification* (+ the **loneliness**). **Cavell** takes exactly this and adds the weight: the other-minds "problem" isn't an epistemic deficiency to be made up by knowledge but our standing in **acknowledgment**, which can be *refused* — skepticism as **evasion** of the other ("Knowing and Acknowledging," *The Claim of Reason*). This is the orientation's "first tension" made textual: the 인정 reading is *half Austin, half Cavell*, and you can see the seam.

### Argument reconstruction (Essay 2)
**Master thesis.** "He is angry" is answered like "that's a goldfinch" — *positioned recognition*, not a special faculty; "I know" is a **performative** (giving one's word), not a description of a superlative inner state; the other-minds "Predicament" mismodels knowing as **introspective access**. The one genuinely special difficulty (the speaker can lie / be misunderstood) is *real but local*.

**A — knowing is positioned recognition, not inference to a hidden item.**
- P1. "How do you know?" = *opportunity* (training + chance) + *acumen* (learned recognition, exercised now).
- P2. "Sign/symptom" applies only to *hidden* items → "we only ever get the symptoms" **begs** the hiddenness.
- P3. The display is anger *vented* (item before us); "how do I get from the scowl to the anger?" is malformed.
- ∴ A. Knowing he's angry = recognizing a **pattern** ("being angry" ≈ "having mumps"); the feeling is real but one *strand*, not "the anger."

**B — "I know" is a performative, not a description.**
- P1. Grammar: know/believe asymmetry; *knowledge* challengeable, *belief* not; "sure" is *for my part*, "know" entitles reliance.
- P2. "I know":"I'm absolutely sure" :: "I promise":"I firmly intend" — a **categorical jump**, not a higher rung.
- P3. "I know" *gives my word/authority* (transmissible); going wrong → I'm *answerable* (a **misfire**, "null and void"), not merely mistaken in a report.
- ∴ B. Treating "I know" as descriptive = the **descriptive fallacy** (HTDTW in embryo).

**C — the Predicament is a mismodeling; the residual difficulty is real but local.**
- P1. A doubt "must have a special basis" (definite, relevant lack; "real?" must specify a contrast) — bare "you might be wrong" is idle.
- P2. Special feature: *the man speaks* — he can **lie**, be **misunderstood** (three doubts). So the difficulty is genuine, not dissolved.
- P3. Yet local: recognized procedures + revisability; and **believing persons is irreducible, needing no justification** — a precondition of communicating, not a shaky inference.
- ∴ C. "How do I know Tom is angry?" ≠ "How do I introspect his feelings?" ("wrong gum tree"). Knowing ≠ first-person access; lacking telepathy is no deficiency.

**∴ Master.** Other minds is positioned recognition of a *pattern* + the irreducible practice of *believing persons*, with "I know" a *giving of one's word*. The one special difficulty (the speaker can dissemble) is real, local, met by ordinary procedure + readiness to revise — not by introspective telepathy. **→ Essay 2 complete.**

## Essay 3 — "Truth" (1950)

*Symposium with Strawson (whom Austin answers in "Unfair to Facts," 1954). The §2.2 engine, now from the inside.*

### "Truth" is an abstract noun — don't reify it
"'What is truth?' said jesting Pilate, and would not stay for an answer." "**'Truth' itself is an abstract noun, a camel… of a logical construction**" — don't ask whether Truth is *substance* / *quality* / *relation*; ask after "the use… of the word **true**." [= the "Meaning of a Word" anti-reification, applied to truth.]

### The bearer of truth is the **statement**, not the sentence
Rivals ruled out: **beliefs** (a picture is "true *to* life, not true *of* it" — faithful, not true); **propositions** ("we never say 'the meaning of this sentence is true'"); **words/sentences** ("ungrammatical, ambiguous, defective, mis-spelled" — not true/false). When we *do* say "his closing words were very true," the **demonstratives** ("*his*," "*were*") show we mean the words "**as used by a certain person on a certain occasion** — that is… **statements**."
- **A statement is a historic event.** Categorial grammar: "**A sentence is made up of words, a statement is made *in* words. Statements are *made*; words/sentences are *used*. A sentence is not English or not good English; a statement is not *in* English.** I coin sentences, not statements." One sentence → many statements ("I say 'It is mine,' you say 'It is mine'").
- The giveaway: "it is a fashionable mistake to take as primary '(The sentence) "S" is true *in the English language*'" — the qualifier betrays that "sentence" ≠ "statement," so it "precisely is *not* what can be true or false." [= the user's point: the sentence is an occasion-independent *type*; only the historic *making* is truth-apt.]

### "When it corresponds to the facts" — a truism, rebuilt
"**The theory of truth is a series of truisms**" — correspondence "can hardly be wrong… still, it can be misleading." Setup: **words** + **world** (may include the words) + the world's **similarities/dissimilarities** ("if everything were absolutely indistinguishable or completely unlike, there would be nothing to say") + **two conventions**: **descriptive** (sentences ↔ *types*), **demonstrative** (statements ↔ *historic* situations). **Truth:** "a statement is true when the historic state of affairs to which it is correlated by the **demonstrative** conventions… is of a *type* with which the sentence is correlated by the **descriptive** conventions." (Inside "is of a type with which" sits *this paper's* fn. 2 — the "sufficiently *like*… a convention is involved" crux.)

### Three deep cuts
- **"It takes two to make a truth."** Hence (a) **no internal criterion** — no feature "detectable in the statement itself" reveals its truth; (b) "a statement cannot without absurdity **refer to itself**." (Rules out coherentism *and* self-referential paradox at a stroke.)
- **No mirroring (anti-*Tractatus*).** The word↔type correlation is "**absolutely and purely conventional**" (a "one-spade language": "tst *nuts*" could be true exactly when "the National Liberals are the people's choice" is). "**There is no need whatsoever for the words… to 'mirror' in any way… any feature… of the situation**; a statement no more needs… to reproduce the 'structure' or 'form' of the reality than a word needs to be echoic." To think it must "is to fall… into the error of **reading back into the world the features of language**." [↔ the analysis-residue fallacy of Essay 1; a frontal shot at the picture theory.]
- **Facts ≠ true statements, ≠ Doppelgänger.** "Fact that S" tempts (i) *fact = true statement* (→ coherence errors: the detective "doesn't crawl round the carpet… he utters statements") and (ii) *one fact per statement* ("for every cap the head it fits" → correspondence errors: "overpopulate the world with linguistic **Doppelgänger**" — negative, general facts). Yet "there *is*… a state of affairs… **toto mundo distinct** from the true statement"; "between **stating**, however truly, that I am feeling sick and **feeling sick** there is a **great gulf fixed**." In discussing truth "**it is precisely our business to prise the words off the world and keep them off it.**" [= the method credo, explicit.]

### Against redundancy — "is true" is NOT superfluous (§4)
Against Ramsey/Strawson ("'p is true' just asserts p; 'is true' is logically superfluous"): **"But wrongly."** *tstST* ("the statement that S is true") refers to the world *inclusive* of *tstS*; *tstS* refers to the world *exclusive* of itself — **different statements**. The **libel** case: trial 1 — was Q's statement "W is a burglar" *true*?; trial 2 — *is* W a burglar? "An arduous business to hold a second trial — why, if the verdict is the same?" Because the *issue* differs. And the theory **confuses falsity with negation**: "'He is not at home' = 'It is false that he is at home'? But what if no one said he was at home? what if he's lying upstairs *dead*?" A language could lack "true/false" but not affirmation/negation. (Fn: to be false = to **mis-correspond** with a fact; the *demonstrative* conventions fix *which* fact.)

### "True" names a *dimension*, not a simple property (§5) — the key turn
"True" is one of a **family** of word-world assessment adjectives — "exaggerated, vague, bald, rough, misleading, too concise" — none "logically superfluous." So "it is **pointless to insist on deciding in simple terms whether the statement is 'true or false'**." Examples: "Is it true or false that **Belfast is north of London**? That the **galaxy is the shape of a fried egg**? That **Wellington won the battle of Waterloo**? **There are various degrees and dimensions of success in making statements: the statements fit the facts always more or less loosely, in different ways on different occasions for different intents and purposes.** What scores full marks in a general-knowledge test may elsewhere get a gamma." Plus the **inept** class (not true/false but *out of place* — "'all the signs of bread' said when the bread is before us"). 
**How the machinery survives** (not refuted, *drawn out*): the clause "is of a type with which" = "**sufficiently like**" was never sharp — *how* like is "sufficiently" is purpose-relative. So "true" is a **dial** (a dimension of fit), not a **switch**. Skeleton (word-world correlation, "it takes two") intact; flesh (graded, occasion-relative fit) added = the orientation's **conventionalist, occasion-relative correspondence**.
**Freedom parallel — already here, 1950:** "We become obsessed with 'truth'… just as… with '**freedom**'… Like freedom, **truth is a bare minimum or an illusory ideal**." [The §4 "A Plea for Excuses" thesis, six years early, on the page.]

### "Every proposition is true or false" = the descriptive fallacy (§6)
"It is jejune to suppose all a statement aims to be is 'true'" — and not every "statement" aims at truth at all. "**The principle of Logic, 'Every proposition must be true or false,' has too long operated as the… most pervasive form of the descriptive fallacy.**" Non-descriptive "statements": "a formula in a calculus… a **performatory utterance**… a value-judgement… a definition… part of a work of fiction." "Not the business of such utterances to 'correspond to the facts.'" ("You *said* you promised" [= "I promise"] vs "You *stated* that you promised" [= "I promised"].) → the trapdoor from a theory of truth into **speech-act theory** (HTDTW).

### Reimer vs Travis — is Austin a correspondence theorist?
**Reimer:** yes — a *spectrum-model* correspondence theorist; statements match facts "**more or less**," the degree fixed by circumstances. Anchor: Austin says coherence/pragmatism miss "the trite but central point that **truth is a matter of the relation between words and world**" (1950) — self-described (revisionary) correspondence.
**Travis (2013):** correspondence is a **nonstarter** for Austin — it requires **two autonomous domains** "whose denizens are what they are *independent* of" Austinian considerations (fair to say / misleading / better description), with truth = a *match*.
**Why Travis cuts deeper (the hunch):** Reimer relocates the circumstance-relativity into the *degree of match* but keeps the **two-autonomous-domains architecture**; Austin's "**sufficiently like… a convention is involved**" denies the **fact-side is autonomously individuated** (typing = convention + judgment + purpose). No independently-given right-hand relatum ⇒ nothing for "correspondence" to be a relation *between*. (= the "'is true' isn't a two-place predicate" objection: refusing two independently-given relata.)
**Verdict:** half *verbal* (does "correspondence" survive gutting the autonomous truth-maker?) + half *substantive* (are the domains autonomous? — Austin: **no**). Reimer wins the *label/letter*; Travis wins the *structure/spirit*. Best held as the orientation's **conventionalist, occasion-relative word-world** view; Travis's "nonstarter" = where Austin's premises *lead* (radicalize the stable descriptive layer), not Austin's own letter. [↔ 01_ Open threads: "is it still correspondence?"]

### Austin contra the Tarskian / disquotational scheme ("'snow is white' is true iff snow is white")
Austin **literally addresses** the T-biconditional: of "'It is raining' is true iff it is raining" he says "**so far so good**" — he *accepts the biconditional as a truism* ("the theory of truth is a series of truisms"); he rejects taking it as the **analysis/essence** of truth. → verdict: *true truism, false analysis.* Objections:
- **Wrong bearer.** The schema makes a *sentence* (relative to a language) the truth-bearer — "'(The sentence) S' is true *in the English language*" is "**a fashionable mistake**" / "a solecism." Only *statements* (historic uses) are true.
- **Rigged example.** "Snow is white" is an *eternal, occasion-insensitive* sentence — exactly the kind Austin warns (fn 4: "Caesar was bald," "all mules are sterile") "**make us overlook the distinction between sentence and statement**." For occasion-sensitive language ("the cat is on the mat," "Belfast is north of London") the disquotation merely *relocates* the occasion/purpose-sensitivity to the right-hand side; the **demonstrative conventions** + purposes are still needed.
- **Disquotation hides the world.** "So far so good" — *but* "between **stating**, however truly, that I am feeling sick and **feeling sick** there is a **great gulf fixed**." The schema makes "it takes two" *look* like "it takes one, written twice"; gone are the two conventions and the gradable "sufficiently like." (So it doesn't really *exhibit* the word-world relation — it disquotes it away.)
- **If read deflationarily (→ redundancy), false** (§4): "'snow is white' is true" is a *different statement* from "snow is white" (libel-trial / *tstST* refers to the world *inclusive* of *tstS*); "is true" is an "**extraordinary word**," not a tame disquotation device; redundancy *confuses falsity with negation*.
- **Deep point (↔ Travis/autonomy).** A Tarskian/Davidsonian truth-conditional theory assigns each sentence a *determinate, occasion-invariant* truth-condition = the **stable descriptive layer** Travis radicalizes away. Davidson's relativizing to speaker + time is a half-step toward Austin's *statement*, but occasion-sensitivity (painted-green-leaves) outruns any fixed parameters; "snow is white" hides this because it is eternal. The T-schema is the formal-semantics form of "two autonomous domains, matched."

### §7 — vs Strawson: a performative *aspect* ≠ no assertion; the close
Strawson: to call a statement "true" is not a further assertion but to *confirm/grant* it. Austin concedes the **performative aspect** ("to say ST is very often… to confirm or grant tstS") but it doesn't exclude the **assertive** one — "to say you are a **cuckold** may be to *insult* you, but it is **also and at the same time to make a statement which is true or false**." [= the constative/performative coexistence that topples the wall in *HTDTW* — here, 1950.] Killer case vs pure endorsement: "what of the case where you state that S and I say nothing but **'look and see' that your statement is true**?" — silent *checking* is impossible if "true" only endorses. **Closing line (file under Reimer):** "if… the rather **boring yet satisfactory relation between words and world**… does genuinely occur, why should the phrase 'is true' **not be our way of describing it**? And if it is not, what else is?"

### Argument reconstruction (Essay 3 — "Truth")
**Master thesis.** What is true is the **statement** (a historic, occasion-bound *making*), not the sentence; a statement is true when the situation it demonstratively refers to is *of the type* its sentence descriptively fits — a **genuine but "boring," gradable, purpose-relative word-world relation** ("it takes two"). So "true" is neither redundant nor a simple property, but the name of a **dimension of assessment**; "Every proposition is true or false" is the **descriptive fallacy**.
**Method.** Don't reify "Truth" (abstract noun); examine the *use* of "true"; prise the words off the world.
- **A — bearer = statement.** Beliefs/propositions/sentences aren't primary bearers (a picture is *faithful*, not *true*); demonstratives ("*his* words *were* true") betray that we mean words *as used on an occasion* = a statement; "'S' is true *in the English language*" is a "fashionable mistake." ∴ truth attaches to the occasion-bound making.
- **B — a genuine, gradable word-world relation.** Two conventions (descriptive sentence↔type; demonstrative statement↔historic situation); true = situation "sufficiently like" the standard. Purely conventional, *no mirroring* ("reading back into the world the features of language"); facts ≠ true statements, ≠ Doppelgänger ("great gulf fixed"). ∴ a *modest but real* relation — "why should 'is true' not describe it?"
- **C — "true" is not redundant (vs Strawson/Ramsey).** "ST" refers to the world *inclusive* of *tstS* → a different statement (libel trial; silent "look and see"); a performative aspect (confirm/grant) doesn't exclude the assertive aspect (cuckold). ∴ "is true" describes a real relation; not superfluous.
- **D — but "true" is a dimension, not a switch.** "Sufficiently like" is purpose-relative → degrees/dimensions of fit (Belfast/Raglan; rough/exaggerated); "true" is one of a family; "like freedom, truth is a bare minimum or an illusory ideal." Not every "statement" aims at truth (formulae, performatives, value-judgements, definitions, fiction) → "Every proposition is true or false" = the **descriptive fallacy** → the trapdoor into *HTDTW*.
**∴ Master.** Truth = the occasion-bound statement's *modest, gradable, purpose-relative* fitting of the world — realist enough that "is true" describes a real relation (vs deflation), deflationary enough that "true" is one appraisal-dimension (vs the true/false fetish), conventionalist about the typing (vs mirroring). [Reimer = the realist *letter*; Travis = the conventionalist *structure* → "nonstarter."] **→ Essay 3 complete.**

### Companion — "Unfair to Facts" (1954) [not close-read]
Austin's reply to Strawson, defending **facts are genuinely in the world** (vs Strawson: "facts are pseudo-entities," "fitting the facts" a useless notion). "Phenomena, events, situations, states of affairs are… genuinely-in-the-world… of all these we can say they are facts. **The collapse of the Germans is an event and is a fact.**" Against Strawson's slide ("what makes 'the cat has mange' true is the fact that the cat has mange — and *that* is not in the world"): the **condition of the cat** *is* a fact *and is* something-in-the-world; the invalid step is inferring "the condition of the cat **is** the fact that the cat has mange." → **Sharpens the realist side:** Austin is realist that there *are* worldly facts (vs Strawson's deflation), while still denying they are *proposition-shaped* (Truth). So the non-autonomy (Travis) is about *typing/individuation*, **not existence**.

## Essay 4 — "A Plea for Excuses" (1956)
*The method manifesto. Predates **Benson Mates**'s 1958 challenge to OLP; Austin pre-empts it; **Cavell** answers it in "Must We Mean What We Say?" (1958).*

### The method, justified (three points)
1. "words are our tools… use **clean tools**."
2. "words are not… facts or things: **prise them off the world**… re-look at the world without blinkers."
3. "our common stock of words embodies all the distinctions men have found worth drawing… in the lifetimes of many generations… more sound, since they have stood up to the long test of the **survival of the fittest**, and more subtle… than any… we are likely to think up in our arm-chairs."

### "Not merely verbal" — linguistic phenomenology (the answer to the 'merely verbal' worry)
"When we examine what we should say when… we are looking **not merely at words**… but also at **the realities we use the words to talk about**: a sharpened awareness of words to sharpen our perception of, **though not as the final arbiter of, the phenomena**." → "**linguistic phenomenology**." [Words = microscope; phenomena = specimen. "Not the final arbiter" = corrigible.]

### The two snags = the Mates challenge, pre-empted
- **Loose/Divergent Usage** (= Mates's *experts-disagree* prong, e.g. Ryle vs Austin on "voluntarily"): "Don't usages differ?" Austin: (a) *most* disagreement is illusory — "we had simply imagined the situation slightly differently" (no situation is "completely described"); a "background of story" shrinks it. (b) *Genuine* disagreement is **data**: "you choose to classify in one way, I in another… we can find *why* we disagree" — "not to be shied off, but to be **pounced upon**" (the wrong-way electron = a discovery; "a genuinely loose talker is a rare specimen to be prized").
- **The Last Word** (= Mates's *authority/finality* prong): Austin **concedes** it. "Ordinary language has **no claim to be the last word**… concentrated… upon the **practical business of life**… not… fed from the resources of the **microscope**… superstition and error… do become incorporated… **Certainly ordinary language is not the last word: in principle it can everywhere be supplemented and improved upon and superseded. Only remember, it is the first word.**" [Fallibilist.]

### Austin vs Cavell on the same challenge
**Cavell** (1958): the native speaker's "we say X" is **not** an empirical hypothesis needing a survey but a claim grounded in **mastery** (non-empirical, first-person authority); it is **normative** ("what we *should* say") and articulates the **categories** of our language — so not "merely" English. *Robust, quasi-transcendental.*
**Austin** (the text): *phenomenology* (words → realities), *survival of the fittest*, *disagreement-as-data*, and explicitly *first word, not last* (corrigible). *Modest, fallibilist.*
→ The recurring pattern: **Cavell's Austin is more foundational than Austin's Austin.** Cavell answers Mates by *raising* the stakes (quasi-a-priori knowledge of categories); Austin answers by *lowering* them (fallible field-data, fine for starting, corrected as you go). [↔ the orientation's "first tension."]

### The donkey, placed in the machinery
Same outcome (your donkey dead), two excuses, because the deviation enters at **different stages**:
- **Case 1 (wrong animal): "by mistake"** = failure at the **appreciation/identification** stage (I executed my plan flawlessly — shot the donkey I aimed at — but mis-appreciated *which* one was mine; the plan was wrong).
- **Case 2 (donkeys moved): "by accident"** = failure at the **executive** stage — specifically an *external contingency* disrupting a correctly-appreciated, correctly-aimed act (not failed aiming-skill: that would be *clumsily/inadvertently*).
→ **Vindicates "linguistic phenomenology" against the 'merely verbal' charge:** one outcome, two precise words that *map where in the structure of agency the deviation occurred* — the distinction tracks the **realities of action**, not just English.

### The machinery of action (§4) — "the abnormal throws light on the normal"
Excuses (breakdowns) "**penetrate the blinding veil of ease and obviousness that hides the mechanisms of the natural successful act**"; they "**pick out and sort out**" which stage broke. Stages (each mapped by its verb/adverb cluster): **intelligence → appreciation of the situation → planning → decision/resolve → execution.**
- **Executive stage** ("where we muff it"): inattention, carelessness, **clumsiness**, tactlessness — and *accident*.
- **Appreciation stage** ("too often overlooked"): military case — flawless intelligence + principles, yet a disastrous plan via "**failure at the stage of appreciation**" (casting facts "into such a form… that our principles can be brought to bear"); civilian: "know the facts yet look at them mistakenly… total misconception" — *mistake*, thoughtlessness, inconsiderateness, lack of imagination. (Math fn: know quadratics, yet answer "33 men.")

### Negative payoff: Freedom dissolved (the dimension-word thesis, explicit)
"Like '**real**', '**free**' is only used to rule out… its recognized antitheses. **As 'truth' is not a name for a characteristic of assertions, so 'freedom' is not a name for a characteristic of actions, but the name of a dimension in which actions are assessed.**" → study the ways an action may *not* be free (the excuses) ⇒ the "problem of Freedom" dissolves. [The truth↔freedom parallel, foreshadowed in *Truth* (1950), now outright.]

### "Fact is richer than diction" (§6) — the qualifier
Against dichotomies: we act "at once on impulse and intentionally," "intentionally yet not deliberately, still less on purpose" (the cliff-push). And: "**we must not expect to find simple labels for complicated cases. If a mistake results in an accident, it will not do to ask whether 'it' was an accident or a mistake**… however well-equipped our language, it can never be forearmed against all possible cases… **fact is richer than diction.**" [= the other face of *first word, not last*: a superb map, not an exhaustive one.]

### Extension (곁가지) — where do *excellences* and *wrongs* reside?
The machinery is built from **excuses** = **agency/responsibility** (where an act fails to be fully yours), *not* **value**. So:
- **Failures of agency** map onto the machinery: **blunder** = misjudgment at *appreciation/planning* (but an *accusation*, the mirror of an excuse); **akrasia** = *decision/resolve* stage; bad reasoning = *appreciation*.
- **Excellences/values** (nobility, excellence, the **탁월수**) are *not* breakdowns → not on the excuse-machinery; they sit on the **justification/value** axis. But the *method* extends — Austin: "forget the beautiful, get down to the **dainty and the dumpy**" = run linguistic phenomenology on the **praise-vocabulary**.
- **Symmetry:** the same stages host both — read **failures** off excuse-words, **excellences** off praise-words. *Appreciation/insight:* blunder ↔ **탁월수** (brilliance = the *seeing*). *Decision:* akrasia ↔ resolve/nerve. *Execution:* clumsiness ↔ deftness. (고결함/nobility = the *motive/end*, not a stage.)
- **도덕적 잘못 = a compound:** (a) act is **bad** (value/justification) + (b) agent is **responsible** (agency/machinery). An *excuse* attacks (b) while conceding (a); a *justification* attacks (a). "A Plea for Excuses" exists to keep **wrongness ≠ culpability**. [↔ Aristotle's *aretē* = positive-space twin of the failure-machinery; cf. Austin, "Agathon and Eudaimonia."]

### Extension (cont.) — akrasia, the decision-cog, and "the Will"
**Akrasia lives at the *decision* cog** — and Austin's criterion for that cog is in the essay: "I **intended**… yet did not act **deliberately**, for I did not **(stop to) ask myself whether to do it or not**." So *deliberately* = stopping-to-weigh = the decision-gap; akrasia needs it (weigh → verdict → act *against* it). **Games** are lived in the *intentional* (see-and-play), often without deliberation → no akrasia (at most *plumping* between equals — not going *against* a verdict; and the end (win) isn't up for grabs). **Moral** action is *deliberate* → the good itself is in question → akrasia possible.
**"The Will" is no explanation** (= Austin's own conviction): a reified *Nothing-in-particular* / Ryle's "doctrine of volitions" — explaining a doing by a doer-behind-it; "reading back into the world the features of language." Austin's replacement = **dissolution + field-guide**: map the plural verbs (decide / resolve / choose / **plump for** / make up one's mind / mean to / on purpose / deliberately), each distinct. "**Make up one's mind**" = the *closing of deliberation* (weighing → being-settled), not an inner act. (Worked in "**Three Ways of Spilling Ink**": on purpose/intentionally/deliberately; the power-side in "**Ifs and Cans**.")
**Honest limit:** Austin *dissolves* the will-question and hands a map; he gives **no positive metaphysics** of decision. Fork: *liberating* (pseudo-problem gone) vs *evasive* (akrasia still wants a *mechanism*). Where **Aristotle** (practical syllogism), **Davidson** ("How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?"), **Frankfurt** (hierarchy of the will) part from him.

### The thirteen concluding remarks — orientation
The essay closes with thirteen numbered observations (the "miscellany" that enacts the method on its own vocabulary). §4 (machinery) and §6 (fact richer than diction) are covered above; below are the remaining ones — **the adverb-grammar trio §§1–3, the standards §5, the worked case §§7+11, the micro-grammar §§8–10, the etymology §12, and the science close §13.** Edition page-heads (*Philosophical Papers*) cited as [p.N].

### "No modification without aberration" (§1) — the load-bearing grammar of excuses
The tempting picture: given "X did A," for any modifier *M* we may ask "Did X do A *Mly* or not *Mly*?" ("Did X murder Y **voluntarily or involuntarily**?") and must answer one or the other — or, minimally, there must be *some* modifier we could "justifiably and informatively" insert. Austin: in the great majority of cases this is false. "**The natural economy of language dictates that for the standard case covered by any normal verb—not, perhaps, a verb of omen such as 'murder', but a verb like 'eat' or 'kick' or 'croquet'—no modifying expression is required or even permissible.**" [p.138] Only a *deviation* from how the act is "naturally done" calls a modifier forth "or even in order." Examples: sitting in my chair "in the usual way," it will not do to say I sat intentionally *or* unintentionally, automatically *or* from habit; at bedtime, alone, "**I yawn: but I do not yawn involuntarily (or voluntarily!), nor yet deliberately. To yawn in any such peculiar way is just not to just yawn.**" [p.138]
→ **This is the grammatical engine of the whole essay.** Excuses *are* modifiers; by §1 they get a grip *only where the act deviates from the standard* — which is *why* "the abnormal throws light on the normal" (§4) and why excuse-words map agency by its breakdowns. [↔ §4 machinery; ↔ the donkey.]
→ **It pre-dissolves a bad free-will question.** You cannot ask "was sitting-in-my-chair *voluntary*?" — "voluntarily" has no purchase on the standard case. So the blanket question "is every act free/voluntary?" is malformed, *before* any theory of the Will. [→ the bridge to "Ifs and Cans"; ↔ Freedom-as-dimension, §4.] "**murder**" = a *verb of omen* — the rare verb with no "standard, unmodified" case, so the exception that frames the rule.

### Limitation of application (§2) — what an adverb *can't* modify is the more revealing
Excuse-adverbs ("unwittingly," "spontaneously," "impulsively") have "**limited ranges of application**" — each fits only a comparatively narrow band of verbs. ("Something in the lad's upturned face appealed to him, he threw a brick at it—'**spontaneously**'?" — it won't take the word.) The payoff is in the *misfits*: "**we are sometimes not so good at observing what we can't say as what we can, yet the first is pretty regularly the more revealing**" [p.139 fn]. Why an excuse *won't* attach elucidates its meaning, the action-group it picks out, and "the machinery of 'action' in general (see 4), or… our standards of acceptable conduct (see 5)." Worked on voluntarily/involuntarily: "join the army or make a gift **voluntarily**," "hiccough or make a small gesture **involuntarily**" — the two classes grow "more circumscribed and unlike each other… until we even doubt whether there is any verb with which both adverbs are equally in place." Apparent shared verbs are illusions that "prove the rule" (break a cup *voluntarily* as self-impoverishment vs *involuntarily* via an involuntary movement = "really very different" acts). [p.139]
→ **The negative-space method, stated outright.** The concept lives in its boundary, not its core. [↔ the general OLP move; ↔ S&S's catalogue of what "looks/seems" can and can't do.]

### Negations & opposites (§3) — which word "wears the trousers"
voluntarily/involuntarily "**are not opposed in the obvious sort of way that they are made to be in philosophy or jurisprudence.**" Their *opposites diverge*: the opposite of "voluntarily" ≈ "**under constraint**" (duress/obligation/influence); of "involuntarily" ≈ "**deliberately**" / "on purpose." "Such divergences in opposites indicate that 'voluntarily' and 'involuntarily'… are **fish from very different kettles**." [p.140] (fn: signing a cheque normally is *neither* "voluntarily" *nor* "under constraint.") So: don't assume a word has an opposite, or *one* opposite — ask rather "**why there is no use for the adverb 'advertently'.**"
**The trousers-word doctrine** (load-bearing, exported wholesale to S&S): "**it will not do to assume that the 'positive' word must be around to wear the trousers; commonly enough the 'negative' (looking) word marks the (positive) abnormality, while the 'positive' word, if it exists, merely serves to rule out the suggestion of that abnormality.**" [p.140] → the word doing the real semantic work is whichever marks the genuine deviation; the other merely *cancels* it. [↔ **S&S: "real" is the trousers-word** — "a real duck" only excludes decoy/toy/dummy; ↔ the **dimension-word thesis**: "free," "true," "real" all work by ruling out their antitheses, not by naming a feature.]
**Positive analysis of "inadvertently"** (not just "wears the trousers"): "**I do an act A₁ (say, crush a snail) inadvertently if, in the course of executing… some other act A₂ (say, in walking down the public path) I fail to exercise such meticulous supervision over the courses of those movements as would have been needed to ensure that they did not bring about the untoward event.**" [pp.140–41] At this "**below supervision in detail**" level "**anything that we do is, if you like, inadvertent, though we only call it so… if there is something untoward about it.**" [p.141] (Adverbs "lead parallel lives" across *doing* / *saying* / *thinking* — fn.) Closing exercise: why *un-/in-* (unwitting) vs *-less* (aimless, reckless, heedless) vs *mis-* (mistake, misconception)? "**Why carelessly but inattentively?**… Here are remunerative exercises." [p.141]

### Standards of the unacceptable (§5) — the snail vs. the baby
"**It is characteristic of excuses to be 'unacceptable'**": for almost any excuse there are cases "of such a kind or of such gravity that 'we will not accept' it" — and the interest is in "the standards and codes we thus invoke." Our supervision over execution falls within "fairly definite limits ('**due care and attention**')," set differently for different act-types. "**We may plead that we trod on the snail inadvertently: but not on a baby—you ought to look where you are putting your great feet.**" [p.143] It *was* (really) inadvertence — but the word "constitutes a plea, which is not going to be allowed, because of standards. And if you try it on, you will be **subscribing to such dreadful standards that your last state will be worse than your first**." Rule-governed acts license different excuses than loose ones: "**a wrong spelling may be a slip, but hardly an accident, a winged beater may be an accident, but hardly a slip.**" [p.143]
→ **An excuse can be *true* yet *inadmissible*** — admissibility ≠ truth. The plea's *factual correctness* doesn't settle whether it exonerates; the **standard of care for the act-type** does. [↔ adds a third axis to the wrongness ≠ culpability map: act-type-relative *standards*.] The baby-plea is self-defeating: to claim you didn't *watch* for babies is to indict your whole pattern of attention ("worse than your first").

### *Regina v. Finney* (§§7, 11) — the worked case; "what is being excused?"
The law-report *source* in action (1874, Shrewsbury Assizes, 12 Cox 625) — "**a somewhat distressing favourite in the class that Hart used to conduct with me in the years soon after the war**" [fn — the Austin–**H. L. A. Hart** Saturday mornings]. Finney, an asylum attendant, drains Watkins's bath, his attention "**taken from the bath**" by a new attendant's question, turns on a tap — "**I did not intend to turn the hot water, and I made a mistake in the tap**" — and scalds Watkins to death. Lush J.'s direction hinges on "gross carelessness" vs "**inadvertence not amounting to culpability—i.e., what is properly termed an accident**." Verdict: not guilty.
**Two morals** [p.145]: (i) counsel and judge "**make very free use of a large number of terms of excuse, using several as though they were… indifferent or equivalent when they are not**"; (ii) "**it is constantly difficult to be sure what act it is that counsel or judge is suggesting might be qualified by what expression of excuse.**" The verdict: "**The learned judge's concluding direction is a paradigm of these faults. Finney, by contrast, stands out as an evident master of the Queen's English. He is explicit as to each of his acts and states, mental and physical: he uses different, and the correct, adverbs in connexion with each: and he makes no attempt to boil down.**" [p.145]
→ **The accused out-speaks the judge** = ordinary language (untheorized) as the *first word*, the jurist's jargon as corruption. (fn: judges convey conviction "through… a pithy Anglo-Saxon which sometimes has literally no meaning at all" — "the man is never on the thing at all.")
**§11 "What modifies what?"** sharpens moral (ii): the judge never fixes *what event* is excused *how* — was the tap turned inadvertently, or Watkins scalded accidentally? was the carelessness in *turning the tap* or in *thinking Watkins had got out*? "**Many disputes as to what excuse we should properly use arise because we will not trouble to state explicitly what is being excused.**" [pp.148–49] Because one deed admits many descriptions: "**we can describe Finney's act either as turning on the hot tap, which he did by mistake, with the result that Watkins was scalded, or as scalding Watkins, which he did not do by mistake.**" [p.149] The same deed splits three ways — **Stages** (dismantle the machinery: intelligence/appreciation/planning/decision/execution, excuse each), **Phases** (painted a picture / laid on this stroke then that), **Stretches** (a term covers a smaller or larger run of events; the excluded ones become "consequences/results/effects"). "**The problems of excuses and those of the different descriptions of actions are throughout bound up with each other.**" (fn: "made a mistake in the tap" = the *basic* use of mistake — "we simply… take the wrong one"; cf. ordered "Right turn," I turn left = "**a simple, pure mistake**," neither accident nor inadvertence; had Finney acted *inadvertently* the tap "would have been knocked… in reaching for the cold tap—a different story.") [→ the **action-individuation** problem; ↔ Anscombe *Intention*, Davidson "Agency" — the descriptions of "what I did."]

### The micro-grammar (§§8–10) — distinctions, the exact phrase, manner vs. style
**§8 Small distinctions, and big too.** Terms of excuse "are not equivalent, and… it matters which we use" — distinguish inadvertence from mistake/accident *and* from "nearer neighbours" (aberration, absence of mind), even "**sheer, mere, pure, and simple**" mistake. Yet "in the grip of thought" we equate "inadvertently" with "automatically," or "collapse succumbing to temptation into losing control of ourselves—**a bad patch, this, for telescoping**." Method declaration: "**All this is not so much a lesson from the study of excuses as the very object of it.**" [p.146 — the distinctions *are* the point, not a means to one.]
→ **The bombe footnote** (the locus classicus that akrasia ≠ compulsion): "**I am very partial to ice cream, and a bombe is served divided into segments**… I am tempted to help myself to two… **thus succumbing to temptation and even conceivably (but why necessarily?) going against my principles. But do I lose control of myself?… Not a bit of it. We often succumb to temptation with calm and even with finesse.**" [p.146 fn] "Plato… and after him Aristotle, fastened this confusion upon us" (moral weakness ≡ weakness of will). [↔ the akrasia note above: this is the *textual anchor* that wrongdoing can be done *coolly, deliberately* — so akrasia sits at the **decision cog**, not at a failure of *control* (the executive stage).]
**§9 The exact phrase and its place.** Not the "key" word alone but its full form: "by mistake," "owing to a mistake," "mistakenly," "it was a mistake to," "to make a mistake in/over/about," "to be mistaken about" — these "function quite differently… or why should we burden ourselves with more than one of them?" And *position* affects sense, the four-way snail set: "**He clumsily trod on the snail / Clumsily he trod on the snail**" (a₁,a₂) vs "**He trod clumsily on the snail / He trod on the snail clumsily**" (b₁,b₂) — in a-forms "we describe his treading on the creature **at all** as a piece of clumsiness, incidental… to some other action"; in b-forms "**to tread on it is, very likely, his aim or policy, what we criticize is his execution of the feat**." [p.147]
**§10 The style of performance.** A further split: "He ate his soup deliberately" can mean a *decided* act *or* a manner — "pause after each mouthful, careful choice of point of entry for the spoon, sucking of moustaches" — i.e. "**with deliberation rather than after deliberation**." [p.147] The *style* sense exists for "carelessly," not for "inadvertently"; sometimes a kindred word takes the style role — "we use '**purposefully**' in this way, but never '**purposely**'." [→ the precision the method demands; micro-confirmation of "fact is richer than diction." ↔ "Three Ways of Spilling Ink."]

### Trailing clouds of etymology (§12) — the "shoving a stone" model & the anti-system
Hardest words: result/effect/consequence, intention/purpose/motive. Two indispensable method-points. **(1) Etymology runs live:** "**a word never—well, hardly ever—shakes off its etymology and its formation**," the old idea "**pervading and governing**" the new senses — "in an **accident** something *befalls*; by **mistake** you *take the wrong one*; in **error** you *stray*; when you act **deliberately** you act *after weighing it up* (not after thinking out ways and means)" [p.150]; "'unwillingly' and 'involuntarily' come from very different sources." **(2) Words carry buried models:** going back (often to Latin) we reach "**pictures or models of how things happen**." The most primitive, "**apt to baffle us through its very naturalness**": "**We take some very simple action, like shoving a stone, usually as done by and viewed by oneself, and use this… as our model in terms of which to talk about other actions and events**" — even where it "is really distorting the facts." [p.150] "**'Causing'… was a notion taken from a man's own experience of doing simple actions, and by primitive man every event was construed in terms of this model: every event has a cause, that is, every event is an action done by somebody—if not by a man, then by a quasi-man, a spirit.**" We still say events "must be 'caused,'" the word "snares us… we unearth and incorporate the lineaments of the ancient model. **As happened even to Hume, and consequently to Kant.**" [pp.150–51] → "a source of confusion and superstition."
→ **The anti-systematic close** (the deepest methodological payoff): "**there is no necessity whatsoever that the various models used in creating our vocabulary… should all fit together neatly… into one single, total model or scheme**… our assortment of models will include some, or many, that are overlapping, conflicting, or more generally simply **disparate**." [p.151] (fn:) it is "too readily assumed" that a cluster of key terms ('right', 'good'…) "must… fit into… some single, interlocking, consistent, conceptual scheme. Not only is there no reason to assume this, but all historical probability is against it… **Just as we cheerfully subscribe to, or have the grace to be torn between, simply disparate ideals—why must there be a conceivable amalgam, the Good Life for Man?**" [↔ "fact is richer than diction"; ↔ pluralism — Austin's standing refusal of the One Big Theory; cf. the deflation of "truth," "freedom," "knowledge" each into a *family*, not an essence. ↔ Wittgenstein family-resemblance; contra a Searle-style systematization.]

### Modern science has the last word (§13) — displacement & compulsion; the close
The fallibilism cashed out by example: "**modern scientists have been able… to reveal [ordinary speech's] inadequacy at numerous points… because they have had access to more comprehensive data and have studied them with more catholic and dispassionate interest than the ordinary man, or even the lawyer**" [pp.151–52]. **Displacement behaviour** — a thwarted animal "**betakes itself to energetic, but quite unrelated, activity… such as standing on its head**"; this "bulks quite large" in human life "yet we have apparently no word… for it" ("**'In desperation'?**"). **Compulsive behaviour** (compulsive washing) — ordinary speech only hints ("just feel I have to," "shouldn't feel comfortable unless I did"); "**there is no adverbial expression satisfactorily pre-empted for it, as 'compulsively' is**" — understandably, since such behaviour "is not in general going to be of great practical importance." [p.152]
→ **The concrete vindication of "the first word, not the last."** Ordinary language is keyed to "the practical business of life," so it *lacks* words for phenomena of no practical moment — exactly where the microscope (psychology, the third *source*) supplements it. The two-snags concession (above) is here *demonstrated*, not just asserted. **The close:** "**Here I leave and commend the subject to you.**" [p.152 — programmatic, not conclusive: a research programme handed on, not a doctrine sealed.]

### Argument reconstruction (Essay 4 — "A Plea for Excuses")
**Master thesis.** "Freedom" names no positive feature of actions but a **dimension** along which they are assessed by *ruling out* the ways they go wrong; so the road to the metaphysics of agency runs *not* through a frontal theory of the Will or the Voluntary but through a **field study of excuses** — the modifiers we reach for when a doing is somehow *not* fully a doing. Excuses chart the **machinery of action** (intelligence → appreciation → planning → decision → execution) precisely because, by the grammar of modification, they grip *only where the act deviates* ("no modification without aberration").
**Method.** "**linguistic phenomenology**" — examine *what we should say when*; a sharpened awareness of words to sharpen perception *of the realities*, "**not as the final arbiter**"; *sources* = the dictionary, the **law reports** (Finney), and **psychology** (displacement/compulsion); ordinary language = "**the first word**," everywhere corrigible.
- **A — why excuses (the frame, above).** clean tools; prise words off the world; survival of the fittest. An *excuse* (≠ justification) concedes the act was bad but contests that it was *fully the agent's* → a controlled site where agency-failure gets labelled. ∴ wrongness ≠ culpability.
- **B — the grammar that makes it work (§§1–3).** no modification without aberration (eat/kick/croquet take no modifier; only deviation calls one forth; "murder" = verb of omen); limitation of application (the *misfits* are the more revealing); negations/opposites — the "negative" word commonly **wears the trousers** (voluntarily/involuntarily = "fish from very different kettles"). ∴ excuse-words are *negative-space* instruments: they chart the normal by marking the abnormal.
- **C — the machinery, freedom, and its bounds (§§4–6).** excuses "pick out and sort out" which **stage** broke ("the abnormal throws light on the normal"); **Freedom dissolves** into the dimension-word thesis (↔ *Truth*); but "**fact is richer than diction**" (no simple labels for compound cases) and excuses are **standard-relative** (snail ≠ baby; a plea's *truth* ≠ its *admissibility*). ∴ a real, structured, *non-exhaustive* map of agency — not a "theory."
- **D — descriptions, words, limits (§§7–13).** *Finney*: terms of excuse are **not** equivalent, and one must state *what act* is excused (turn-the-tap-by-mistake vs scald-Watkins-not-by-mistake → stages/phases/stretches); attend to the **exact phrase and its position** (clumsily/snail; *with* vs *after* deliberation; the bombe — wrongdoing done coolly ⇒ akrasia at the *decision* cog, not lost *control*); **etymology runs live** (the "shoving-a-stone" model of *causing*; "even Hume… Kant") and our models need not form **one scheme** ("why… the Good Life for Man?"); **science** exposes gaps ordinary language never needed to fill (displacement, compulsion) — "the first word," not the last. ∴ the method is powerful *and* bounded — a field-guide, not a system.
**∴ Master.** Don't theorize Freedom or the Will; **study excuses** — the negative-space modifiers that, stage by stage, show where a doing fails to be fully a doing. The yield is a *corrigible, plural, occasion-sensitive* map of agency, continuous with the dimension-word treatment of "true" and "real," and deliberately innocent of any single unifying metaphysics. [↔ *Truth* (freedom∥truth, foreshadowed); ↔ *Other Minds* ("I know"/"I promise" — the act behind the word); → *Ifs and Cans* (the *power* side: "could have," "can if I choose").] **→ Essay 4 complete.**
