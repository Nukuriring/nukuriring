## Part 0 — Method & Map (orientation)

### The first word, not the last
Austin is filed under "Ordinary Language Philosophy" beside Wittgenstein, but his *reason* for caring about ordinary language is the opposite of Wittgenstein's. For Wittgenstein/Cavell, ordinary use is the **last word**: you look at use, the philosophical cramp eases, you win "peace" (*Investigations* §133), and — officially — there is no theory to build. For Austin, ordinary use is the **first word**: a body of *evidence* you collect in order to go on and build something positive and theoretical. Same opening move, opposite sequel. Wittgenstein rests; Austin presses on. The engine of the pressing-on is **accuracy** — Hampshire: Austin applied "the same standards of truth and accuracy to a philosophical argument, sentence by sentence, as he would have applied to any other serious subject-matter."

His method, stated in "A Plea for Excuses" (1956), has three moves: (1) "words are our tools, and… we should use clean tools"; (2) "words are not… facts or things: we need therefore to prise them off the world… so that we can… re-look at the world without blinkers"; (3) "our common stock of words embodies all the distinctions men have found worth drawing… in the lifetimes of many generations" — so they are likely sounder and subtler than armchair coinages. Crucial qualifier: "ordinary language is not the last word… Only remember, it is the first word." He called the method **"linguistic phenomenology"**, with the stress on *phenomenology*: words are the microscope, the **phenomena** (the world) are the specimen.

### Distinctions are findings, not vocabulary lessons
Because ordinary distinctions are evidence about the phenomena, Austin's recurring weapon is the **deflation of a false dichotomy**: philosophers "in their hurry" force complex cases into crude either/ors; he slows down and shows the dichotomy was never exhaustive. Standing slogan: **"fact is richer than diction."** This is the Austin that **Travis** inherits (occasion-sensitivity) and the one the SEP places in the **"Oxford Realism"** lineage (Cook Wilson, Prichard → Travis, McDowell, Snowdon, Martin, Williamson): perception and knowledge are *primitive forms of apprehension*, and what we apprehend are ordinary, mind-independent bits of the world. His epistemology and his philosophy of language are one project.

### The map (three arenas)
The SEP carves Austin into three arenas, one method running through all:

1. **Language & Truth** — the method; a deflationary-but-realist account of truth; and the culmination, **speech acts** (constative/performative collapsing into locutionary / illocutionary / perlocutionary; the "descriptive fallacy").
2. **Knowledge & Perception** — "Other Minds" (knowing as a standing; "I know" vs. "I promise") and *Sense and Sensibilia* (the demolition of sense-data and the "argument from illusion").
3. **Action & Freedom** — "A Plea for Excuses" (the anatomy of how an action goes wrong) and "Ifs and Cans" (against the conditional analysis of "could have").

Through-line: in each arena a discipline is hypnotized by one crude dichotomy (true/false, sense-data/material-objects, free/unfree); Austin uses the *first word* of ordinary distinctions to break the spell and start the real work.

## Part 1 — Language & Truth (SEP §2.2)

**Texts:** "Truth" (1950), with "Unfair to Facts" (1954) as the sequel; the occasion-sensitivity point is pushed further in *How to Do Things with Words* (1962).

### The Strawson provocation
"Truth" is a reply to **Strawson**. Strawson held that "is true" is **performative** — to say "that's true" is to *affirm/endorse/agree*, not to describe a statement as having a property — and he charged correspondence theorists with the **"descriptive fallacy"**: treating a performative device as if it were a description. (Flag the irony: the very phrase Austin will later wield in *How to Do Things with Words* is here aimed *at* the correspondence theorist *by* Strawson, and in "Truth" Austin is partly on the descriptivist side. The dialectic flips later.)

Austin's reply: "is true" **does** have a descriptive function (perhaps alongside performative ones) — it characterizes the obtaining of a relation between a statement and the facts. But defending correspondence does *not* mean swallowing the crude picture. "The theory of truth is a series of truisms": "a statement is true when it corresponds to the facts" "can hardly be wrong… I do not really think it is wrong at all. Still, it can be misleading." So he keeps "correspondence" and rebuilds what *correspondence* and *facts* mean.

### The machinery — two conventions
First he disambiguates "statement": a **stating** (the dated, historic act of stating) vs. **what is stated** (the proposition). Austin's interest is the *stating* — "the historic use of a sentence by an utterer." (Note: this already smuggles in the act-perspective that blooms into speech acts.)

Correspondence is then underwritten by two distinct conventions:

- **Descriptive conventions** — tie sentence-**types** to **types** of situation/way-for-things-to-be. "The cat is on the mat" ↔ the type [cat-on-mat].
- **Demonstrative conventions** — tie a particular **stating** (a dated event) to a particular **historic** situation in the world: *which* one you are actually talking about, fixed on the occasion by tense, pointing, context.

Definition: **a statement is true when the particular state of affairs it is hooked to by the demonstrative conventions (the one it "refers" to) is of a *type* with which the sentence used is correlated by the descriptive conventions.** Two ropes, two anchors — one to a worldly particular, one to a type; truth = the particular falls under the type.

### The crucial wrinkle — "of a type with which" = "sufficiently *like*"
Austin's footnote: "is of a type with which" means "is sufficiently **like** those standard states of affairs with which." For a statement to be true, one state of affairs must be sufficiently like certain *standard* ones "to merit the same description" — "which is no longer a purely natural relation." "This is red" is not "this is like those," nor "this is like those called red." That things are *similar* I can literally see; that they are the **same** I cannot literally see — "in calling them the same colour a convention is involved." 

Upshot: **states of affairs do not, by themselves, dictate which type they fall under.** Classification requires a human **decision/judgment** — not arbitrary, but going beyond what the eye delivers. The realism (a mind-independent world of particulars) and the conventionalism (types and classification are *ours*) are the two halves of one view.

### The payoff — occasion-sensitivity (the Travis seed, now explicit)
Because judgment mediates classification, the **same** particular can count as "of the type" for some purposes and not others (the rose may be "sufficiently like" standard red things for one purpose, not another; and the set of "standard cases" itself shifts with purpose and time). Hence:

> "Sentences as such are not either true or false" — truth turns "not only on what a sentence is, nor yet on what it means, but on… the circumstances in which it is uttered." (1962a: 110–111)

The **Lord Raglan / Battle of Alma** example: "Lord Raglan won the battle of Alma" is fine (true-enough) in a schoolbook, but not in a work of historical research (a soldier's battle; his orders never reached some subordinates). The *sentence* isn't true or false; a particular *stating* of it, on an occasion, with its intents and purposes, is. Separate two questions: (i) is the **sentence** true? (would require *every* use to be true) vs. (ii) is **what is stated on this occasion** true? This is the 1950 embryo of mature Travis: fix the words, fix the world, vary the occasion → vary the truth-evaluable content.

### Facts as particulars ("Unfair to Facts," 1954)
The second thing the crude picture gets wrong is "facts." Strawson treats facts as proposition-shaped pseudo-entities ("the fact that *p*") — the world pre-digested into statement-shape, which makes correspondence idle. Austin (with the etymology, *factum* — a thing done): **facts are worldly particulars** — things, events, states of affairs — not proposition-shaped. "The fact that *p*" talk is an *indirect* way of pointing at the particulars that make *p* true. Why it matters: if facts were already proposition-shaped, they'd dictate their own classification and the whole judgment/convention story would have nothing to do. Austin needs facts to be "dumb" particulars so the work of classifying falls to us.

### Deflationism (the hinge to speech acts)
Despite defending "is true" as descriptive, Austin also leans **deflationary**: calling a statement "true" is saying it has one of a family of more specific merits — satisfactory, correct, fair, accurate. "True/false" is just "a general dimension of being a right or proper thing to say." Once true/false is demoted to *one* dimension of assessing statings (alongside correct/appropriate/fair/exaggerated), and once stating is itself seen as an **act** assessed much as other acts are (well/badly, aptly/inaptly), the special metaphysical status of "the statement" evaporates — and we are on the road to treating *all* utterance as action.

### Discussion — deepening §2.2

**Does this collapse the statement/performative contrast? (→ §2.3)** Yes — the pressure is toward collapse. "Truth" already makes stating an *act* (assessed correct/appropriate), makes truth occasion/purpose-relative (Raglan), and deflates "true" into one of a family of appraisals. So in *HTDTW* the contrast dismantles from both ends: statements can "misfire" too (e.g., "The present King of France is bald" — void for lack of reference, like a performative misfire), and truth-assessment becomes the fact-fitting *dimension of success* of an illocutionary act (stating). End-state: constating is one kind of performing; "true/false" is one appraisal among many. The "descriptive fallacy" = privileging the constative as the basic case.

**Is it still correspondence, or deflationism in a coat?** Best read as a *third thing*: **conventionalist, occasion-relative correspondence.** Realist about the relata (mind-independent particulars), deflationary about the property ("true" = a thin appraisal: satisfactory/fair/correct), conventionalist about the typing that links them. He keeps the *form* of a word-world relation genuinely described by "is true," but drains the metaphysical "mirroring" (facts aren't proposition-shaped, so there's nothing to mirror). Realist underneath — not deflationist all the way down — but the explanatory work sits on the convention/judgment side.

**Where does Travis go beyond Austin?** Austin keeps a stable *descriptive-convention* layer (sentence-type ↔ situation-type) and localizes occasion-relativity in the *application* step ("is this particular sufficiently like the standard cases, for these purposes?"). Travis pushes occasion-sensitivity *up into the truth-condition itself*: even with meaning and reference fixed, the same sentence ("the leaves are green") can be true on one occasion, false on another (painted vs. naturally green leaves) — meaning underdetermines truth-conditions; only an occasion fixes *which* condition is in play. Travis's textual hook is precisely Austin's "this is red / I cannot literally *see* that they are the **same** / a convention is involved" footnote — continuous with Wittgenstein on rule-following / "going on in the same way" (*PI* §§185–242). Austin saw that sameness needs convention + decision but stopped at the classification step; Travis says that insight, taken to the end, dissolves the standalone meaning-layer too.

### Open threads (live dialectic)

- **Austin "void" ≠ Strawson "gap."** Both put truth at the level of the *statement/use*, not the sentence — Strawson's "On Referring" is use-level too ("referring is something someone uses an expression to do"), so the contrast is *not* sentence-vs-act. They differ in (i) *home*: Strawson houses presupposition-failure in a theory of *reference/presupposition* (→ truth-value **gap**); Austin houses the same Russellian example in a *general theory of act-infelicity* (→ **void**, same genus as a christening with no ship). (ii) *Uniformity*: Strawson's gap is a near-uniform semantic outcome of presupposition-failure; Austin's "void" is a verdict on *this act on this occasion* and is occasion-**defeasible** — he won't blanket-void every use of a non-referring sentence (overtly fictional / hypothetical / loose uses can come off), even though the *standard* assertion is still void, not true.
- **Felicity = a determinable, not a substantive master-notion.** There is a general *grammar* of going-right/going-wrong (infelicities: misfires vs. abuses; later the five illocutionary classes), but the *content* of "success" is fixed only per act-class and occasion — and it goes **meta**: *which* axis (true / valid / sincere / apt) is in play, and what it demands, is itself occasion-set. So truth is *one irreducible dimension the schema files*, not a reductive species of one super-standard.
- **"Similar I can see, same I can't" → the conceptualism fault line.** Austin's footnote posits a seeable layer of brute *similarity* + a convention-laden layer of *sameness*. Pressure: "similar *in what respect*?" — respects are concept-like, so the brute bottom layer may already be structured (Sellars/McDowell, *Myth of the Given*). This is exactly where the "Oxford Realism" lineage splits (McDowell's conceptualism vs. a non-conceptual given). Hedges: Austin doesn't thematize it (*S&S* is negative/therapeutic), and would likely distrust the concept-vs-given dichotomy itself. On-ramp to §3.

## Part 2 — Speech Acts and Truth (SEP §2.3)

**Texts:** *How to Do Things with Words* (1962), "Performative Utterances" (1956); roots in "Other Minds" (1946) and "Truth" (1950).

### A *second* reason "sentences as such aren't true/false"
§2.2 gave one reason: even for *statings*, the fit to the facts is occasion-relative. §2.3 adds a **distinct, independent** reason: any sentence can be used to perform *many* linguistic acts, and not all yield something truth-assessable. Two sub-claims: (a) whether a use makes a *statement* (something truth-apt) depends on more than meaning; (b) some uses do things not true/false at all. Two separate hammers on one idol.

### The constative/performative distinction — built to be demolished
Opening picture: **constatives** (statements) appraised true/false; **performatives** ("I take thee…", "I name this ship…", "I bequeath…", "I bet…") appraised **felicitous/infelicitous**. Performative infelicities: **misfires** (act not brought off — wrong procedure/person, already married) vs. **abuses** (act done but insincerely — a marriage of convenience).

But Austin is **not** defending this. Four tells (SEP): (i) it sorts utterances by sentence-type→use, which he distrusts on principle; (ii) he gives *no* argument that performatives lack truth-value ("I assert this as obvious and do not argue it"); (iii) he states it using "*I assert*…", an apparently performative form that would falsify his own generalization; (iv) the footnote — "Everything said in these sections is provisional, and subject to revision in the light of later sections." A ladder to be kicked away.

### The collapse (symmetric assessment)
None of the attempts to draw the line succeeds — *that is the point*. Argument = symmetry of assessment:
- **Statements catch performative diseases:** "France is hexagonal" can *misfire* (no France; or no suitable intents/purposes manifest) and be an *abuse* (speaker disbelieves it).
- **Performatives catch constative diseases:** "I warn you the bull is about to charge" is criticizable as **mistaken** (fact-failure), not merely unhappy, if no bull.
- "*I state that*…" meets every formal/lexical mark of a performative yet "surely [is] the making of statements, … essentially true or false."

→ **No sorting of sentences** into performative-apt vs. constative-apt. Constating is just another thing done with words.

### The replacement model: locutionary / illocutionary / perlocutionary
Three acts performed at once in one utterance:
- **Locutionary** — producing an utterance with sound+grammar+lexis up to sentence-meaning (*phatic*) and a definite content/sense-reference (*rhetic*).
- **Illocutionary** — individuated by content *and* **force** (stating, warning, promising). Same content "I'll be home for dinner" can be *promised* or *stated*: same locutionary act, different illocutionary force.
- **Perlocutionary** — individuated by *consequential effects* on audience/speaker/others (persuading, alarming, deterring).

Austin's stated interest: "fasten on the second, illocutionary act and contrast it with the other two." (SEP: "probably Austin's central contribution.")

### Payoff — truth-assessment is *illocutionary*; the two "fetishes"
Deeper thesis: modes of assessment (true/false *and* happy/unhappy) apply **most fundamentally to the illocutionary act**. Two diagnosed errors: (i) treating felicity as really about *perlocutionary* effects (so not about the specifically linguistic act); (ii) treating truth as applying to a *locutionary* act conceived as free-standing — doable "just by virtue of the meanings," without illocutionary purpose. Counter: **locutionary acts are *abstractions* from illocutionary acts**; truth is directed most fundamentally at the illocutionary act. Truth becomes "of a piece with" felicity — appraisal of an *act* as a right/wrong thing to say *in these circumstances, to this audience, for these purposes*:

> "'true' and 'false'… do not stand for anything simple at all; but only for a general dimension of being a right and proper thing to say…" (HTDTW 144–145)

Two conclusions: the true/happy distinction is "ultimately **unprincipled**," and a *mixture* of assessments applies to nearly all utterances. With five classes of illocutionary verbs (verdictives, exercitives, commissives, behabitives, expositives) Austin means to "play Old Harry with [= wreak havoc on] two fetishes: (1) the **true/false fetish**, (2) the **value/fact fetish**."

### Weaker vs. stronger reading (+ Frege–Geach)
How far does "truth is illocutionary" go?
- **Weaker:** you can't assess an utterance without attending to its **force(s)** — but a **locutionary core** (a proposition) *might*, for some acts, still be assessed force-independently (true/false).
- **Stronger:** no force-free locutionary core can be detached for force-free assessment at all.

The stronger reading must face the **Frege–Geach problem** (Geach 1965): a valid inference ("*p*; if *p* then *q*; ∴ *q*") needs the *same* content "*p*" whether **asserted** (premise) or merely **entertained** (antecedent). If content can't be pried from force, what shared "*p*" underwrites the inference? This is why most keep a force-free propositional layer — and the seam where Austin's insight, over-extended, strains (Quine, Geach, later Travis press here).

### Sidebar (SEP brackets it): serious vs. parasitic uses — Derrida vs. Searle
Austin sets aside "non-serious" uses (stage, poem, jest) as **parasitic** on serious ones. **Derrida** ("Signature Event Context," 1977) attacks the distinction and its priority; **Searle** (1977) defends Austin; the row became the canonical Analytic/Continental standoff.

### Continuity with §2.2
§2.2 made *stating* an act and *truth* one occasion-relative dimension; §2.3 *completes* it — stating is **one illocution among many**, the constative/performative wall falls, truth-assessment is reassigned to the illocutionary act, "of a piece with" felicity. The **descriptive fallacy** (privileging describing/stating as the basic thing language does) is the error the whole apparatus is built to cure.

## Part 3 — Knowledge (SEP §3.1)

**Texts:** "Other Minds" (1946); *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962) [perception proper → Part 4].

### The nine theses (SEP's reconstruction), compressed
1. **Knowledge is a basic form of apprehension** of how things are — *not* belief + extra conditions (anti-"JTB+"). Knowing *guarantees*: in some sense "if you know, you can't be wrong"; the guarantee can reach subject-independent truths. → Oxford Realism / Williamson's knowledge-first (2000).
2. Knowledge = **successful exercise of judgmental capacities in propitious circumstances** (acumen + opportunity).
3. Those capacities are **inherently limited and fallible**.
4. …yet that is **consistent with** their succeeding, in many circumstances, so as to yield knowledge.
   → (3)+(4) **undercut foundationalism**: no specially infallible foundations, no specially fallible superstructure; capacities can misfire about *anything* (even one's own experience) and be reliable enough about *ordinary* things (there's a pig).
5. For perceptual knowledge of the subject-independent world, **perception must contact that world, not stop at sense-data**. → Oxford Realism.
6. Standard arguments otherwise (the **argument from illusion**) are "at best unconvincing." [→ Part 4]
7. **Testimony**: being told by someone who knows can put you in a position to know.
8. "X knows that p" can serve as an **assurance** others may act/believe/claim-to-know on.
9. **"I know" is performative, not descriptive** — like "I promise," it *gives one's word* (the first about how things are; the second about how one intends them to be).

### Knowing vs. believing — by the challenges, not the inner state
Which challenge is apt: "**How** do you know?" vs. "**Why** do you believe?" Failure differs: fail the first → *you don't know*; fail the second → not that you don't believe but that you *oughtn't*. Decisive line: saying "I know" is **not** "a specially striking feat of cognition, superior… even to being quite sure — for there is nothing in that scale superior to being quite sure" (1946: 99). Knowing is **not a super-grade of confidence**; it's a different *kind* of thing — apprehension + standing.

### Fallibilism — and the refusal of modal infallibilism
Austin is *haunted* by "If I know, I can't be wrong" but **never quite endorses** it. He grants a third-person sense, glossed as a *ban on saying* "I know it is so, but I may be wrong." But he **rejects** that knowing requires it to be *impossible* to have erred (same capacities, same circumstances, wrong verdict) — since capacities are inherently fallible (3), that would make knowledge impossible. So: **fallibilist.** "The human intellect and senses are inherently fallible and delusive, but not *inveterately* so. Machines are inherently liable to break down, but *good machines don't (often)*" (1946: 98). (SEP flags the **performative** proposal (9) as *in tension* with a strict "knowing excludes being mistaken" — the weaker/stronger seam, in epistemic dress.)

### The pig — knowledge as *settling*, not inferring
"…I may say 'That's a pig'; and this too will be 'incorrigible'… if the animal then emerges and stands plainly in view… its coming into view doesn't provide me with *more evidence* that it's a pig, I can now *just see* that it is, the question is settled" (1962a: 114–115). Knowing ordinary things isn't always inference from evidence; sometimes being well-placed simply *settles* it.

### The anti-skeptical engine — "enough is enough"
Challenges invite us to detail **credentials/acumen** ("not just anyone can tell a goldfinch by its head") or **facts** ("by the red head"). Two constraints on a *legitimate* challenge:
1. The challenger must have a **definite lack in mind** ("larks also have red heads"). A bare "you might be wrong" with no candidate alternative is **idle**.
2. "**Enough is enough: it doesn't mean everything.** Enough means enough to show that (within reason, and for present intents and purposes) it 'can't' be anything else… It does *not* mean enough to show it isn't a *stuffed* goldfinch" (1946: 84).
→ Seed of **relevant-alternatives** epistemology (Dretske) and contextualism. *This is what the performative/authority reading buys against the skeptic:* it reclassifies "you might still be wrong" as an idle challenge unless a concrete defeater is named.

### Knowing is classificatory — the conceptualism thread returns
"Any descriptive word is classificatory, involves recognition and in that sense memory… only when we use such words are we knowing anything" (1946: 92). "**Sensa are dumb**, and only previous experience enables us to identify them" (1946: 97). So Austin commits, in epistemology, to *exactly* the two-layer picture from the "Truth" footnote: **dumb given** + **fallible classification**. → The earlier "*similar* I can see, *same* I can't" was **not a slip** but a load-bearing commitment; the Myth-of-the-Given pressure is correspondingly real.

### Knowledge as a standing (testimony + assurance)
(7)+(8): you can come to know **by being told** by someone who knows; "I know" functions as an **assurance** licensing others to act. Knowledge is partly a **social, normative standing** — something *given* and *relied on* — the natural home of the "I know" = "I promise" analogy (9).

### Lineage
**Knowledge-first** (Williamson 2000) ← thesis (1); **relevant alternatives / contextualism** (Dretske, Lewis, DeRose) ← "enough is enough"; **disjunctivism / Oxford Realism** about perception (Hinton, Snowdon, McDowell, M. Martin) ← (5)+(6) [→ Part 4].

## Part 4 — Sensory Perception (SEP §3.2)

**Text:** *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962, Warnock's reconstruction) — largely **negative/therapeutic**: undermine the case *for* sense-data, not build a positive theory.

### Target
The sense-datum doctrine: "we never *directly* perceive material objects, only sense-data." Austin dismantles the *considerations for* it — chiefly the **argument from illusion**.

### The argument (SEP's 5 steps)
(i) In illusion we have an experience *as of* something F, but nothing is F. (ii) Still, *something* we experience is F → a **sense-datum**. (iii) Sense-data occur where no material thing of the sort is present → sense-data ≠ material things. (iv) **Indiscriminability principle:** experiences introspectively indistinguishable have objects of the *same sort*; so a veridical experience indistinguishable from a sense-datum one *also* has sense-data as objects. (v) Generalize → *every* experience has sense-data.

### Austin objects to *every* step
- **Undefined terms:** "material thing," "sense-data," and especially "**directly**" — inflated past any clear contrast.
- **Against (iv):** *indiscriminable ≠ same kind of object.* **Soap made to look exactly like a lemon** is introspectively indistinguishable from seeing a lemon, yet a different object. [The premise later **disjunctivists** — Hinton, Snowdon, McDowell, M. Martin — target: deny the common factor and the bad case can't generalize to the good.]
- **Signature blow, against (i)/(ii)** via the "Other Minds" split *sensory opportunity* vs *judgmental acumen*: the slide from "the stick **looks** bent" to "there is something **bent** I experience" is illegitimate.

### The "looks bent" deflation
A straight stick looking bent in water is **no anomaly** — it's what refraction predicts; it needs **no** bent sense-datum. Austin grants things look ways they aren't, but: (a) **looks are public** — others see them, a **camera** records them — not private data of *your* experience; (b) **judgment mediates** — a *trained* observer noting the water **withholds** "it's bent," so the experience does **not** *dictate* the judgment. Nothing forces a bent *thing* into the account.

### Lexical levers
**look** (experience) ≠ **seem** (judgment) ≠ **appear** (1962a: 33–43) — the argument smears them together. "**Directly**" was never given stable meaning. "**Real**" names no single property — a *substantive-hungry, "trouser" word*: it lives off a specific contrast (*real* cream vs. synthetic, a *real* duck vs. a decoy, *real* colour vs. a trick of the light); the negative "wears the trousers" ("not real *in what way?*"). So "do we perceive *real* things?" has no answer absent a specified contrast.

### illusion ≠ delusion
The argument assimilates the bent stick (illusion) and the alcoholic's pink rats (delusion). Austin separates them: illusions involve experience of **ordinary things and their looks**; standard delusions don't. To squeeze a sense-datum from delusion you'd have to show *both* that the judgment rests on experience at all (not dysfunction) *and* that it's experience of something *beyond* the environment (not a disordered take on a shadow). Absent both, nothing non-ordinary is proven.

### Disjunctivism seed
Austin treats reports ("I see two pieces of paper," "I see pink rats") as **non-committal** about whether the experience even has an *object* — letting veridical and hallucinatory cases differ *in kind* rather than share a common datum. "Close to endorsing a form of **disjunctivism**" (Soteriou 2009) → Hinton, Snowdon, McDowell, M. Martin.

### Method note
*S&S* = the method of Parts 0–3 turned on perception: expose undefined terms, refuse the forced dichotomy (material objects *vs* sense-data), multiply ordinary distinctions, reassign the explanatory load to *judgmental acumen*. **Negative**, not doctrinal.

## Part 5 — Action & Freedom (SEP §4)

**Texts:** "A Plea for Excuses" (1956), "Ifs and Cans" (1956), "Three Ways of Spilling Ink" (1966), "Pretending" (1958).

**Three theses:** (1) attend to *specific* action-talk, not a frontal assault on "freedom/action"; (2) **"No modification without aberration"** — responsibility needs only that the act be a *normal instance of something one does*; (3) Austin leans **incompatibilist** — ordinary "could have" talk is, he suspects, incompatible with determinism.

### §4.1 Excuses & the machinery of action
- **Dimension words again.** "Acting," "acting freely/responsibly" are *dimension words* (like "truth"), grouping specific characterizations. **Aggravations** mark *distinctive* responsibility (on purpose, intentionally, deliberately — "Three Ways of Spilling Ink"); **excuses** *mitigate* it (by accident, by mistake, inadvertently, involuntarily, through clumsiness…).
- **Justification vs. excuse.** A *justification* owns the act but denies it was bad ("I did it, and rightly"); an *excuse* concedes it was bad but pleads reduced/no responsibility. Excuses **don't deny the deed** — they contest the *manner of agency*.
- **Excuses = the "infelicities of action."** Same move as the speech-act *felicity conditions* (§2.3): an action can fail to be fully *mine* at specific joints, as a performative misfires at specific conditions. An excuse can mitigate for the **whole** act, a **sub-component**, the **consequences**, or flag that a happening isn't a paradigm action.
- **Why excuses (method).** *The abnormal illuminates the normal.* Our words for "doing an action" in the full sense are thin and abstract; our vocabulary for the ways acts *go wrong* is rich and field-tested. So the negative space exposes the **"machinery of action": "the receipt of intelligence, the appreciation of the situation, the invocation of principles, the planning, the control of execution and the rest"** (1957: 179) — and different excuses target different stages.
- **"No modification without aberration."** Modifiers are in place only when something is *odd*: "I sit in my chair, in the usual way… it will not do to say either that I sit in it intentionally or that I did not… I yawn: but I do not yawn involuntarily (or voluntarily!)… To yawn in any such peculiar way is just not to just yawn" (1957: 190). "Voluntarily" is not an always-present positive ingredient; it marks the *absence of a specific aberration* (and "voluntary"/"involuntary" needn't target the same element).
- **Against the single-machinery picture.** *Different* machinery underwrites freedom for *different* action-types; different modifiers target *different* parts; a modifier's aptness also depends on whether its target figures in *normal* cases.

### §4.2 Freedom & Ability ("Ifs and Cans")
- **Freedom is a dimension word, like truth** — not a positive characteristic of actions, but the dimension along which actions are assessed, understood via the ways they *fail* to be free. [= "**Free in what way?**", the "real"/"true" trouser-word treatment.]
- **Determinism worry.** "I couldn't have done otherwise" is a general excuse; the "all-in" "could have done X at t" needs circumstances at t *consistent* with doing X — which determinism (C fixes E) appears to deny.
- **Against the conditional analysis of "can."** Austin rejects Moore (1912)/Nowell-Smith (1954): "S can A" ≠ "S will A *if* S chooses" (the "if" in "I can if I choose" is **not** a conditional "if"); and ≠ "If S tried, S would A."
- **The missed short putt.** "I miss a very short putt and kick myself because I *could have holed it*. It is not that I should have holed it *if I had tried*: I did try, and missed… I am talking about conditions *as they precisely were*, and asserting that I could have holed it. **There is the rub.**" (1956a: 218 fn.1) → "I could have" (conditions exactly as they were) ≠ any "I would have, if…".
- **Ability is inherently fallible** — "a human ability or power or capacity is inherently liable not to produce success, on occasion, and that for no reason." [Same structure as the inherently-fallible *judgmental capacities* of §3.1.]
- **Incompatibilist lean.** If a deterministic explanation of the *miss* existed, the golfer *could not* have holed it in conditions exactly as they were — so ordinary ability-talk looks *incompatible* with determinism (Austin's thorn; most OLP was compatibilist).
- **Masking.** Ability retained but exercise precluded by interference; the conditional analysis would need to specify *all possible masks* non-circularly — Austin thinks impossible (abilities are sometimes "masked brutely"). (Modern: finks/masks — Bird, Fara, Johnston.)

### Synthesis — one move, four arenas
**truth** (§2.2) · **speech acts** (§2.3) · **knowledge/perception** (§3) · **action/freedom** (§4). In each: a discipline gripped by a forced dichotomy (true/false; constative/performative; material-objects/sense-data; free/unfree); Austin takes the *first word* of ordinary distinctions as data, multiplies them, dissolves the dichotomy — replacing a would-be *positive characteristic* with a **dimension word** (true, real, free) understood through its specific *failures* (infelicities, illusion-cases, excuses). *"Fact is richer than diction."*
